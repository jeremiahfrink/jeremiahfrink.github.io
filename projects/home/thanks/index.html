The purpose of our celebration

Our family Thanksgiving tradition explores the harvest and thanksgiving customs of a different culture through food and shared experiences. This year, Ethiopian traditions guide the day, with additional recipes from East Africa. These festivals occur at different times of year and belong to different communities; this is our family’s Thanksgiving inspired by them, not a recreation of one Ethiopian holiday.

Plan to present the meal on large platters, then let each person move selections to a small individual plate. Keep the mushroom tibs and meat-free sides available to the pescatarian. Animal fat and eggs are acceptable for this guest, but avoid meat broth or meat ingredients in dishes they will eat.

The celebrations behind the meal

Irreecha: thanksgiving, water, greenery and peace

Irreecha is a living Oromo celebration of gratitude to Waaqa (God), especially after the rainy season as people welcome renewal and abundance. Gatherings at lakes and rivers include singing, prayer, traditional dress and fresh grass or flowers dipped in water. The water, fresh growth and coming season can point to life, renewal, mercy and the gifts of creation. Reconciliation and social harmony are also important themes. The public festival is not simply a food holiday: it has Oromo religious, cultural and communal meaning.

Family observance: Place flowers, greenery and a bowl of clean water on the table. Before the meal, briefly explain Irreecha and invite each person to dip a flower tip in the water as a symbolic family gesture of gratitude for life and provision. You may follow with a prayer of thanksgiving in your own faith. Say plainly that this is your family’s reflection inspired by Irreecha, not an Oromo ceremony.

Enkutatash: new year and renewal

Enkutatash, Ethiopia’s New Year, arrives in September following the rainy season. Yellow adey abeba flowers signal renewed growth. Depending on family and region, children may share flowers, greeting cards, songs and drawings. Family meals, visiting and welcoming a new year matter as much as any one recipe. Doro wat is especially associated with important Ethiopian occasions, including holidays.

Family observance: Decorate with yellow flowers; let younger family members create small cards of thanks or hopes. Ask one question at dinner: “What is something we hope to cultivate or renew in the coming year?” This is a family activity inspired by Enkutatash rather than a customary Enkutatash script.

Meskel: light, faith and gathering

Meskel is an Ethiopian Orthodox Christian feast honoring the Finding of the True Cross. The Demera bonfire is part of a religious celebration, accompanied by worship and communal gathering. The fire is not simply a general harvest symbol. For a Christian family, the connection of light, remembrance and faith may be especially meaningful; traditions and interpretations should be described accurately rather than staged as a Meskel service.

Family observance: After dinner and dessert, light an outdoor bonfire if weather and local rules permit, or enjoy a large supervised fireplace fire if it rains. Explain the real meaning of Meskel, then use the fire as a setting for your family’s own stories, prayers or gratitude. Keep a screen on the fireplace and supervise children.

Kenyan connections at the table

Chapati, nyama choma, mandazi, vitumbua and kabalagala reflect different East African eating traditions; they are not all foods from Ethiopian harvest festivals. Use the Kenyan décor from your travels as a clearly identified celebration of the wider region. A little conversation about where each item came from will make the distinctions meaningful.

Ugali with kuku (chicken) is worth saving for a dedicated Kenyan meal. With two chicken dishes and many breads and starches already on this menu, adding it here would dilute rather than deepen the experience.

Ethiopian communal eating: connection and hospitality

A traditional Ethiopian meal may be arranged on a large injera-lined platter, with several wat (stews) and vegetable dishes placed over the flatbread. People tear off injera to scoop food with the right hand; many families share from the same platter. Gursha, the gesture of offering another person a bite, expresses affection and hospitality. Your platter-plus-small-plate approach gives everyone the visual tradition and a comfortable way to choose their food.

Presentation: Put a large injera-lined platter or decorative serving platter at the center for display, with each stew in an accessible bowl or section. Separate meat dishes from mushroom tibs and vegetable sides. Offer serving spoons and personal plates; those interested can use injera to scoop part of their serving.

Coffee ceremony: a gathering after the feast

The Ethiopian buna coffee ceremony is an expression of hospitality and time spent together. Beans may be washed, roasted in a pan, ground and brewed in a clay jebena, then poured into small cups. Flowers or grasses sometimes decorate the setting; popcorn or roasted grains commonly accompany the drink. Three successive rounds are known as Abol (first), Tona (second) and Baraka/Bereka (blessing). Traditions vary by region and household.

Because the family meal will be early-to-mid-afternoon, hold the coffee gathering AFTER the main meal, immediately before or alongside dessert. Allow about 35–60 unhurried minutes. Three small rounds work better than full mugs. Children and non-coffee drinkers can take part with tiny servings of herbal tea, decaf tea, hot chocolate, warm spiced milk, or decaf coffee. Those replacements are family accommodations, not traditional coffee-ceremony beverages.

Set the tray with Ethiopian coffee, a grinder and brewer (jebena optional), small cups, popcorn, and flowers. Use an ordinary pot, French press or pour-over if a jebena is unavailable.
Abol: first pour and welcome. Tona: return for a second small cup and conversation. Baraka: final cup, associated with blessing; you may offer a family prayer or blessing.
Optional conversation prompts for your family (not prescribed Ethiopian ritual): a gratitude, a person to appreciate, and a hope for the coming year.

Preparation considerations and recommended order

These are flexible work blocks rather than a timed kitchen schedule. Finish the main meal in the early afternoon; serve the coffee ceremony afterward. Work backward from when you want to eat, giving the fried sides and quick sautéed dishes priority near service.

Block A — 2–4 days ahead: shop and check equipment

Buy specialty berbere, alicha kimem, matafecha, korarima, cassava flour and banana leaves early. Confirm beans/coffee, flowers, pans, oil and refrigerator space. Let the bananas ripen.
Choose whether to use optional potato dipping sauce and banana leaves. Check spice blends for turmeric if that flavor is unwanted.

Block B — Day before: long-cooking and stable dishes

Make doro wat and doro alicha; cool promptly in shallow containers and refrigerate within two hours. Add or refresh hard-boiled eggs when finishing. Reheated stews often taste even better.
Bake himbasha and defo dabo, cool completely and wrap. Make optional potato dipping sauce; marinate lamb in the refrigerator. Premeasure dry mixtures for mandazi and vitumbua.
Parboil viazi karai potatoes; clean green beans and carrots; wash and dry spinach. Chop some aromatics, labeling containers. Keep raw meat separate from ready-to-eat ingredients.

Block C — Meal day, several hours before: breads, setup, base preparations

Cook chapati and hold wrapped; warm the Ethiopian loaves near service. Make kabalagala earlier if needed, then rewarm gently. Prepare the platter, individual plates and pescatarian utensils.
Begin roasting or grilling lamb with enough time for cooking and resting. Reheat stews safely to 165°F; maintain hot dishes above 140°F if holding. Set out coffee-ceremony items before guests sit down.

Block D — Final 60–90 minutes before main meal: crisp and quick dishes

Roast berbere potatoes in uncrowded pans. Cook fasolia. Make beef tibs and mushroom tibs separately shortly before serving; spinach gomen cooks quickly and should be near the end.
Fry viazi karai in batches close to meal time for best crust; hold on a rack in a low oven if needed. Keep excess fried food from steaming under a lid.

Block E — After the meal: coffee and desserts

Begin the coffee gathering once the main course is cleared. Prepare the first small coffee round; offer hot chocolate/tea simultaneously. Let the other two rounds accompany conversation.
Mandazi are best fried near serving, but to avoid having someone stuck at the stove during the ceremony, fry shortly before the meal and rewarm lightly; vitumbua can also be cooked in advance and warmed. Set dessert portions on a shared tray.

Food safety and pacing notes

Chicken should reach 165°F internally; beef/lamb temperatures depend on cut and preferred doneness, with safe handling and resting important. Refrigerate perishable dishes within two hours of cooking or serving, and do not leave stews sitting warm for hours. Fried desserts and two potato dishes share frying equipment: consider frying viazi karai before the meal and desserts earlier or after service. The four meat entrées can retain their modest original yields because only six guests eat meat, and not everyone will sample each spicy dish.
