Why this matters now

AI is already in classrooms, offices, and homes. The question for a district is no longer whether to respond but how to build the capacity to respond well.

Students and staff are using AI at near universal rates, often without shared expectations. Bans have given way to guidance in most places, and higher education now expects students to arrive with AI literacy and explicit citation habits. At the same time, state and federal rules keep shifting, and vendors keep adding AI to tools districts already own.

There is a finite window to shape how AI enters education so that it is guided by human values like equity and inclusion, not only by the market. Across many district engagements, the same lessons keep showing up.

Policy is not the whole system

Many districts stop at a board policy. Policy sets the foundation; governance keeps it accountable and current; guidance makes it usable in daily practice. All three are needed.

The roll-out, phase by phase

Districts do not need to do everything at once. Open a phase to see what to do, who is involved, and which toolkits support the work. Phases 1 through 3 are where most districts spend their first year.

1Awareness and GroundworkBuild internal understanding, lay foundational plans, and surface what is already happening.

What to do

  • Run an AI readiness assessment: survey staff, inventory tools already in use, and review current policy. Summarize findings for leadership.
  • Charter an AI task force or policy and ethics working group with cross-role membership (leaders, teachers, instructional technology, IT, counsel, a board liaison).
  • Hold a launch session: orientation, purpose, and expectations for the development work.
  • Deliver a shared "Basics of AI" session for leaders and the task force so everyone starts from the same language.
  • Identify early adopters and existing use cases across buildings. No formal pilots yet.
  • Begin listing the family and community groups and concerns that will need to be addressed later.

Who lead

Superintendent or designee, task force chair, instructional technology lead.

Supporting resources

AI Task Force Toolkit Basics of AI presentation CGCS and CoSN Gen AI Readiness Checklist AI Tools Being Used in Education (inventory starter)
2Direction Setting and GuardrailsSet boundaries, formalize direction, and support deeper understanding.

What to do

  • Hold a collaborative visioning session: local principles, policy purpose, and scope. Write a district position statement on AI.
  • Curate and annotate sample policies and guidance from other districts and states, then co-develop a policy and guidance framework naming the required components.
  • Draft the key artifacts: AUP addendum, usage guidelines for staff and students, data rules (what may and may not be entered into AI tools), and scenario-based guidance.
  • Run two draft review and scenario-building sessions with the task force to work through gray areas and edge cases.
  • Define the AI tool vetting process (privacy, technical, instructional, risk review) and start an approved tools list.
  • Begin deeper professional learning for the development group and start designing role-based supports. Draft family messaging and FAQs.

Who lead

Task force, curriculum and instruction, IT and data privacy, legal counsel, board policy committee.

Supporting resources

3Early Implementation and FeedbackMove from planning to practice in controlled, supported ways.

What to do

  • Finalize and approve policy. Publish guidance with worked examples and the core scenarios.
  • Train the trainers: a full-day session for instructional technology facilitators and coaches to review guidance and build deployment materials for their buildings.
  • Give site and department leaders short recorded microlearning sessions plus a working session on localizing implementation.
  • Launch foundational professional learning for all staff: responsible use, evaluating AI output, and the district guidance. Record completion before tool access.
  • Run instructional pilots with a reflection cycle. Capture use cases and effective prompts.
  • Launch family communication: what AI is, how students may use it, how data is protected, the approved tools list. Offer intro sessions for parents.
  • Share a project resource hub so staff can find everything in one place.

Who lead

Instructional technology facilitators, building leaders, professional learning team, communications.

Supporting resources

4Broadening Use and System AlignmentSupport widespread use with coherence, resources, and accountability.

What to do

  • Embed AI into standing processes: tool vetting requests, risk review, new staff onboarding.
  • Integrate AI literacy into curriculum and digital citizenship. Set a teacher competency expectation that is simple, practical, and non-evaluative.
  • Offer self-paced modules and leader-specific learning alongside coaching cycles.
  • Expand AI-integrated practice across content areas and grade levels. Collect exemplars and use cases.
  • Build a communication rhythm with families that highlights student and educator work.

Who lead

Curriculum leaders, coaches, instructional technology, communications.

5Reflection, Refinement and SustainabilityEnsure long-term growth, equity, and responsible evolution.

What to do

  • Hold a reflection and revision session with the task force after the first implementation cycle. Gather feedback and lessons learned.
  • Review tools annually for privacy and effectiveness. Revisit risk assessments and guardrails using real use data.
  • Evaluate growth and gaps against the educator continuum. Set long-term AI fluency goals for every role.
  • Institutionalize what works: coaching networks, shared prompt banks, a cadence for leadership check-ins.
  • Keep family and community engagement going and make the evolution of AI use transparent.

Who lead

Cabinet, task force, data and assessment, professional learning.

The four work areas inside the toolkit

Within the Policy and Guidance strand, the task force organizes its work into four areas. Each has a "where we are now" check, resources to help, and a planned next steps table the team fills in.

Guidance and guidelinesClear expectations and common language for responsible AI use across roles, with examples to reduce gray-area confusion.
  • Overarching expectations for AI use
  • Teacher use: allowed, not allowed, must ensure
  • Student use, disclosure, and academic integrity
  • Designing assignments: prohibited, limited, with disclosure, encouraged
  • Leadership roles and family communication
Tool selection and data privacyHow the district reviews and approves AI tools, age considerations, state law alignment, and what data can and cannot be entered.
  • Tool categories: staff only, instructional for students, operational
  • Vetting: privacy and security, technical, instructional, risk, final approval
  • Age bands K to 5, 6 to 8, 9 to 12
  • Staff and student training before use
  • Unapproved tools and ongoing monitoring
Professional learningTraining priorities and roll-out supports to build staff readiness and keep implementation consistent across buildings.
  • Professional learning formats matrix (spark segments to coaching cycles)
  • AI Literate Educator Continuum: Acquire, Deepen, Create
  • Role-based tracks for leaders, coaches, teachers
  • Example roll-out plans and three implementation profiles
AI with studentsStudent expectations, AI literacy supports, and guidance for assignment and assessment design in a world where students have AI access.
  • Spectrum of opportunities: lesson, routine, event, project, program
  • Depth of literacy: swim (all), snorkel (most), scuba (some)
  • Digital citizenship competencies
  • Student guideline examples and scenario sets

All four areas live in the AI Task Force Toolkit, which a district copies and localizes.

Start from your role

The same process looks different depending on where you sit. Pick the entry point that matches your seat.

District leader

What the Leader Toolkit walks you throughA step-by-step guided build. By the end you have a Leadership Implementation Plan and a Local Guidance Package.

Everything in the toolkit carries one of three tags. District set is the floor, grounded in policy and law: you communicate and uphold it. Recommended default is the best recommendation: adopt it as written or adapt it. Your decision is a choice the toolkit frames but you call.

  • Section 1: Getting ready as a leader. Done when you can give the why in your own words, you have completed the leader readiness checklist, and you have located your context against the district phases.
  • Section 2: Understand your context. Done when your practice scan, staff capacity snapshot, decision-ownership map, stakeholder map, and risk scan are filled in.
  • Section 3: Adopt your governance. Done when you have worked the decision points for all nine guidance areas (tool approval and data privacy; professional communications; scenario response protocols; required training; academic integrity; instructional materials; assessments and grading; student use of AI tools; family and community rights) and your decision log is complete.
  • Section 4: Set your teacher toolkit expectations. Done when your require / recommend / reference table is complete and you have chosen how you will verify readiness without policing.
  • Section 5: Build your roll-out and support plan. Done when your timeline, PD plan, communication plan, team, and measures are set. Sequence: you first; staff awareness and the training gate; staff use AI in their own practice first; supported classroom pilots; broaden with reflection.
  • Section 6: Be ready for the hard moments. Done when you have read the six leader scenarios (in the Scenarios view) and adapted two for your context.

Appendices: consolidated implementation plan, governance decision log, communication calendar, and an index of linked district resources.

How education systems are putting AI to work

Field examples of what other systems have tried, grouped by area. Nearly all are back office and adult facing: the business of running the system. Three commitments show up in every one.

Human centered

AI reveals what is in the data; people verify, investigate, and decide.

Governed

Every workload enters through an intake path and is measured from the start.

On controlled infrastructure

The work runs where the system, not a vendor, holds the data.

Operations of the physical systemTransportation, food, facilities, and assets: the daily business of running a district, with no student-facing technology anywhere.
A regional collaborative focused on operational AI
C21 Canada initiative, 2026What it is

Twelve Metro Vancouver districts serving nearly three quarters of the province's students built a standing AI network. Published wins include real-time family registration and communication across languages and staff-found payroll improvements.

How it works

Superintendents attended personally rather than delegating. Deliberately few supported platforms ("when you support everything, you support nothing"). Shared principles, locally led practice.

What it suggests for a district

Leadership-level attention and governance before scale. A district does not need to invent this structure; a regional service agency or consortium usually already has it.

Device and asset tracking
Named by teams at a large urban district, 2025 readiness reviewWhat it is

Manual warranty tracking, named as cumbersome by a district's own service teams.

How it works

Runs entirely on data the technology office already owns, small in scope, through a governed intake path, measured from the start.

What it suggests for a district

A modest, universal burden and a low-risk rehearsal of the repeatable process (intake, build, measure, decide) any larger workload will need.

Transportation route intelligence
Published examples: Denver; Colorado Springs D11What it is

AI-generated routing scenarios and vehicle to student matching. Denver modeled the impact of ten school closures. A neighboring district recovered more than 17,000 hours of learning time in one year through on-time arrival.

How it works

AI produced and scored the scenario space; transportation professionals set parameters and made every call. Co-developed with a vendor rather than bought off the shelf.

What it suggests for a district

Frame it as recovered learning time, not technology. The first question is discovery: where does the routing data live today, and who controls it?

Food service forecasting and waste intelligence
Published examples: Nebraska-Lincoln; Georgia TechWhat it is

One institution cut dining food waste roughly 40 percent; another applied the same tracking to a 53 million dollar in-house operation.

How it works

Instrument the operation first: continuous waste tracking built the dataset, AI turned it into forecasting, and food service professionals kept all planning authority.

What it suggests for a district

Higher education numbers do not transfer; the pattern does. Projections stay illustrative until the district's own data speaks.

Finance, procurement, and legacy modernizationSolicitations, spend, and the legacy applications running beneath them.
Legacy modernization, proven in the field
Completed work at a large urban districtWhat it is

A pilot migration of a mainframe application was completed and met its success criteria, with AI accelerating the work.

How it works

A bounded application, chosen deliberately. Success criteria defined before work began; engineering owned every decision.

What it suggests for a district

AI-assisted rewrites are fast to build and hard to maintain without a governed platform and a named owner per workload. The discipline, as much as the technology, made the result credible.

Solicitation drafting with buyers in control
Published examples: Jordan District, UT; Equalis GroupWhat it is

District buyers draft and refine RFP criteria and scopes in minutes instead of hours. A cooperative serving roughly 1,300 districts trained a model on its own solicitation corpus.

How it works

Human in the loop by design: AI drafts, buyers keep full editorial control and final language. Grounding in institutional documents is what made outputs usable.

What it suggests for a district

A document-foundation task before it is an AI task, with counsel in the loop throughout. Procurement usually sits outside IT, so plan it as a two-office conversation.

Documents, compliance, and administrative workflowThe unstructured paper of public education, where the field evidence and district-named needs line up most densely.
Compliance documentation and high-volume correspondence
Named by teams at a large urban district, 2025 readiness reviewWhat it is

Compliance processes carrying heavy unstructured documentation, produced and managed manually; correspondence on the order of 30,000 items handled by hand.

How it works

Governed retrieval and drafting over documents the organization owns outright, on infrastructure already in the building, measured from day one.

What it suggests for a district

The strongest first proving workload: no student records, honest metrics fast, and a full rehearsal of the repeatable process. Set the expectation of workflow proof with real but modest numbers.

Policy consolidation as a byproduct of building
C21 Canada initiative: Rainy River DSB, ONWhat it is

Building a planning assistant forced consolidation of fragmented policies into one accessible location, a benefit realized before launch.

How it works

The grounding requirement did the work: to give an AI only vetted documents, the documents first had to be found, reconciled, and organized.

What it suggests for a district

The preparation an AI workload demands is itself data governance progress. Scope the consolidation into the first workload and the project doubles as records management.

Staff agents inside the organization's own tenant
C21 Canada initiative: Foothills SD, ABWhat it is

AI agents for staff workflows and family communication; AI-supported triage kept service to families whole through a staffing disruption.

How it works

Built inside the division's own tenant: a sovereignty decision reached independently, then relied on when operations were strained.

What it suggests for a district

Where it ran is the transferable choice. Name early that any family-facing communication needs a translation-quality review process.

Technology operations and cyberThe technology office's own code, tickets, and alerts. Every workload runs on data the organization already holds, for internal users first.
Technology operations workloads
Named by technology teams at a large urban district, 2025 readiness reviewWhat it is

Code completion and refactoring for developers; ticket volume, time-to-close, and bottleneck analysis with AI-aided queue management; troubleshooting keyed to error codes; AI-enabled security alert triage.

How it works

Entirely on the technology organization's own code, tickets, and logs, for internal users, with well-established reference implementations.

What it suggests for a district

Highest readiness in the landscape. Queue analysis is service-improvement analytics, about tickets and never about people. The people who would run every future workload build skills here first.

District-built micro-apps
An emerging pattern in the fieldWhat it is

Districts and individual staff increasingly build their own small AI-assisted applications: schedule helpers, form processors, dashboards, created in days rather than procured in months.

How it works

Fast to build and easy to abandon: typically no security review, no data agreement, no inventory entry, and no owner of record once the builder changes roles.

What it suggests for a district

Not whether staff build tools, but through what path. Security review, hosting, and inventory are roles a technology office already holds; the intake-and-inventory pattern below is the ready-made answer.

Analysis and decision support from the system's own dataThe question leadership is ultimately measured on. In every example AI reveals patterns worth investigating and educators verify and decide; rich data is used without any automated determination about any student.
AI analysis of standardized assessment data
C21 Canada initiative: Chinook's Edge SD, ABWhat it is

Applied AI to provincial assessment results to surface performance trends for school leadership teams.

How it works

Accuracy required careful data segmentation, learned iteratively. Vendor privacy limits pushed the work into a controlled server environment.

What it suggests for a district

Trustworthy analysis needs data organized for the question, and the controlled environment this system reached by necessity is the posture a district should assume by design.

Intervention insight from aggregate data
C21 Canada initiative: Dufferin-Peel Catholic DSB, ONWhat it is

Reading intervention insight for teachers from aggregate screener patterns, with individual student profiles avoided by design.

How it works

Co-developed by curriculum, technology, and reading specialists working only on aggregate patterns; instructional expertise owned the design.

What it suggests for a district

Rich data in and aggregate insight out can coexist, with every action a professional's. Instructional specialists led; one system's related answer was a dedicated data analyst role.

Finding schools that outperform expectations
A working example on public, aggregate dataWhat it is

A model built from public, aggregate school characteristics flags schools exceeding expectations year over year: candidates for investigation, not validated exemplars. No public ranking, no prescribed action.

How it works

Expectation-versus-actual framing, aggregate inputs only. The design answers who decided, and from what data, before anyone asks.

What it suggests for a district

Candidates for investigation is the design, not a caveat: the model finds where to look; educators determine what is happening there.

Knowledge navigation, productivity, and communicationAI supporting the adults who run the system. Chat is one interface in this area, never the point of the platform.
A coach for navigating what the system knows
A concept to exploreWhat it is

Two capabilities on one governed foundation: a walled-garden coach grounded only in vetted intervention documentation, offering ideas to consider rather than answers to obey; and guided inquiry, where a leader states what they want to understand and the tool recommends an approach and returns results with visualizations.

How it works

The credibility reference is StretchAI, launched by ISTE+ASCD in June 2026 after three years of development: walled garden, trained only on vetted research-based publications, role aware.

What it suggests for a district

The opening question is diagnostic on its own: where does knowledge about interventions live across the district today? In most systems it is scattered, and discovering that is itself a data readiness finding.

A system-owned AI at state scale
Published example: a state education departmentWhat it is

A department-built generative AI tool in its own environment: trialed in 16 schools, expanded to 50, rolled out to roughly 100,000 staff after surveys showed workload savings; students later, separately safeguarded.

How it works

Two disciplines: sovereign hosting from day one (data under departmental control, training no outside models), and expansion gated on evidence at every step, staff before students.

What it suggests for a district

Evidence for the governed platform, not a case for another chatbot. The staircase scales down as well as up: own staff first, then a small cohort, then everyone, gated on evidence.

What it suggests for a district

Everything is inventoried
The intake-to-production pathWhat it is

Every workload enters through a governed path: a live inventory of what is running, on what data, with what access, and who owns it.

How it works

Intake, reference architecture, deployment, and measurement as one repeatable path rather than case by case exceptions.

What it suggests for a district

A durable answer to the questions boards and auditors ask of every public system, and a ready home for the micro-app question. The inventory is part of the work, not overhead on it.

Reading the landscape: a sequence, not a menu

The most compelling public stories need the most preparation, and the readiest areas tell the quietest stories.

Prove (now)

Documents and technology operations: one or two proving workloads on the district's own documents and tickets; the repeatable workflow built and measured in house.

Show (next)

Operations, finance, and procurement: the visible back-office work, where a small group of interested offices can scope data access together.

Aim (the destination)

Analysis and decision support, with knowledge navigation throughout: the district's own data question answered responsibly, on a proven workflow and a governed foundation, with people deciding.

Scenarios: where guidance meets daily practice

Scenarios are how a task force tests its draft guidance and how staff learn to make consistent decisions in gray areas. Three sets below: six classroom scenarios with a recommended response, six scenarios that land on a leader's desk, and 21 discussion themes a task force can work through. Each set is from the toolkits, in full.

Six classroom scenarios

From the AI Task Force Toolkit. Each gives the situation, a recommended response, and what to do better next time.

Scenario 1: Student uses AI when it was not allowed

A student submits an assignment that appears to include AI-generated text even though the teacher had indicated that AI could not be used for this task. When asked to explain the process, the student admits they used an online AI tool because they felt unsure how to begin.

Recommended response

  • Have a calm, reflective conversation about the purpose of the assignment
  • Ask the student to walk through their thinking and identify what they understand
  • Allow the student to revise the work using approved processes
  • Document the incident according to district guidelines, focusing on learning rather than punishment

Better next time

  • Include a clear AI-use statement on every assignment
  • Provide a model of what responsible AI support looks like and what is prohibited
Scenario 2: Student enters personal information into an AI tool

A teacher discovers that a student typed their full name and the name of their school into an AI tool while seeking feedback on a writing assignment.

Recommended response

  • Explain why sharing personal information violates privacy expectations and state law (confirm the district's privacy interpretation)
  • Contact the family to inform them of the incident and the teaching steps taken
  • Reteach privacy and digital safety expectations to the class
  • Notify the appropriate district contact if additional follow-up is required

Better next time

  • Provide students with a simple, repeated script: "Never enter your name, school, or identifying details into AI tools"
  • Offer district-approved AI tools that automatically prevent PII submission
Scenario 3: AI generates inaccurate, biased, or inappropriate output

A teacher uses an AI tool to create sample math word problems. One output includes biased assumptions about students' backgrounds, and another gives an incorrect solution.

Recommended response

  • Do not use the flawed content in instruction
  • Treat this as an opportunity to model critical evaluation of AI output
  • Report the issue to the instructional technology team if the tool is district-approved
  • Document patterns of problematic output for tool-review purposes

Better next time

  • Review AI output carefully before classroom use
  • Teach students that AI tools are fallible and must be checked for accuracy and fairness
Scenario 4: Student submits work entirely produced by AI without disclosure

A high school science student submits a lab analysis with sophisticated language that does not match their in-class work. When asked, the student shares that they had AI write the full response because they "didn't know how to say it."

Recommended response

  • Ask the student to explain their understanding orally or through a guided conversation
  • Provide structured supports to rebuild the student's ability to analyze and explain findings
  • Require a resubmission with a process log or with reflection on how AI was used
  • Reinforce that disclosure is required when AI contributes to work

Better next time

  • Add checkpoints that require students to show drafts, reasoning steps, or data interpretations
  • Embed reflection prompts that AI cannot perform for them
Scenario 5: Teacher uses AI in a way that conflicts with district expectations

A teacher uses an AI tool to draft report card comments that are copied directly into the grading platform with minimal revision. Some comments are vague or mismatched to student performance.

Recommended response

  • Provide coaching on professional use of AI and the requirement for human review
  • Clarify expectations that AI may not produce final evaluative statements without teacher customization
  • Model effective use of AI as a thought partner or starting point rather than a final product
  • Offer professional learning if needed

Better next time

  • Encourage teachers to treat AI as a generator of drafts, not final outputs
  • Reinforce that evaluative communication must be personalized and accurate
Scenario 6: Approved AI tool malfunctions or produces problematic output during a lesson

A district-approved AI writing support tool begins producing repetitive or inappropriate content during a classroom activity, confusing students and interrupting instruction.

Recommended response

  • Pause student use of the tool immediately
  • Redirect the class to an alternative task
  • Capture a screenshot or description of the issue
  • Report the malfunction to the technology team so the tool can be reviewed

Better next time

  • Preview tools before live use when possible
  • Have a backup plan if technology becomes unavailable

Scenario 1: A family complaint about AI reaches your office

The floor (district set)

Families have the right to know, to ask, and to opt out without penalty. Document the objection and resolution.

Recommended move

Listen first; most complaints are requests for reassurance wearing armor. Explain the purpose and the protections in plain language: approved tools only, data agreements, no PII, the FOIL test (if it would not be released under a public records request, it does not go into AI), and the non-AI path for their child. Offer the opt-out without defensiveness. Loop the teacher in supportively; they should hear it from you before the rumor mill. Document and follow up within a week.

Your decision

Who else you involve, and your follow-up timing.

Scenario 2: A teacher is using an unapproved tool with students

The floor (district set)

Only approved tools may be used; remind and redirect first; escalation is for repeated or intentional use.

Recommended move

Assume good intent; the most common cause is enthusiasm plus not knowing the list. Conversation first: what were they trying to accomplish? Connect them with the instructional technology coach for the approved way to do it, or submit the tool for review. Review any student-facing materials it produced. Treat it as the system working, not a scandal.

Your decision

How you log it locally so a pattern would be visible.

Scenario 3: Student PII was entered into an AI tool

The floor (district set)

Immediate report to the Data Privacy Officer or designated administrator; the district handles logging, tool review, and any family notification.

Recommended move

Speed over blame. Get the report made the same day, support the staff member who came forward (they did the right thing by surfacing it), and let the district process run. Afterward, debrief with staff as a teachable moment: this is exactly why the prohibited-inputs list exists.

Your decision

How you re-teach the rule afterward without shaming the person who reported.

Scenario 4: Your staff splits: enthusiasts racing ahead, others refusing

The floor (district set)

Teachers hold the classroom call under board policy. The training gate applies to everyone who uses AI; the floor applies to everyone, period.

Recommended move

Harness the enthusiasts: make them pilots and PLC leaders, with the guardrail that being ahead does not exempt anyone from the gate or the floor. Do not force the refusers; keep PD invitational and attached to their real problems. Watch the equity seam: a student's AI experience should not depend on the luck of classroom assignment, which is an argument for shared department norms, not mandates.

Your decision

Your pilot design and pace.

Scenario 5: A teacher declines to allow AI entirely

The floor (district set)

That is their right under board policy, and their students may not be disadvantaged for it.

Recommended move

Respect it without a campaign to convert them. Keep them in the AI literacy conversation anyway: their students still live in an AI world, and ethics, critical evaluation, and disclosure norms can be taught without tool use. Revisit naturally each semester; positions soften through colleagues' results, not pressure.

Your decision

How student AI literacy is covered for students in non-AI classrooms (advisory, library or media, digital citizenship).

Scenario 6: A board member or reporter asks what your building is doing with AI

The floor (district set)

Public statements representing the district require disclosure and approval; media routes through district communications.

Recommended move

Speak confidently to your plan and the district frame: adopted policy, training before use, approved tools with data agreements, family rights, and a teacher toolkit behind every classroom. Route formal media requests through district communications. Never improvise numbers.

1. Cheating and academic integrityCheating with AI

General issue: Cheating with AI

Scenario 1

During a history class, a high school junior submits a five-page essay on the causes of World War I. The writing is far more advanced than their usual work, with flawless grammar and nuanced arguments. When the teacher asks about specific points in the paper, the student struggles to explain them. Later, it is revealed that the essay was generated entirely by ChatGPT with no student edits.

Scenario 2

A middle school student takes home a math packet and uses an AI-powered math solver app to complete every problem. The answers are correct, but the teacher notices the student did not show any work and has difficulty explaining even the basic steps when called on in class.

Items to consider

  • Is this an intentional violation, or does the student not fully understand when AI use becomes dishonest?
  • Does the assessment format (take-home quizzes, essays) unintentionally encourage AI misuse?
  • How can teachers design tasks where process and student reasoning matter more than final answers?
  • What supports or conversations about ethical use are missing?

Opportunities and positive angles

  • Forces rethinking of assessment strategies toward oral defenses, authentic tasks, or portfolio-based work.
  • Opens discussions about transparency and digital citizenship.

Stakeholders

Teachers, students, parents, administrators.

Equity considerations

Students with greater access to premium AI tools at home may have more opportunities to cheat, widening performance gaps.

General issue: Messaging about AI adoption in schools

Scenario 1

A superintendent posts a district-wide announcement celebrating AI as a "game-changer" for schools. The message excites some staff but alarms others, especially parents who fear for student data. Without nuance, the statement sparks community pushback at the next board meeting.

Scenario 2

A principal sends a weekly newsletter drafted with AI. Parents appreciate the polished format, but one parent realizes the AI mistakenly listed the wrong date for parent-teacher conferences. Families begin questioning whether important school messages can be trusted if they are machine-generated.

Items to consider

  • How can leaders balance enthusiasm with transparency and caution?
  • Should schools disclose when AI is used in official communications?
  • What opportunities exist to involve parents and students in the conversation?

Opportunities and positive angles

  • Builds credibility when leaders model thoughtful, ethical AI use.
  • Engages the wider community in shaping AI policy.

Stakeholders

Administrators, parents, students, school boards.

Equity considerations

Families with limited tech literacy may feel more excluded or skeptical without targeted communication efforts.

General issue: Rolling out AI without adequate planning or support

Scenario 1

A district adopts an AI-powered grading system with the promise of reducing teacher workload. Teachers quickly realize the tool produces inconsistent feedback. Some teachers spend more time double-checking than before. Without clear protocols, frustration grows, and adoption stalls.

Scenario 2

A state education department mandates AI literacy modules for all students. However, schools receive little guidance on how to integrate the modules into already-packed curricula. Teachers scramble to fit them in, often cutting existing lessons. The rushed rollout causes resentment and confusion.

Scenario 3

Teachers want to implement AI to increase efficiency but do not have time to implement the teaching of how to use AI. Since AI is not on the state tests, "what gets measured is what gets done." Teachers will be all over the spectrum on their ability to use it effectively, so students get inconsistent support: some classes are taught heavily and others avoid it. Teachers are not given PD on AI use.

Items to consider

  • Are teachers and staff given time to test AI tools before full adoption?
  • How do districts evaluate tools for reliability before purchase?
  • What ongoing PD or troubleshooting systems are in place?
  • Are there parents who want to opt their children out of AI education?
  • Will secondary teachers be required to participate in AI if they have fears about it?
  • If teachers are permitted to avoid AI in education, is this keeping the students in their class from developing skills needed for their generation?

Opportunities and positive angles

  • Provides a chance to involve teachers early in pilot programs.
  • Can encourage cross-district collaboration and shared resources.
  • AI and tech as a related art: the library can expand to include multimedia and AI use.
  • AI and thinking offered as an elective.
  • Students who are interested in AI have opportunities to explore in class lessons.

Stakeholders

District leaders, teachers, curriculum directors, unions.

Equity considerations

Schools with fewer staff or resources may struggle more with rushed rollouts, leaving them behind in implementation.
