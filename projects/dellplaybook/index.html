About

Who I am and why districts talk to me

Before Dell I spent my career on the district side of the table: teacher, instructional coach, administrator, and executive director over technology and instruction, including nine years as a director at a BOCES/RIC in New York. I hold a PhD in Teaching, Curriculum and Change and serve on the ISTE+ASCD National Faculty.

What that means for you: when a superintendent or curriculum director sits down with me, they're talking with someone who has held their job. That opens conversations a portfolio pitch can't, including with the decision-makers who rarely take a traditional vendor meeting, and it keeps doors open for you across leadership transitions that would otherwise reset an account.

Two things I don't do, on purpose: I don't close, and I won't pitch into a listening meeting. Districts trust me because I'm not the one asking for the order, and that trust is what I bring to your accounts. The full picture of the role: The Role of the Strategist.

The Cues

When to bring me in

The best habit is a standing one: set a cadence with me to walk your account set, so the moments below land on an existing relationship. And when you hear yourself say one of these, that's the cue.

"The superintendent wants to talk about AI, and I don't want to walk in with a product deck."

My most common engagement right now: AI strategy conversations that start from the district's own commitments, from a first briefing through project scoping.

"The district's instructional people will be in the room."

Curriculum directors and instructional leaders evaluate you differently than IT does. I help you prepare for that room, or I join you in it.

"This account is stalled. They take my calls but nothing moves."

A listening-first engagement built around the district's published plans often restarts what transactional outreach can't.

"They think e-rate or a grant will pay for this, and I'm not sure that's right."

Funding confusion loses deals late. I'll tell you what a funding claim actually means before you build a forecast on it.

"Their tech plan or RFP is on the street, and our response should speak their language."

I build crosswalks from what the district has committed to in writing, so our response reads like it was written by someone who read their plan. Because it was.

"There's a conference in my state. Should we be there, and how?"

Conference strategy, session content, and the organizer relationships. Start with the Regional Conference Playbook below, then let's talk.

A good ask looks like

"I'm working with [district] in [state]. They've published a plan with [a goal you noticed], and [the moment: the superintendent asked for our point of view / the account is stalled / a meeting is coming]. It's in [timeframe] with [who's in the room]. I'd like your help shaping the conversation, and I think you should be in the room. Can we talk this week?"

Account, timing, what the district has said, who's in the room, and what you need. That's everything I need to say yes quickly.
