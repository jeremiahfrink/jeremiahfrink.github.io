The Role

The Role of the Strategist

What an Education Strategist is, what they actually do, and how to put yours to work. Five minutes here will change how you use the role.

The Idea

What a strategist is

Every strategist on this team is a career educator: people who taught, coached, led buildings and districts, and ran regional and national education work before Dell. When a superintendent, CTO, or curriculum director sits down with a strategist, they're talking with someone who has held their job. That credibility opens conversations a portfolio pitch can't, and it keeps doors open long after a meeting ends.

Strategists work with districts at no cost to the district: facilitating planning, delivering professional learning, connecting districts working on similar problems, and serving as a partner to think with on whatever the district is navigating. The work is real service to the field, and that's the point.

The principle behind the role: serving the field well is how we serve the organization. A strategist makes you more effective in K-12 and more respected in K-12, and those are the same goal.

The Work

What strategists actually do

Four kinds of work, all of which you can put in motion for your accounts.

District engagement

Listening-first sessions built from the district's own published commitments, AI strategy conversations with leadership, meeting preparation and presence, and long-arc relationship building with districts and regional organizations.

The mode: questions do the listening, field observations do the showcasing. No pitch in a listening meeting, ever.

Documents built for districts

Crosswalks that map a district's own plan, goal by goal, to where we genuinely fit (including honest "district-led" labels where we don't), plan reviews, district profiles, AI field examples, and question sets for specific meetings.

Every document starts from the district's language, not our portfolio. The honesty is the credibility mechanism.

Seller enablement

The Understanding K-12 session series, state and regional fluency briefings before your meetings, conference strategy, and the quick-reference resources on this site.

The goal: every AE can hold a two-minute education conversation before any product word appears.

Student programs & field presence

Connecting districts to Girls Who Game, Student TechCrew, Data Dunkers, and Tech Career Circuit; sessions and presence at the state and regional conferences where your accounts actually show up.

Program conversations land with leadership in a way product conversations can't.

The Exchange

How an engagement works

You bringYour strategist doesYou get back
The account context, anything the district has published (strategic plan, tech plan, board priorities), who will be in the room and what they own, and what you want to be true afterward The preparation, the session or conversation itself where it helps, and the documents: crosswalks, profiles, question sets, follow-up framing in the district's own language A session capture, an honest read on where the district actually is, and concrete next steps scoped to who has the authority to act on them. The relationship stays yours

One timing rule: bring your strategist in early. A strategy conversation shaped from the beginning goes further than one they're parachuted into the day before. And for a first-time introduction to a district, use the intro email templates and check with your strategist on framing first.

The Boundaries

What strategists don't do, on purpose

  • They don't close. The transactional conversation, pricing, and deal mechanics stay with you and your DM. Districts trust strategists precisely because they're not the ones asking for the order.
  • They're not demo resources or product SMEs. For deep technical configuration, you bring in the right specialist together.
  • They won't pitch into a listening meeting. If a district was promised a listening conversation, that's what they get. The showcasing happens through the quality of the questions.
  • They won't force a fit. If what a district needs isn't something we do well, your strategist will say so, to the district and to you. That honesty is why districts keep inviting us back, and it's worth more to your account over time than one stretched deal.

Getting Started

What a good ask looks like

Any outreach works, but a well-framed ask gets a faster, better answer. The shape:

A good ask looks like

"I'm working with [district] in [state]. They've published a plan with [a goal you noticed], and [the moment: the superintendent asked for our point of view / the account is stalled / a meeting is coming]. It's in [timeframe] with [who's in the room]. I'd like your help shaping the conversation, and I think you should be in the room. Can we talk this week?"

Account, timing, what the district has said, who's in the room, and what you need. That's everything a strategist needs to say yes quickly.
