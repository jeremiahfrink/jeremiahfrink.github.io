"I'm trying to understand a district"

Structure, people, and priorities: the map before the meeting.

Who actually decides what in a district?
Quick answer

The board governs, the superintendent and cabinet manage, and buildings adopt. Map every district contact on two axes: what they can decide (operations) and what they can spend (money). They are rarely the same people.

WhoWhat they ownWhat they care about
School boardPolicy, budget approval, hiring the superintendent. Governance, not management.Community trust, defensibility, cost
SuperintendentRuns the district; the CEOThe strategic story, and whether it's board-defensible
CFO / business officialThe money and the funding sourcesTotal cost, which pot pays, compliance
Asst. supt. C&I / CAOCurriculum, instruction, PDLearning outcomes, educator capacity
CTO / tech directorInfrastructure, devices, systemsSecurity, manageability, support burden
Principals & coachesBuilding-level adoptionWhether it works in real classrooms
ProcurementHow buying is allowed to happenBids, contracts, vehicles, thresholds

The credibility line outsiders cross: pitching operational detail to a board member, or strategy to an operations person. Match the conversation to the authority.

The adoption trap: a signed district contract is not classroom adoption. Principals, coaches, and teachers decide whether the thing actually gets used.

What are BOCES, ESCs, IUs, and co-ops, and why do they keep coming up?
Quick answer

Educational Service Agencies: regional intermediaries between state and districts. They run shared services and, crucially, cooperative purchasing. Often the contract vehicle, the credibility channel, and the sell-once-reach-many aggregation point in a region.

StateWhat they're called
New YorkBOCES (and 12 RICs within them)
PennsylvaniaIntermediate Units (IUs)
OhioEducational Service Centers (ESCs)
ElsewhereESDs, AEAs, co-ops; names vary, function repeats

New York's version has real contract mechanics that trip up deals. Full explainer: NYS & BOCES/RICs Explained.

How do I read a district in ten minutes?
Quick answer

Grab the snapshot facts, then look through three lenses: what the cabinet is consumed by, what the tech team is carrying, and what the board and community are pushing. If those three lists look the same, you haven't looked hard enough.

The snapshot (five facts, all findable fast): superintendent and their tenure, how the board is constituted, who the technology chief reports to, enrollment and its direction, and how the budget gets approved (board vote, town meeting, referendum, or a city council's appropriation).

LensWhat consumes their attention
Superintendent & cabinetMoney, headcount, enrollment, facilities, labor, board relations, and whatever is currently drawing public criticism
Technology & digital learning leadersRefresh cycles, contract reviews, privacy obligations, security exposure, and the gap between what leadership promised and what the team can staff
Board & communityThe named organizations that show up: parent advocacy groups, the union, the coalitions. In large districts this lens often sets the agenda for the other two

Two labels that change how you walk in: for anything live in the district, know whether it's settled (context you can reference) or actively contested (a relationship risk you step around). And sort every policy fact into three layers: inherited from the state (one line, see the state guide), carved out (where this district is exempt or uniquely burdened, and where the best conversation usually lives), or purely its own (the plan, the board politics, the org chart).

The layer test: would the neighboring district in the same state have the same fact? If yes, it's inherited and worth one line. If no, it deserves your attention.

"I'm timing my outreach"

The district calendar decides when doors open. Align to it instead of chasing it.

When does the money actually move?
Quick answer

Budget development runs roughly November through February, boards adopt in spring, and the fiscal year opens July 1. If you show up in April with a new idea, you're a year late.

Budget development (the window where ideas get funded):

JulAugSepOctNovDecJanFebMarAprMayJun

Device decisions land December through February, not spring. Academic and PD decisions lock by February or March, so those conversations happen summer through fall. POs concentrate in late summer and again before fiscal year close.

When should I reach out, and about what?
Quick answer

Match the touch to the district's moment: vision conversations in summer, budget-shaping in fall, decision support in winter, delivery and relationships in spring.

The two clocks: Dell's H2 and the district's fiscal year don't line up. Territory owners run both plays at once: closing activity in one hand, next-season relationship building in the other.

"What's different in my state?"

The same conversation lands differently in every state. Open yours before the meeting.

State notes: route to market, privacy, phones, AI, and the thing to watch
Quick answer

Five facts per state below. For the full picture, use the State-by-State Guide.

New York
  • Route to market: BOCES is near-mandatory outside the Big 5 (NYC, Buffalo, Rochester, Syracuse, Yonkers, who can't use it). BOCES Aid changes the district's net cost, so "is it aidable?" often decides before price does. Highest-leverage action: get listed as a BOCES service.
  • Privacy: Ed Law 2-d is the region's strictest: six required contract disclosures, a Data Protection Officer signs, and a 7-calendar-day vendor breach clock most standard MSAs can't meet. Statewide registry makes compliance portable, except NYC, which runs its own process.
  • Phones: bell-to-bell statewide, year two. District-issued devices are expressly exempt, making them the only sanctioned classroom screen just as ESSER-bought fleets age out.
  • AI: no state guidance, statute, or money at all; BOCES, RICs, and NYSCATE fill the gap regionally.
  • Watch: piggybacking authority sunsets June 2027 and the state IT umbrella contract expires December 2026 with no successor; the regional layer, not a national co-op, is the aggregation route.
Pennsylvania
  • Route to market: 29 Intermediate Units with a strong contracting rail (PEPPM, KPN) but no aid incentive pulling districts through it. A PEPPM listing is the standard entry.
  • Privacy: no statute at all; the working regime is contractual through the IU13-run alliance and the PA standard agreement.
  • Phones: not law; the state funded storage pouches instead and uptake was minimal.
  • AI: nothing from the state; district adoption is running well ahead of policy, which makes helping districts think the door.
  • Watch: cyber charter tuition drains roughly $1B a year from districts and dominates the money conversation.
New Jersey
  • Route to market: split in two: county offices hold regulatory power with no purchasing role; ESCs purchase with no regulatory role. An ESCNJ award travels; a national co-op award alone does not.
  • Privacy: no student privacy statute; a voluntary alliance is the closest thing to a standard.
  • Phones: in force this school year, bell to bell, K-12, buses and events included.
  • AI: advisory guidance, stale since early 2025; bills parked in committee.
  • Watch: bid threshold is $53K with a Qualified Purchasing Agent ($39K without), the least piggyback-friendly rules in the region.
Maryland
  • Route to market: 24 county-scale districts and no regional layer; MEEC (all 24 are members) is the rail, and a MEEC award exempts districts from their own bid requirement.
  • Privacy: statutory and per-district; not a registry state, so plan for 24 separate agreements.
  • Phones: law in force; board policies due by 2027-28 with annual reporting.
  • AI: the strongest posture in the region: statute plus guidance, a named state AI coordinator, district policies due fall 2026, and a procurement rubric districts must use. Compliance is the door.
  • Watch: cybersecurity certification due June 2027 at all 24 systems, and the Blueprint funding cliff shapes every budget conversation.
Massachusetts
  • Route to market: collaboratives are strong on services, weak on technology purchasing; the statewide contract or MHEC cooperative (covers K-12 through June 2029) is the path.
  • Privacy: contractual via the TEC alliance: sign once, reuse statewide.
  • Phones: not law; legislation stalled in conference.
  • AI: state guidance plus educator training, with district policy work phasing in.
  • Watch: town meeting and council appropriation under Proposition 2.5 sets the money rhythm; DESE guidance versus district-built approaches is a live AI tension.
Virginia
  • Route to market: no regional layer with purchasing power; cooperative-friendly procurement fills the gap. The standard privacy agreement travels through the VSPA consortium.
  • Privacy: SOPIPA-style statute plus the consortium exhibit; a spring 2026 breach on the state-provided LMS made notification duties feel real statewide.
  • Phones: bell to bell in force since July 2026.
  • AI: a mandate technically in force with state guidance still unpublished, so the local duty hasn't triggered; districts are waiting.
  • Watch: 40-plus localities voted on a 1% school capital sales tax in November 2026; the funding formula redesign is funded but stalled.
Connecticut
  • Route to market: six RESCs that are large operators in their own right: sell to them, not through them. No aid incentive moves districts through the regional layer.
  • Privacy: the strictest in the region: a non-conforming contract is void. Signing the state Model TOS Addendum and registering on the Educational Software Hub is cheap and genuinely differentiating, since only a fraction of tracked products comply.
  • Phones: not law; an advisory State Board position recommends bell to bell with no deadline.
  • AI: advisory, from an agency outside education, and it counsels against standalone AI policies; the binding piece is computer science including AI, effective July 2026, unfunded.
  • Watch: the foundation funding number hasn't moved since 2013 and the FY2028 fix is unwritten; budgets are town-by-town, mechanism set by each charter.
Rhode Island
  • Route to market: effectively no regional layer; EdTechRI membership unlocks both master purchasing agreements and the privacy alliance, and it works by phone call rather than published process.
  • Privacy: thin student data chapter; the hard obligation is the general breach law, including identity protection for minors until age 18 plus two years, the most expensive single breach obligation in the region.
  • Phones: in force since August 2026 as a district policy duty, with the state defining the school day.
  • AI: advisory by its own admission, but $18M in federal AI-literacy grants landed in August 2026, so funded activity is starting.
  • Watch: the 2027 funding formula rewrite is already costed by district name (Providence gains, several suburbs lose), and it will dominate leadership attention.
Vermont
  • Route to market: the supervisory union is not a channel, it's the operating layer: it employs the superintendent, runs the business office, and usually runs technology. Sell to the SU or you are not selling. Seven new CESAs are still deciding what they are; get in front of their directors.
  • Privacy: duties sit on the vendor; sign the Vermont standard agreement through the state-coordinated alliance.
  • Phones: in force, arrival to dismissal, all grades.
  • AI: the thinnest advisory guidance in the region: no mandate, money, or training.
  • Watch: Town Meeting Day (first Tuesday of March) is the earliest budget calendar in the territory; a proposal not at a board by roughly October has missed the year. The excess-spending penalty makes every new dollar contested.
New Hampshire
  • Route to market: no regional layer and the legislature declined to build one; the CTO Council is the decision channel, and a Massachusetts cooperative runs the privacy alliance.
  • Privacy: the sharpest single constraint in the territory: monitoring software on a student device requires a public hearing plus written parental consent, adding a public meeting to that whole product category.
  • Phones: in force since July 2025, first bell to dismissal, with an annual review duty boards tend to forget.
  • AI: nothing published from the state; the only artifact is a voluntary association document.
  • Watch: March votes with February deliberative sessions; a failed budget triggers an automatic default budget.
Maine
  • Route to market: no regional layer; the loosest procurement in the territory (no general bidding threshold, entirely local board policy).
  • Privacy: sign the free, Department-sponsored state privacy agreement.
  • Phones: a uniform bell-to-bell rule with adoption due August 2026.
  • AI: advisory toolkit with no mandate or money; a 2026 law on AI companion chatbots binds vendors with real penalties.
  • Watch: establish the status of the state 1:1 device program before quoting anything on devices; budgets pass at a meeting, then must be validated by referendum within 45 days.
Delaware
  • Route to market: a 42-account state (19 districts plus 23 charters) with no regional layer and no state privacy agreement, so every deal negotiates alone. The state education department itself is the service agency: statewide SIS, SSO, assessment.
  • Privacy: operator-facing statute; arrive with its terms already in your own paper (45-day deletion, no targeted ads, no sale, no profiling, subprocessor flow-down).
  • Phones: policy mandate with posting due August 31, 2026; the statute is silent on district-issued devices.
  • AI: advisory guidance plus a strategic target of 75% of districts and charters using AI tools by 2028 "with governance compliance," undefined.
  • Watch: the four-district northern New Castle County consolidation (43,000+ students) is in motion, legislative approval 2027 at the earliest; deals there should anticipate it.
West Virginia
  • Route to market: 55 county systems and no regional tier; WVDE's Division of Technology is the de facto rail (statewide network, data center, E-rate, student system). Build that relationship first; there is no third door.
  • Privacy: unusual prohibitions matter for AI and analytics: no emotion-detection ("affective computing") data, no biometric reporting; vendor contracts must carry safeguards and penalties. No state agreement, so 55 separate reviews.
  • Phones: in force, instructional time only (not bell to bell); every violation must be logged in the state student system.
  • AI: the state calls its AI and Digital Science Framework first in the nation: universal AI literacy before graduation, statewide by 2027-28. A strong expectation with a firm date, not a mandate.
  • Watch: the universal Hope Scholarship and enrollment decline shape every county's math, and eight counties are under state intervention, where a state-appointed superintendent signs.
Washington DC
  • Route to market: not one district: 66 LEAs. Every fact is one of three things: citywide (OSSE, reaching ~100,000 students), DCPS-only (one buyer, ~52,000), or each charter separately (up to 65 decisions, ~48,000, no central gatekeeper). Confusing the three wastes a year.
  • Deal structure is strategy: a multiyear contract of any value dies by default without affirmative Council approval; a single-year deal under $1M needs no Council involvement.
  • Phones: a Council law binds all 66 LEAs, and storage is an unfunded problem across all of them: 66 buyers with the same need.
  • AI: no citywide gate; DCPS has its own acceptable-use policy and mandatory security review of every application, and several charter networks are ahead of DCPS. Don't assume DCPS leads.
  • Watch: leadership transition through mid-2027 (new mayor, interim chancellor) means slow, risk-averse DCPS decisions; the charter sector is not in transition and can move now.
Ohio (territory overlap)
  • Route to market: 51 ESCs plus 16 ITCs; the ITC hosts the student system and runs co-ops, making it the natural relationship.
  • Procurement: a statutory bid exemption for instructional computer hardware and software.
  • Phones: in force since January 2026.
  • AI: a real mandate: model policy plus district adoption required by July 2026.
  • Watch: universal vouchers (~$1B/yr) ruled unconstitutional and on appeal; levy mechanics under HB 920 freeze existing revenue yield.

Condensed from the cross-state comparison work, verified August 2026. These facts move; when a deal turns on one of them, check the full guide or ask your strategist.

"I'm walking into a conversation"

What's on their minds, and how to show up ready.

What are the four conversations happening in every district?
Quick answer

AI, screen time and devices, student data privacy, and the technology funding squeeze. Every district in the territory is somewhere on all four, and each one has a question that opens it.

ConversationWhere districts areThe question that opens it
AIGovernance before tools: policy, guardrails, adult readiness. State postures range from real mandates to total silence"Where is your AI policy work right now, and who owns it?"
Screen time & devicesCommunity pushback on screens colliding with aging 1:1 fleets and refresh decisions. Cell phones live under this heading but are a different conversation: personal-device restriction laws (bell-to-bell in much of the territory), where district-issued devices are usually exempt and storage is often unfunded. Don't mix the two up; a leader frustrated about phones may still be fully committed to their 1:1 program"How are you telling the device story to families this year?" and, separately, "How is the phone policy going, and what did it change in classrooms?"
Student data privacyRegimes vary enormously by state, from sign-once registries to contract-by-contract negotiation to statutes that void non-conforming contracts"What does your vendor approval path look like?"
Funding & the rising price of technologyPost-ESSER budgets moving cautiously just as component supply and pricing pressure push refresh costs up, with unfunded mandates (phone storage, security builds) competing for the same dollars"How is the technology budget conversation changing for you this year?"

Where a district sits on each is often set by its state; check "What's different in my state?" below before the meeting. These four are also how one district can be read against another, which is exactly what district profiles do.

How do I prepare for an intro meeting?
Quick answer

Read their published plans, know who's in the room and what they own, and bring questions built from their commitments. Questions do the listening; your prep does the impressing.

  • Their priorities: strategic plan and recent board minutes read, two or three of their own commitments pulled out to ask about
  • The room: each attendee mapped to what they decide and care about (see the who-decides table above)
  • Your state fluency: current initiatives, the data privacy law that governs them, and how they're allowed to buy (state notes above; as one example, in New York expect Ed Law 2-d to come up early)
  • The two-minute standard: you can talk about what districts in this state are working on before any product word appears
  • One thing of value to leave: an introduction, a field observation, a useful resource; something that matters in a year, not doughnuts

The intro-meeting rule: if it was framed as a listening conversation, it stays one. The pitch waits for the meeting that's about the pitch.

What funding might be in play, and what should I never assume?
Quick answer

Six pots: operational budget, capital and bonds, federal Titles, e-rate, competitive grants, and the procurement vehicle that governs how they're allowed to buy. When someone names a funding source, ask a clarifying question before you build a forecast on it.

PotPays forWatch out
Operational budgetThe everyday: staff, refresh, licensesBuilt Nov-Feb; adopted in spring
Capital / bondsBuildings and big infrastructureVoter approval and long timelines in many states
Federal TitlesTargeted populations and programsCompliance strings on every dollar
E-rateOn-premises network onlyNever laptops, never hotspots (off-campus eligibility rescinded 2025)
Competitive grantsSpecific initiativesSlow, restricted, real work; no guaranteed Dell return
Procurement vehiclesNothing; they're how buying is allowedContract-specific rules (see BOCES explainer)

Field flag: "e-rate is paying for the devices" is almost always actually carrier, OEM, or cooperative contract pricing. Ask which contract; the answer tells you who the real customer is.
