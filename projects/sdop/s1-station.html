Preparing the lesson and the space

Thought partner

The curriculum is given. The shape of how it lands is not. Everything here happens before a resource exists.

Available, underusedThought partner

Scenario: The unit is set and the pacing guide says five days. The teacher knows that teaching it whole group will lose half the class by day two, and has never built stations before.

Strategy: Find the arc of the unit before designing anything, then build the rotation around the places students actually stall.

Before you go in

Put the given unit into Gemini Notebook. Ask where the arc is, and where three different kinds of learner would lose it.

Do not ask it to build the rotation. Ask what the rotation has to solve.

With them

Build the rotation out loud, in front of them. Let them pick which station is theirs to run and which one they want you to model.

K–8: you stay on the Notebook side. 9–12: students can be handed the same Notebook to prepare with.
You leave withA rotation plan for one real lesson next week.
Watch out forDescribe students by need, never by name. No student identifiers in the notebook.
Then what: the stations need materials. is where you build them.
Available, underusedThought partner

Scenario: A teacher's Classroom is one long scroll of undated posts. Students cannot find last week's work, families cannot see what the unit is, and the teacher has stopped trying.

Strategy: Give the unit a structure in Classroom before adding one more thing to it.

Before you go in

Draft the topic structure and the sequence from the given curriculum, so you arrive with a proposal instead of a blank screen.

With them

Tag standards and skills together. The tagging has to be theirs or it will not survive past October.

Standards and skills tagging has been sitting in Classroom all year. Almost nobody has opened it.
You leave withOne unit tagged and sequenced in a real Classroom.
Watch out forIf you drive the tagging, it is your structure and they will abandon it.
Then what: the assignment now has a home, so the rubric is worth generating. .
EstablishedThought partner

Scenario: A lesson has been taught the same way for three years and it is fine. Some classes get it and some do not, and the teacher cannot say why.

Strategy: Name the three places the lesson loses students, then open the conversation with one of them.

Before you go in

Give Gemini the lesson as written. Ask where a student loses the thread. Push back on the generic answers until you get three that are specific to this content.

With them

Open with one. Not three. One is a conversation, three is a critique.

You leave withOne opening question you would actually use.
Watch out forThe first answer is always generic. The value is in the third.
Then what: you guessed where it breaks. tells you whether you guessed right.
Available, underusedThought partner

Scenario: A teacher moved from fifth grade to second in August. They have the curriculum, a box of someone else's materials, and no idea what next Tuesday looks like.

Strategy: Build one week, not one year, out of whatever was left behind.

Before you go in

Load the new grade's curriculum plus last year's leftovers into one Notebook. Mixed file types are fine now, slides and PDFs included.

With them

Walk one week. A person who changed grades in August cannot hear a year plan in September.

You leave withOne week, laid out, for someone currently underwater.
Watch out forInherited materials often contain student work. Check before you upload anything.
Then what: you will be back here every week. is how you stop re-establishing context each visit.
EstablishedThought partner

Scenario: The assessment comes with the curriculum. The teacher did not write it, cannot change it, and wants to know their students are ready for it.

Strategy: Work backwards from the assessment to the skills and knowledge it actually requires, then compare that against what the pacing guide teaches.

Before you go in

Put a released or sample version of the assessment into Gemini. Work backwards item by item to what a student has to be able to do.

With them

Lay that list next to the pacing guide and find what is assessed but never taught. Talk about the gap. The gap is where the coaching is.

You leave withA named gap between what is assessed and what is paced.
Watch out forSecure assessments stay out of every AI tool. Use a released or sample version only.
Then what: a named gap needs something to close it. is where you build it.

Showing up better

For you

Nothing here produces something for a teacher. It produces time, or it produces you arriving better prepared. Both count. Pick this station if you are tired.

EstablishedAssistant

Scenario: You support eleven teachers across three buildings. By the time you have remembered what you agreed with the third one, the visit is half over.

Strategy: Put one teacher's context somewhere it stays, so you arrive knowing instead of remembering.

Build it once

A Gem holding the grade, the subject, the curriculum they are given, what they are good at, what worries them, and what you are working on together.

What it buys you

You stop re-explaining the same context every time you need to think about this person. Gems are shareable here, so a co-coach can use it too.

You leave withOne Gem, for one teacher, usable Monday.
Watch out forDescribe the role, not the person. Nothing evaluative you would not say to their face.
Then what: use it to arrive with the lesson question already worked out. .
Available, underusedThought partner

Scenario: You have been assigned a chemistry teacher and your background is elementary literacy. You can talk about instruction all day, and you cannot ask a single question about their content.

Strategy: Read the curriculum well enough to ask one real question, and be honest about what that is and is not.

Build it once

Load the curriculum into Notebook. Run the quiz and the flashcards against yourself to find where you are thin.

What it buys you

You walk into a subject you do not know with a question that proves you read it. That is the whole credibility problem, in fifteen minutes.

You leave withOne question for a teacher whose content is not yours.
Watch out forIt makes you a credible reader, not a content expert. Say that to them out loud.
Then what: now you can work backwards from their assessment. .
New in AugustAssistant

Scenario: You built something good in a notebook last spring. Two colleagues wanted it and you ended up rebuilding it twice, slightly differently each time.

Strategy: Take a copy of something already built and make it yours, instead of starting over.

Build it once

Take the copy of the staying sane notebook, then swap in your own sources: your schools, your curriculum, the questions you get every week.

What it buys you

This is also the demonstration. Copying a shared notebook shipped in August and it is the most useful new thing on this page for anyone who supports a lot of people.

You leave withYour own copy, with at least one source that is yours.
Watch out forA copy does not stay in sync. From the moment you take it, it is yours alone.
Then what: the same copy move is how you serve a whole grade team at once. .
EstablishedThought partner

Scenario: One teacher makes the same objection every time and it stops you every time. You have never had a good answer ready, only a defensive one.

Strategy: Rehearse against the objection in private so the first time you say it well is not in front of them.

Build it once

A Gem that makes their actual objection, in their actual wording. Not the polite version.

What it buys you

Somewhere to be wrong first. You arrive with a two minute answer that does not sound like a pitch.

You leave withAn answer you have already said out loud once.
Watch out forIf the Gem convinces you the teacher is wrong, you built it badly. Make it argue well.
Then what: arguments rarely win this. An artifact sometimes does. .
EstablishedAssistant

Scenario: You write the same three messages every week. The follow up after a visit, the scheduling one, and the one explaining the same tool to a different person.

Strategy: Build the three once and stop starting from an empty box.

Build it once

Name the three you actually send, not the three you think you should send. Build a Gem for each, or one Gem that handles all three.

What it buys you

Minutes, repeatedly. This is the least interesting item on the page and probably the highest return.

You leave withOne message you will never write from scratch again.
Watch out forAnything going to families should still sound like you. Read it before you send it.
Then what: spend the time you just saved on the teacher who needs it. Any station.

Building the resources

Assistant

Something exists at the end of this station that did not exist before. Pick the one your teacher would actually use, not the one that shows off the most features.

New to usAssistant

Scenario: The assignment exists and students complete it. Grading takes forever, feels inconsistent between classes, and students argue about the score because nobody can point at anything.

Strategy: Generate a rubric from the assignment they already have, then cut it down together until it is something they would really use.

Before you go in

Generate the draft rubric in Classroom from their existing assignment. Bring a draft, not a demonstration.

With them

Cut it together. Generated rubrics are always too long and too generous with criteria. The cutting is the coaching.

You leave withOne rubric a teacher actually agreed to.
Watch out forA rubric nobody shortened is a rubric nobody uses. Four criteria beats nine.
Then what: feedback needs something to point at. .
Available, underusedAssistant

Scenario: A teacher has a rubric they have used for six years. It works. It lives on paper in a drawer, and it is not attached to anything students can see.

Strategy: Digitize what already works instead of replacing it with something better.

Before you go in

Nothing. This one is a genuine walk up. Bring a phone.

With them

Photograph the rubric. It comes back digital and attaches to the assignment. Nothing about their practice changed, which is exactly why it lands on a skeptic.

You leave withOne paper artifact, now working in Classroom.
Watch out forPhotograph the rubric, not a page of graded student work.
Then what: the assignment now deserves a structured home. .
New in AugustPersonalization

Scenario: The whole grade is assigned the same text. The reading range in those rooms is four years wide, and every teacher is solving it alone at nine at night.

Strategy: Build the leveled set once, then share it as a copy so each teacher builds on top of it rather than starting over.

Before you go in

Build it once in Notebook against the text everyone is already assigned. Three levels, same content, same ideas.

With them

Share the copy. Each teacher adds their own sources on top without touching yours.

This is the August capability, and it is the difference between helping one teacher and helping eleven.
You leave withA shareable notebook and a list of who gets it.
Watch out forLeveling is not tracking. Levels move with the student and no student gets labeled.
Then what: in grades 9 to 12 students can copy it themselves. .
New in AugustAssistant

Scenario: A teacher delivers the same ten minute introduction four times a day, and again individually for every student who was absent. It is the same ten minutes every time.

Strategy: Record it once from the deck they already have, and get those ten minutes back for something else.

Before you go in

Find the explanation they repeat most. Usually it is the unit opener or the one procedure everyone forgets.

With them

Record straight from Slides. No new tool, no new file to manage, and it is not AI, which matters for some of your teachers.

You leave withOne recorded explanation that stops being taught live.
Watch out forRecording in a room with students in it is a different conversation. Record it empty.
Then what: now students can respond to it rather than sit through it. .
Available, underusedAssistant

Scenario: Families ask what the unit is about. The substitute has no idea. Students cannot say what they are working toward. The information exists, in a fifty page curriculum document.

Strategy: Make the one page version, and decide who it is for before you make it.

Before you go in

Put the unit into Notebook and generate the infographic. There are ten styles now and most people have never opened the menu.

With them

Decide the audience. For families, for the substitute, or for students is three different documents, not one document with three uses.

You leave withOne page, with a named audience.
Watch out forCheck the content before it goes out. A handsome infographic with a wrong date still has a wrong date.
Then what: if you cannot get the unit onto one page, the unit structure is the problem. .

Engaging students

Personalization and creation

Students in grades 9 to 12 can use Gemini and Gemini Notebook directly. Below grade 9 they cannot, so the work happens on the teacher's side and students see the product. Both versions are here, and both are real coaching.

New in AugustPersonalizationGrades 9–12

Scenario: Students study by rereading the packet. It does not work, their scores say so, and nobody has ever taught them another way to prepare.

Strategy: Give students the sources and let them build their own study materials, rather than handing them yours.

Before you go in

Seed a Notebook with the core content for the unit. Sources only. No study materials, because the building is the learning.

With them

Students copy it and build their own set: flashcards, quizzes, the summary in whatever form helps them. Same content, their construction.

K–8 version: the teacher builds the set and students choose which format they use. The choosing is still theirs.
You leave withA seeded notebook and a plan for who copies it.
Watch out forDirect student use is grades 9 to 12 only. Below that the teacher drives. This is the line people get wrong.
Then what: what they build tells you what they think matters. Take that back to .
EstablishedPersonalizationAll grades

Scenario: Every student turns in the same slide deck with different pictures. The assignment technically asked for creativity and reliably produced twenty six identical things.

Strategy: Decide what has to be the student's own, then build the assignment around protecting that.

Before you go in

Build the assignment in Adobe Express. Decide what must be theirs: the script, the images, the sequence, the voice.

With them

Students narrate over what they made. This is the one student creation tool open at every grade we serve, which makes it the most useful thing here for elementary coaches.

You leave withOne assignment, ready to hand to a teacher.
Watch out forIf a generated image does the thinking, the assignment is about prompting, not the content. Decide which you want.
Then what: they made something. Respond to the moment, not the whole thing. See the feedback scenario below.
Available, underusedPersonalization

Scenario: Exit tickets get collected every day and read almost never. The teacher knows roughly how it went and cannot say which students need what tomorrow.

Strategy: Find the pattern in what students wrote, and turn it into one grouping decision for tomorrow.

Before you go in

Strip the names. Put the responses into Notebook and ask what the class does not understand, not who.

With them

Decide who gets what tomorrow. This is the scenario that connects a tool to an actual instructional decision, which is the whole point of the morning.

You leave withOne grouping decision you can defend.
Watch out forNames come out before anything goes in. If you cannot strip them quickly, skip this one today.
Then what: the pattern shows where the lesson loses people. .
New in AugustAssistant

Scenario: Feedback on student work is a paragraph at the bottom. Students read the grade, skip the paragraph, and make the same mistake next time.

Strategy: Put the comment at the moment it applies instead of at the end.

Before you go in

Find a student video already sitting in Drive, or record a short one yourself to practice on.

With them

Comment at the timestamp. A student reading feedback at the second it applies is a different experience from a paragraph at the end.

Not AI, which makes it the safest thing on this page to hand to a nervous teacher.
You leave withOne video carrying real timestamped feedback.
Watch out forPractice on your own video first. Do not learn this on a real student's work.
Then what: comments land harder when they point at agreed criteria. .
Available, underusedThought partnerGrades 9–12

Scenario: Students receive summaries of everything. They have never had to go into a source and find something out, and it shows the moment a question is not in the notes.

Strategy: Give them the sources and the first question, then pay attention to what they ask next.

Before you go in

Load the unit sources into a Notebook students can use. Write the one question you want them to start from.

With them

Students interrogate the sources instead of receiving a summary. The teacher watches what gets asked, which is better formative information than most exit tickets.

K–8 version: the class asks, the teacher types, everyone watches the answer come back with its citations. Same habit, teacher at the keyboard.
You leave withA student ready notebook and an opening question.
Watch out forNotebook answers only from the sources you give it, so the sources are the lesson. Choose them like you would choose a text.
Then what: what students ask is better planning data than the pacing guide. .
