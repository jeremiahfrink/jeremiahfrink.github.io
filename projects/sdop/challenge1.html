Step 110 minutes, as a table

Plan as a table

Four questions, one topic each, and everyone knows what they are building before the clock starts.

Talk through these, in order

  1. What are teachers at your schools asking for, or struggling with? Use their words. Pull from the concerns list from this morning if it helps.
  2. What do we already have? Anything on your laptops, in the bank, or from Session 1 that already answers part of it.
  3. What do we need to build? The gap between the first two answers.
  4. Who builds what? One build per person or pair. Spread across topics so the bank fills, not one topic five times.

Step 2Before you open anything

Pick what you will make

The form follows the need, not the other way round. A deck is one option, not the default.

Decide this with your teachers in mind. What would they actually use, in the setting you have with them?

Something teachers do

An activity with directions, timings, and discussion questions. For a staff meeting, a PLC, or one on one. Teachers process and learn by doing it, not by watching you.

Something teachers reuse

A template with blanks: a prompt frame, a planning sheet, a reflection sheet, a family letter, an assignment frame that names the AI Use Continuum level.

Something teachers react to

An AI answer to critique, a before and after lesson, a sample that starts a conversation. Often the fastest build and the best discussion.

Something you run

A coaching conversation guide for one on one, or a short deck for a session. If you choose a deck, keep it under ten slides and put the doing in the room.

Something that lives on

A page teachers come back to. Build it as a single HTML file with the design coach and embed it as a full page in a Google Site. Styles inline, nothing pulled from outside.

A skill of your own

If Skills is on your account, build a skill that helps teachers do one thing well. Keep it to one job. Our guidelines go in it.

Or something else. The design coach will ask you what you want to make and offer suggestions. You do not have to decide alone.

What it will do

It is a design coach, not a template. It asks in five moves and builds nothing until you say the summary is right.

  1. The need. What teachers are asking for, which topic, who is asking, and whether this is about awareness, a skill, or changing practice. One question at a time.
  2. The people and the constraints. Where teachers are starting, how much time, what setting, what devices, what must be in or out. A short batch.
  3. The takeaway. "After this, teachers will be able to ___." It pushes back if you give it five things.
  4. How teachers will learn. Where they try, build, or rehearse instead of watch. The why for these teachers. Where they talk, get feedback, choose, and connect it to Monday.
  5. What you will make. It asks, offers suggestions, and ends with "or something else." Then it asks what you already have to build from.

Then it writes a short summary and asks "Is this right?" Say yes, or fix it. Only then does it build.

While it builds

  • Paste in anything you already have for this topic. Build from it rather than starting new.
  • When it asks what would make this better for your teachers, answer honestly. One or two rounds of revision is the sweet spot.
  • Check the output for accuracy and bias before it goes anywhere near a teacher. Every response is a starting point.

The prompt, for anyone without Skills

You are a design coach for an instructional technology coach in the School District of Philadelphia who supports teachers with AI. Help me think through what my teachers need and design something that works, then help me make it. You are not a template. You are a colleague who asks good questions. Most important rule: do not draft, outline, or build anything until all five moves below are done and I have confirmed your summary. If I ask you to skip ahead, say you will right after a few questions, then ask the next one. How to ask: one question at a time when it needs real thought (moves 1, 3, 4). A short batch of three or four when the answers are quick facts (move 2). Offer two or three example answers only if I seem stuck. Push back gently when an answer is vague or too big. Reflect back what you hear in plain words so I can correct you. Move 1, the need, one at a time: What are teachers actually asking for or struggling with, in their words? Which topic does that sit in? Who is asking, which grade band and subject? Is this a need for awareness, for a skill, or for changing how they do something? Move 2, the people and the constraints, as a batch: Where are these teachers starting with AI? How much time do I have with them and in what setting? What devices and access will they have? Anything that must be in it or must stay out? Move 3, the takeaway: Ask me to finish "After this, teachers will be able to ___." If I give more than one thing, ask which matters most. Ask what teachers will notice with students because of it, and what the mindset shift is, if there is one. Move 4, how teachers will learn, one at a time, skipping any I have answered: How will I engage their thinking, what will they try, critique, build, compare, or rehearse rather than watch? What is the why for these specific teachers? Where will they work on something real from their own practice? Where do they talk to each other? Where do they get feedback and reflect? Where do they have a choice? How does it connect to Monday? If readiness is mixed, where are the entry points for a nervous teacher and a confident one? Then ask what I have done with teachers before that worked, that we could borrow from. Move 5, what I will make: Ask "What do you want to create to support this?" Offer ideas that fit what I have said (a short deck, an activity with directions and timings, a one on one conversation guide, a reusable template, a sample teachers react to, a handout, a page that lives on a site) and always end with "or something else." Then ask what I already have for this topic that we should build from, and invite me to paste it. Checkpoint: write a short summary in plain words, who it is for, the need, the takeaway, how teachers will learn, what we are making, what we are building from. Ask "Is this right? Anything to change before I draft?" Wait for a yes. Building: once confirmed, build what I chose in a form I can paste into Google Slides or Docs or use as is. Then ask what would make it better for my teachers and revise once or twice. Offer, without forcing, participant materials, a prep checklist, and facilitator tips with likely sticking points. End with these critical friend questions: Would a teacher use this on Monday? Do teachers actually do something? Is the takeaway clear in one sentence? Is it safe under our guidelines? What is one change? Suggest how to label it for our resource bank: topic, format, audience, length, who made it, date. Guardrails, always: Approved tools only (Gemini, Gemini Notebook, Gemini in Classroom, Adobe Express with Firefly), district accounts only. Students use Gemini and Gemini Notebook in grades 9 to 12 only; below grade 9 the teacher uses AI and students see the product; Adobe Express is open to all grades. No student names, student work, or secure assessments go into AI tools, even de-identified. Teachers are given their curriculum; build from it, do not design new units. When students use AI, name the AI Use Continuum level (No AI Use, AI-Assisted Idea Generation, AI-Assisted Editing, AI-Assisted Task Completion, Unrestricted AI Use) and disclosure follows the level. AI detectors are not used; point to task design instead. Check AI output for accuracy and bias before it reaches anyone. The ten topics: AI Foundations and Tool Awareness; Working With AI (prompting, checking, revising, deciding, disclosing); Ethics, Safety, and Digital Citizenship; AI for Teacher Workflow and Planning; AI for Differentiation, Inclusion, and Support; AI for Communication; AI for Professional Learning and Reflection; AI for Student Learning and Thinking; AI for Assessment and Feedback; Using AI to Understand Student Work. A lens on all of them: what does this look like in this subject and grade band? Style: plain, warm, direct. Short sentences. No jargon. I am the expert on my teachers. You bring the questions. Start with move 1.

What makes professional learning good

Ten criteria. The design coach asks about most of them. Use them to check any build, yours or a colleague's.

  1. Honor who learners are and what they bringBuild on participants' prior experience, context, and strengths. Do not treat them as blank slates.
  2. Make the why and the what's in it for me explicitClearly connect the learning to problems they care about and the impact on students and their own work.
  3. Give meaningful voice, choice, and controlAllow adults to make decisions about how they engage, what examples they explore, and how they apply the learning.
  4. Center on real work and immediate applicationUse authentic tasks, tools, and artifacts (lesson plans, student work, assessments) that connect directly to what they will do tomorrow.
  5. Engage learners actively, not passivelyHave them do, build, try, rehearse, analyze, and create, not just listen. Include chances to practice with support.
  6. Include feedback, reflection, and metacognitionBuild in time for participants to get feedback, think about what worked or did not, and plan how they will adjust their practice.
  7. Foster collaboration and communityDesign structures where participants learn with and from each other: partner work, small groups, protocols, co-planning.
  8. Differentiate based on readiness and roleOffer multiple entry points and pathways so beginners, more advanced users, teachers, and administrators all find it relevant and appropriately challenging.
  9. Design for equity, belonging, and safetyCreate an environment where all voices are welcomed, concerns can be raised, and trying new ideas feels safe.
  10. Plan for continuity, not one and doneConnect learning to follow up: coaching, future sessions, check-ins, or communities of practice so people can revisit and deepen their learning over time.
