1. Snapshot

Last verified: August 2026

Leadership & Governance
Governor Kathy Hochul (D), in office since August 2021. On the ballot November 3, 2026
Secretary of Education None. No cabinet education secretary and no governor-appointed state chief. The Board of Regents governs education
Commissioner of Education Dr. Betty A. Rosa, permanent since February 8, 2021. Also President of the University of the State of New York. Confirmed in seat February 2026
State chief selection Appointed by the Board of Regents and serving at their pleasure. The Governor has no appointment or removal power
Board of Regents 17 members elected by the Legislature in joint session, five-year terms, unpaid. Chancellor: Lester W. Young, Jr. Sets regulation, standards, graduation requirements
Local control Strong. Roughly 680 to 700 districts, most fiscally independent with their own taxing authority and a May voter budget vote. The Big 5 city districts are fiscally dependent
Scale
School districts Roughly 680 to 700. 675 were subject to the tax cap for FY beginning July 1, 2026 (excludes the Big 5)
Students / schools ~2.42 million students (2024-25 BEDS); roughly 4,400 to 4,800 public schools
Regional layer 37 BOCES and 12 Regional Information Centers. The Big 5 are statutorily excluded from BOCES membership
Largest districts New York City (~850K, about 35% of state enrollment), Buffalo (~30K), Rochester (~22K), Yonkers, Syracuse (~19K). Largest non-Big-5: Brentwood UFSD, under 18,000
Money & Calendar
Funding formula Foundation Aid (2007, the Campaign for Fiscal Equity response). Weighted for poverty, English learners, disability, sparsity. Revised in FY2026 and FY2027 (see Section 4)
Budget cycle Annual. State fiscal year begins April 1, the regional outlier. Districts still run July 1 to June 30
Current state budget FY2027 enacted May 26-27, 2026, 57 days late. $39B total school aid, $27.4B Foundation Aid, 2% guaranteed minimum increase per district
Legislative session Annual. Convened January 7, 2026; last scheduled session day June 4. Executive Budget due mid-to-late January
District budget rhythm Built January-April, adopted by late April, approved by voters at the polls the third Tuesday in May. May 19, 2026. May 18, 2027. Revote in June if defeated
Property tax cap Levy growth capped at the lesser of 2% or CPI (Ch. 97 of the Laws of 2011). 2026-27 factor: 2.0%, fifth straight year at the ceiling. Override takes 60%
Quick Yes/No
State AI guidance for schools? NO. None as of August 2026, only a March 2024 informational Regents presentation. 30-plus states have acted; New York has not (see Section 2)
Cell phone restriction? YES. Statewide bell-to-bell, all grades, including lunch and passing periods. Education Law 2803 (L. 2025 ch. 56), in force since SY2025-26 (see Section 2)
Screen time action? YES, two platform laws. SAFE for Kids Act (final rules July 28, 2026, compliance January 25, 2027) and the Child Data Protection Act (in force June 20, 2025)
Student data privacy law? YES, among the strictest in the country. Education Law 2-d plus 8 NYCRR Part 121: named Data Protection Officer, NIST CSF alignment, 7-day vendor breach clock (see Section 2)
SDPC / standard NDPA state? YES to both. NYSED bought statewide SDPC membership in November 2023. New York uses the National DPA with NY Exhibits G, J, K, L, administered by the 12 RICs
1:1 / state device program? No statewide mandate. The Smart Schools Bond Act ($2B, voter-approved 2014) funded much of the installed base. No recurring refresh fund
ESA / voucher program? No state program, and one of the firmest non-voucher states in the country. Hochul signaled intent May 8, 2026 to opt into the federal tax-credit scholarship. Not finalized
Collective bargaining Mandatory and strong. Taylor Law (Civil Service Law Article 14). Strikes prohibited. The Triborough Amendment continues expired contract terms, including step increases

2. Hot Topics: The Four Questions of 2026

Last verified: August 2026

These are the issues New York district leaders are answering to their boards right now. Note the asymmetry: the state moved hard and early on phones and privacy, has two platform-side screen time laws in the pipeline, and has done essentially nothing on AI.

ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE | No statewide guidance. Governed by default through Ed Law 2-d.

  • Where it stands: NYSED has issued no statewide P-12 AI guidance as of August 2026. The only state action is a March 11, 2024 informational presentation to the Regents, which named Ed Law 2-d, FERPA, and COPPA as the controlling framework and had no regulatory effect. More than 30 states have published guidance; New York has not, and educators have gone public asking Albany to act.

  • What governs instead: AI vendors are handled as ordinary third-party contractors under Ed Law 2-d: signed DPA, published supplemental information, encryption, seven-day breach clock, and a flat bar on commercial use of student data, which is the operative constraint on model training. NYSED's privacy FAQ was last revised May 7, 2021, so districts are applying a 2020 regulation to 2026 products.

  • Pending, not passed: A9190 (Carroll) would prohibit AI in classrooms before high school, with carve-outs for disability interventions and teacher administrative tasks. S7691 (Gonzalez), an AI literacy grant program, sits in Senate Education. Two adjacent laws do bind: the RAISE Act (signed December 19, 2025, effective January 1, 2027) reaches frontier developers only, while the AI Companion Models law (GBL Article 47, in force November 5, 2025) reaches student-facing chatbots.

  • On the ground: The work is regional. BOCES and RICs are vetting AI tools and running teacher AI professional learning, and NYSCATE is the statewide venue. New York City issued preliminary guidance March 24, 2026 then paused finalization after backlash in June, so even the largest district's policy is unsettled.

Source: NYSED data privacy portal | Regents AI presentation, March 2024 | A9190

SCREEN TIME | Two platform laws. SAFE for Kids rules are final; compliance begins January 2027.

  • SAFE for Kids Act: S7694A, signed June 20, 2024. Bars addictive online platforms from serving algorithmic feeds to users under 18, or notifications to minors between midnight and 6 a.m., without verifiable parental consent. Attorney General rulemaking took two years; final rules landed July 28, 2026 and compliance begins January 25, 2027, so the law is final but not yet enforceable.

  • Child Data Protection Act: General Business Law Article 39-FF, effective June 20, 2025 and in force. Under 13 follows COPPA; ages 13 to 17 require the minor's own informed consent unless processing is strictly necessary. The AG's guidance expressly preserves Ed Law 2-d and FERPA, so 2-d remains the controlling regime for school-directed edtech.

  • Litigation status, unresolved: No suit has been filed against the SAFE for Kids Act as of August 15, 2026, and no injunction exists. NetChoice called the rules unconstitutional in a July 29, 2026 statement but has not filed in New York. Treat a challenge before January 2027 as plausible and open, and do not assume New York follows Virginia's enjoined path.

  • Why districts care: These are platform obligations, not school mandates, so they create no district compliance work. They shape the conversation districts are having with parents, and they arrive alongside school-facing money: FY2027 expanded Teen Mental Health First Aid to all 10th graders statewide and grew school-based mental health clinic satellites past 1,300, up from 872 in 2020.

Source: AG final SAFE for Kids rules | Child Data Protection Act guidance | FY2027 youth mental health package

CELL PHONES / CONNECTED DEVICES | Bell to bell, all grades, in force since September 2025. First enforcement reports due September 1, 2026.

  • The law: Education Law 2803, enacted as Chapter 56 of the Laws of 2025 through the FY2026 budget, signed May 9, 2025, effective August 1, 2025. It rode in the budget rather than moving as a standalone bill, which is why guidance came from the Governor's office and NYSED State Aid rather than a Regents rulemaking. New York is the largest state with a bell-to-bell restriction.

  • Scope is broader than phones: The statute covers internet-enabled devices generally, naming smartphones, tablets, and smartwatches, across all instructional and non-instructional time including homeroom, lunch, recess, and passing periods. Having no lunch or passing-period exception is what separates New York from most classroom-only bans. Exemptions cover educational purpose, medical necessity, emergencies, translation, caregiving, and IEP or 504 accommodations.

  • School-issued devices are exempt: Devices supplied by the district, charter school, or BOCES for an educational purpose are expressly excluded. By removing the personal phone as an instructional fallback, the law increases reliance on district-managed fleets. Read it as a tailwind for managed 1:1 programs, not a headwind.

  • The live deadline: Beginning September 1, 2026, every district, charter, and BOCES must publish an annual website report on the prior year's enforcement, disaggregated by demographic group with a disparities analysis. State funding was $13.5 million one-time at $10.90 per secondary student, a fraction of real cost. Statute bars suspending a student where a phone violation is the only reason.

Source: State implementation resources | Albany Law analysis of Ed Law 2803 | NYSED funding guidance

STUDENT DATA PRIVACY | Ed Law 2-d and Part 121. The strictest vendor regime in the region, and the gate on every deal.

  • The statute: Education Law 2-d, enacted in 2014 after the InBloom collapse, which is why the regime is unusually strict. It covers student PII and, unusually, teacher and principal APPR data, and defines third-party contractor broadly enough to capture free tools and click-wrap agreements. Breach penalties are the greater of $5,000 or $10 per affected individual.

  • The regulation is where the burden sits: 8 NYCRR Part 121, effective January 29, 2020. Every agency must post a Parents Bill of Rights, designate a Data Protection Officer registered with NYSED, adopt a policy aligned to the NIST Cybersecurity Framework, train all staff with data access annually, and publish seven-element supplemental information for every contract. No de minimis exception: one free classroom app triggers the same paperwork as an enterprise SIS. The DPO, not the CIO, is the decision gate.

  • The clocks are aggressive: Vendors must notify the district within seven calendar days of discovering a breach, the term most cannot meet under a standard MSA and the most common negotiation friction point. Districts then notify NYSED's Chief Privacy Officer within 10 days and families within 60. Separately, General Municipal Law Article 19-C (July 26, 2025) requires cyber incident reports to state Homeland Security within 72 hours.

  • The efficient path in: New York layers Exhibits G (the 2-d rider), J (district policy and Parents Bill of Rights), K (vendor security plan), and L (supplemental information) onto the National DPA, managed and funded by the 12 RICs through the RIC One Risk Operations Center. Sign once with a lead district or RIC, post it to the SDPC registry, and other agencies join rather than renegotiate. The Big 5 sit outside RIC membership and need separate agreements.

Source: Education Law 2-d | Part 121 overview | RIC One Risk Operations Center

3. Governance & Decision-Making

Last verified: August 2026

Authority flows differently here than anywhere else in the region. The Legislature elects the Board of Regents in joint session, the Regents appoint the Commissioner, and the Commissioner leads NYSED. The Governor appoints no one in that chain. The Regents and Commissioner control the rules. The Governor and Legislature control the money, and the Executive Budget is constitutionally powerful. Foundation Aid changes and the phone restriction both moved through the budget, not the Regents. Both doors matter and they rarely move together.

  • Leadership is stable, which is unusual this year: Commissioner Betty Rosa (permanent since February 2021) and Chancellor Lester W. Young, Jr. (since January 2021) were both confirmed in seat by a February 27, 2026 NYSED statement. Ceylane Meyers-Ruff was appointed Senior Deputy Commissioner for Education Policy in 2026. Regent Roger Tilles departed in February 2026 after 21 years.

  • November 2026 election, provisional note: Hochul is on the ballot November 3, 2026 against Bruce Blakeman (R), and all 213 legislative seats are up. Treat the fiscal and legislative outlook here as provisional until the new session settles in January 2027. The structural difference from Ohio and Massachusetts matters: a change of administration would not change who runs NYSED. It would change budget posture and the durability of Hochul-branded initiatives.

  • Two different budget audiences: Most districts are fiscally independent and take the budget to a public referendum in May, so the budget audience is the electorate. The Big 5 (New York City, Buffalo, Rochester, Syracuse, Yonkers) are fiscally dependent, have no budget vote, and are appropriated by mayor and city council. NYC mayoral control was extended two years to June 30, 2028 in the FY2027 budget.

  • BOCES is the structure to understand: 37 Boards of Cooperative Educational Services (Education Law 1950-1951) serve every district except the Big 5. Each is led by a District Superintendent who is simultaneously the BOCES chief executive and, by statute, the Commissioner's representative in the region. BOCES Aid reimburses a wealth-adjusted share of purchases made through an approved cooperative service agreement (CoSer) the following year, roughly 21% on services and 40% on administrative and capital, so a CFO asks whether something is BOCES aidable before comparing a direct quote. Nested inside the BOCES are 12 Regional Information Centers running student information systems for more than 95% of districts, state reporting, hosted infrastructure, and the statewide privacy machinery. Statewide platform in New York means the twelve RICs, not NYSED.

  • Legislature: Senate Education is chaired by Sen. Shelley Mayer (D, Westchester), active on Foundation Aid legislation; Assembly Education by Assemblymember Michael Benedetto (D, Bronx). Money runs through Senate Finance (Krueger) and Assembly Ways and Means (Pretlow). The Joint Legislative Budget Hearing on Elementary and Secondary Education, January 29 in 2026, is where everyone testifies. No established K-12 technology champion has emerged, which is an open lane.

Sources: Board of Regents | BOCES primer | RIC One | Conference of Big 5

4. Funding & Budget Rhythm

Last verified: August 2026. FY2027 state budget enacted May 26-27, 2026

  • How the formula works: Foundation Aid, enacted in 2007 to settle the Campaign for Fiscal Equity litigation, is the main unrestricted operating aid. Take a per-pupil foundation amount, adjust by a Regional Cost Index and a Pupil Need Index (poverty, English learners, disability, sparsity), subtract the expected local contribution based on wealth, and the state pays the difference. Save harmless, which guaranteed no district received less than the prior year regardless of enrollment loss, has been the contested element for a decade.

  • The formula is being rewritten, through the budget: The Rockefeller Institute delivered a 314-page study on December 2, 2024. FY2026 adopted its core technical fixes: poverty measured by Small Area Income and Poverty Estimates instead of 2000 Census data, and the English learner weight raised from 0.50 to 0.53. FY2027 added first-ever weights for students experiencing homelessness and foster care and kept the 2% guaranteed minimum increase that functionally replaced pure save harmless.

  • Current budget, and a very late one: FY2027 was enacted May 26-27, 2026, 57 days past the April 1 deadline, at roughly $268 billion. School aid totals $39 billion, up $1.7 billion, with Foundation Aid at $27.4 billion. Also in it: universal pre-K consolidated at roughly $1.6 billion with full-day access required for all eligible four-year-olds by 2028-29, and a five-year delay in the electric school bus mandate to 2032, which frees near-term capital capacity. The lateness is what districts will raise: they adopted their own budgets in April and faced voters on May 19 before the state told them their aid.

  • The tax cap governs everything local: Chapter 97 of the Laws of 2011, permanent since 2019, caps growth in the total levy, not the rate, at the lesser of 2% or CPI, with exclusions for voter-approved capital. The 2026-27 factor is 2.0%, the fifth straight year at the ceiling, and exceeding it requires 60% voter approval. In May 2026, 42 districts tried and 24 budgets were defeated, 17 for missing 60%. A budget defeated twice forces a contingency budget with zero levy growth that excludes equipment purchases and leases entirely, which is the sales-cycle risk here.

  • Where technology money lives: BOCES Aid is the largest structural lever outside the Big 5. The Smart Schools Bond Act ($2 billion, voter-approved 2014) funded connectivity, classroom technology, and security, with 1,911 plans approved as of August 3, 2026, but it is a one-time capital pot, not a refresh fund. The recurring aid ceilings have not moved in a generation: Textbook Aid $58.25 (Ed Law 701), Computer Software Aid $14.98 (751), Instructional Computer Hardware and Technology Equipment Aid $24.20 (753). Building Aid covers capital projects, which is why infrastructure gets bundled into bond referenda. No state device refresh program exists, and the Regents' own 2026-27 request contained no technology line item at all.

Sources: FY2027 enacted budget summary | Rockefeller Foundation Aid study | OSC tax cap announcement | State aid for technology purchases

5. Procurement & Vendor Path

Last verified: August 2026

  • Framework: General Municipal Law 103 governs district and BOCES purchasing: competitive bidding above $20,000 for purchase contracts and above $35,000 for public works, applied to aggregate annual purchases of the same commodity rather than per transaction. Award goes to the lowest responsible bidder, or on a best value basis where adopted. Professional services sit outside 103 and are procured by RFP under board policy.

  • State vehicles: GML 104 lets districts buy directly off Office of General Services centralized contracts without their own bid, and NYSED endorses this path. The IT Umbrella family is the main technology vehicle, awarded on lowest price by expedited request for quote. Two cautions: the distributor-based IT Umbrella (Award 22876) expressly prohibits all cloud products, so SaaS needs a different vehicle, and that award runs only to December 6, 2026.

  • Cooperatives, and a 2026 court decision: Authorized paths are OGS contracts, county contracts, BOCES cooperative bidding and CoSers, inter-district cooperative bidding under GML 119-o, and piggybacking under GML 103(16) on contracts competitively awarded by another government. 103(16) requires an enabling local law, and in Lynch, Inc. v. Board of Education of the Maine-Endwell CSD (Third Department, May 21, 2026) the court held it cannot be used for public works. Goods piggybacking survives, and 103(16) is scheduled to sunset June 30, 2027.

  • National cooperatives are not the clean path they are elsewhere: NYSED's cooperative purchasing guidance does not list Sourcewell, E&I, NCPA, or Omnia at all, and states districts may not piggyback except as specifically authorized. New York is a harder state for national co-ops because 103(16) requires both a local enabling law and a governmental lead agency that bid competitively. In practice the BOCES CoSer is the regional path, and it carries the aid advantage besides.

  • The compliance layer is the real gate: For anything touching student data, Ed Law 2-d and Part 121 come before procurement, not after. The sequence that works: sign the New York DPA with Exhibit G through a RIC or lead district, post it on the SDPC registry so others can join, prepare the supplemental information and Parents Bill of Rights addendum, document NIST CSF alignment and encryption, then start the procurement conversation. Doing procurement first and privacy second is the most common way a deal stalls here.

Sources: GML 103 | NYSED cooperative purchasing | OGS Award 22876 | Lynch v. Maine-Endwell CSD

6. Technology & Digital Learning Posture

Last verified: August 2026

  • Devices: No statewide 1:1 mandate, no device standard, no device requirement. The Smart Schools Bond Act funded the bulk of the current installed base. Refresh is unresolved, with no state program and recurring aid ceilings of $24.20 per pupil for hardware.

  • Assessment infrastructure: The computer-based testing transition for grades 3-8 is complete and mandatory as of spring 2026 for ELA, math, and elementary and intermediate science. Every district had to reach a device-per-tested-student ratio and a network capable of concurrent testing by then. That cuts both ways: the infrastructure exists, and the money for it is already spent. Regents exam CBT status is separate and less settled.

  • Statewide platforms and cybersecurity: There is no statewide learning management system and no statewide content platform; the RICs are the de facto platform layer. On security, Part 121 requires a NIST CSF-aligned data security policy at every agency, publicly posted, with profiles and a gap action plan. General Municipal Law Article 19-C (effective July 26, 2025) adds incident reporting to state Homeland Security within 72 hours and ransom payment reporting within 24, and those reports are FOIL-exempt, so public incident data will be thinner here than elsewhere. NYSED also will not approve Smart Schools plans containing facial recognition.

  • AI capacity is regional, not state-led: No statewide NYSED AI guidance and no identified state-funded K-12 AI professional development. What exists is BOCES-run teacher AI programming, RIC-level vendor vetting, and NYSCATE as the statewide venue. Empire AI, the state's flagship AI investment, is higher education focused. There is an open door and no incumbent program to displace.

  • Framing: New York frames educational technology through a compliance and student wellbeing lens, not an innovation lens: a prescriptive privacy regime, mandatory cyber incident reporting, the nation's largest bell-to-bell phone restriction marketed on behavior outcomes, and a refusal to approve facial recognition. Conspicuously absent are a state instructional technology vision, a state LMS, an AI framework, a device standard, or an edtech innovation grant. The first district question is not whether something transforms learning. It is whether it is 2-d compliant and BOCES aidable.

Sources: NYSED computer-based testing | RIC One privacy and security service | GML Article 19-C reporting

7. Instructional Priorities & Mandates

Last verified: August 2026

  • Literacy, and now math: Back to Basics is a Governor's initiative rather than a Regents one, funded at $10 million to train 20,000 educators in the science of reading. Every district had to certify to NYSED by September 2025 that curriculum, instruction, and professional development align with evidence-based reading practice. FY2027 applied the same playbook to math: NYSED must establish math instructional best practices by January 2027 and districts must review their math curricula by September 2027. That is a live curriculum-adoption driver.

  • Computer science: The Computer Science and Digital Fluency Learning Standards were adopted in December 2020 and full implementation began September 1, 2024, so they are mandatory now. CS is not a graduation requirement; courses count as electives or toward a third math or science unit. The bottleneck is certification: teachers of dedicated CS courses need CS certification or a Statement of Continued Eligibility, so districts are meeting the standards largely through integration.

  • Accountability: The ESSA indicator set changed in 2025-26: attendance replaced chronic absenteeism, student growth returned at the elementary and middle level, and college, career, and civic readiness returned at high school. Final determinations released February 12, 2026 identified 17 schools newly for receivership support with 3 exiting. New York retains a school receivership model, unusual among states.

  • Graduation redesign is the biggest story in the state: Under NY Inspires, New York is adopting a Portrait of a Graduate (formally adopted July 2025, not November 2024 as often reported), shifting credits from seat time toward proficiency, sunsetting the Regents exam graduation requirement, and moving to a single unified diploma. The class of 2027 is the last cohort required to pass Regents exams to graduate, the one-diploma system begins fall 2027, cohorts entering in 2027 and 2028 face new credit requirements including one required CTE credit, and full implementation with a statewide transcript arrives in 2029. Regents exams continue for federal accountability. As of June 2026 NYSED had not released detailed specifications, and principals describe being in limbo. Label as pending.

  • CTE and workforce: One CTE credit becomes a graduation requirement for the 2027 and 2028 cohorts, a dated and material mandate. CTE delivery outside the Big 5 is overwhelmingly a BOCES function through regional CTE centers, which is why the Regents asked for $10.1 million specifically for CTE in non-BOCES districts. Multiple P-TECH grant cohorts reach the end of their terms in 2026 and renewal status is unresolved.

Sources: NY Inspires graduation measures FAQ | Chalkbeat on graduation uncertainty | CS and Digital Fluency standards | Back to Basics

8. Landscape & Pressures

Last verified: August 2026

  • Enrollment is falling, and the epicenter is New York City: Cornell's Program on Applied Demographics found K-12 enrollment fell from roughly 3.1 million to 2.8 million between 2013-14 and 2023-24, with nearly 90% of districts losing students and an aging population as the driver. NYC traditional public enrollment fell 19.1% over that period and accounted for most of the statewide loss in 2023-24, while Long Island was flat. The familiar upstate-decline, downstate-growth framing is out of date. The 2% Foundation Aid minimum increase exists precisely because formula results would otherwise cut aid to shrinking districts. New York districts plan to spend over $37,000 per student, consistently the highest in the nation.

  • Workforce: NYSUT published an educator workforce report in January 2026 focused on teacher preparation. Special education is a documented shortage area with dedicated NYSED personnel shortage projects. A new pressure point arrived with the September 2024 computer science certification requirement, which created a shortage category exactly as the CS standards became mandatory.

  • Facilities: Building Aid reimburses a wealth-adjusted share of approved capital costs through an assumed amortization, and the NYSED Office of Facilities Planning reviews plans and issues permits for all district and BOCES capital work. The fact that matters most: voter-approved capital expenditures are excluded from the tax cap, which is why districts route cabling, electrical, and network work through bond referenda rather than the operating budget.

  • Choice pressure is limited but shifting: New York has no ESA and no voucher program. Charter growth is capped out where it matters: of the statewide cap of 460, 84 charters remain available outside New York City and zero remain in the city, and no cap increase passed in FY2027. The live variable is federal: Hochul signaled intent on May 8, 2026 to opt New York into the new federal tax-credit scholarship program, a significant break from precedent. Status is announced intent, not finalized.

  • Federal climate: New York districts carry substantial federal exposure, with New York City alone receiving roughly $2 billion. Districts spent 2025 and 2026 managing funding threats, including a Senate amendment that would have cut about $170 million from NYC schools before it was defeated. No named state contingency or backfill fund has been identified, and a $268 billion budget enacted 57 days late leaves limited appetite for new reserves.

Sources: Cornell enrollment brief | Empire Center enrollment analysis | NYSED charter fact sheet | Chalkbeat on the federal tax-credit scholarship

10. Key Notes for Engagement

Last verified: August 2026. WORKING DRAFT for Jeremiah's edit. Research-generated engagement judgment, not settled advice

How to think about New York right now:

  • Learn BOCES before anything else: Outside New York City this is a long-tail market: the second largest district is under 31,000 students. District-by-district does not scale, and the state built the answer into its structure. A District Superintendent is both a regional CEO and the Commissioner's statutory representative, which makes that one relationship worth twenty district relationships. BOCES Aid also means a district's net cost through a CoSer can beat a direct quote even at a higher gross price.

  • Ed Law 2-d is not a checkbox, it is the door: The strictest vendor privacy regime in this batch. Every agency has a named Data Protection Officer who is the real decision gate, every contract requires published supplemental information with no de minimis exception, NIST CSF alignment is required by regulation, and the vendor breach clock is seven days. Fluency here distinguishes a serious partner from a vendor who spends eight months discovering the rules.

  • The phone ban is a tailwind, and there is a deadline this month: School-issued devices are expressly exempt, which makes district-managed fleets the sanctioned classroom technology precisely as ESSER-bought devices age out with no state refresh fund. Meanwhile the first annual enforcement reports are due on district websites September 1, 2026. Ask how year one actually went rather than leading with product.

  • The AI vacuum is the clearest opening in the state: No statewide guidance, no model policy, no vetted vendor list, a privacy FAQ last updated in 2021, and a pending Assembly bill that would ban AI in classrooms before high school. Districts are absorbing that risk locally and BOCES are filling the gap regionally. The educator-first move is helping districts and BOCES think through policy, professional learning, and 2-d-compliant implementation while the state is silent.

  • Budget empathy means understanding the May vote: The state budget was 57 days late, so districts faced voters without knowing their aid, and the levy cap has been pinned at 2% for five straight years.   • Graduation redesign is the anxiety, and the opening: Every district must build proficiency-based credit tracking, portfolio and capstone assessment, work-based learning documentation, and a statewide transcript by 2029, and as of June 2026 the specifications were not published. This is a listening conversation first, and it matters more to a New York superintendent right now than any technology question.

  • Two New Yorks, two motions: The Big 5 sit outside BOCES and RIC membership entirely: no budget vote, no BOCES aid, no shared DPA, appropriated by city hall. Everything above about the regional motion is inapplicable there. Plan separate motions, and do not mistake a win in one for a pattern in the other.
