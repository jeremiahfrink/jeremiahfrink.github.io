1. Snapshot

Last verified: August 2026

2. Hot Topics: The Four Questions of 2026

Last verified: August 2026

Massachusetts is close to the mirror image of New York on these four. It has the most developed AI guidance and educator training in the batch and the least enacted law on phones and social media. Its student privacy protection is contractual rather than statutory, and the most consequential recent action came from a court, not the legislature.

ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE | Real guidance, real educator training, no mandate. A policy phase is underway this school year.

  • What exists: DESE published Massachusetts Guidance for Artificial Intelligence in K-12 Education for 2025-26. It is explicitly advisory: the Department states it is not recommending or requiring the use of AI, and that those decisions remain with local leaders. It sets five principles for district policy: data privacy and security, transparency and accountability, bias awareness and mitigation, human oversight, and academic integrity.

  • It contains real procurement guidance: The guidance advises districts to conduct harmful bias audits during procurement and to ensure vendor contracts address AI capabilities, data practices, and compliance with privacy and accessibility law. That is the closest thing Massachusetts has to AI procurement rules, and it is worth reading before any AI conversation. On academic integrity it advises against relying on AI detection tools, recommending disclosure practices and assessment redesign instead.

  • The timing is the opportunity: DESE's rollout ran from a task force in August 2024 through implementation support in 2025-26 to a policy considerations phase in 2026-27. That policy phase is happening right now, and districts are writing AI policy this school year with DESE encouragement. Educator supports already exist: an AI Literacy for Educators course with certification, a six-session recorded webinar series, and facilitation guides in two formats.

  • Around the edges: The state's marquee AI money goes to industry and computing infrastructure through the Massachusetts AI Hub at MassTech, not K-12. One exception is worth naming: a $500,000 public-private paid AI internship placed 400 high school students from 76 schools in 66 districts in summer 2026. MassCUE runs PEAK, now in its second cohort. There is no AI statute and no state AI procurement vehicle.

Source: DESE AI guidance | DESE AI landing page | AI internship program

SCREEN TIME | Nothing enacted. Everything is in conference. The courts are ahead of the legislature.

  • Three vehicles, all unfinished: The combined phone and social media bill (S.2581 and H.5366) is in conference. The Senate's competing addictive-design bill (S.3164) would set defaults for users under 18: no algorithmic feeds, autoplay, or infinite scroll, usage reminders after one cumulative hour, and notifications off overnight. The Massachusetts Data Privacy Act (S.2608), which passed the Senate 40-0 and would ban selling minors' data outright, is also in conference, held up over whether enforcement is Attorney General exclusive or includes a private right of action.

  • The House and Senate disagree on approach: The House wants an age ban: no accounts under 14, parental consent for 14 and 15, and age verification using best available technology. The Senate prefers design regulation. That disagreement is what is holding up the phone bill too, since the House attached social media provisions to it.

  • Where the state actually moved: the courts: In Commonwealth v. Meta Platforms, brought by Attorney General Andrea Campbell, the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court held on April 10, 2026 that Section 230 does not shield Meta from claims about addictive design features. It was the first state supreme court to so rule, and the case proceeds to the merits. Massachusetts is behind New York legislatively and ahead of it judicially.

  • What is true right now: There is no Massachusetts social media statute in force. Healey proposed her own restrictions in the January 2026 State of the Commonwealth. Under the 194th General Court's new joint rules none of these bills died at the July 31, 2026 deadline; they remain eligible for fall pop-up formal sessions. Tell districts these are live but unfinished.

Source: SJC Section 230 ruling coverage | Senate S.3164 coverage | End of formal sessions status

CELL PHONES / CONNECTED DEVICES | No statewide restriction is in effect. The bill is stuck in conference. Do not tell a district this is law.

  • Where it actually stands: The vehicle is An Act to promote student learning and mental health, S.2581 in the Senate and H.5366 in the House. The Senate passed it 38-2 on July 31, 2025. The House amended and passed it 129-25 on April 8, 2026, adding social media provisions. The Senate rejected those amendments on May 7, 2026 and a conference committee formed. It did not pass by the July 31, 2026 deadline and was still unresolved on August 12, 2026.

  • The phone provisions themselves are not the problem: Reporting is consistent that the phone restrictions are uncontroversial and the social media age-ban provisions are what is stuck. The bill is not dead: under the new joint rules, bills in conference survive the deadline and can pass in pop-up formal sessions in the fall or at year end.

  • What it would require if enacted: All districts would adopt policies prohibiting student use or possession of personal devices during the school day, with local flexibility on method (pouches, lockers, or other storage). Exemptions cover health care provider notes, IEP accommodations, medical devices such as glucose monitors, and travel to off-campus learning. Districts must keep at least one way for parents and students to reach each other, notify families annually by September 1, and file policies with DESE. DESE would have 180 days to develop a model policy, and that clock does not start until enactment.

  • Two things a technology partner should note: First, all of this concerns student-owned devices. School-issued Chromebooks, laptops, and tablets are outside the scope, so a restriction would not touch 1:1 programs and would if anything increase reliance on them. Second, the House version directs DESE to oversee a pilot for 10 districts testing device-disabling technology, the only procurement-adjacent provision in either bill. No state funding for storage was identified.

Source: S.2581 bill history | Senate passage summary | House passage summary

STUDENT DATA PRIVACY | Contractually demanding, statutorily light. Sign once with TEC and you are cleared statewide.

  • No student-specific vendor statute: Massachusetts has never enacted a SOPIPA-style student online privacy act. What governs instead is a stack: MGL c.93H breach notification, 201 CMR 17.00, the student records statutes at MGL c.71 s.34D, s.34E, and s.34H, and 603 CMR 23.00. The architecture is fundamentally different from New York's Education Law 2-d.

  • 201 CMR 17.00 is the unusual piece: It applies to any entity that owns or licenses personal information about a Massachusetts resident, wherever that entity sits, and requires a Written Information Security Program: designated security coordinator, risk assessment, employee training, access controls, incident response, and annual review. It also requires encryption in transit over public networks and on portable devices, and requires third-party service provider contracts to obligate equivalent safeguards. Because districts hold employee Social Security numbers, they are covered entities, which makes this the de facto security mandate.

  • Student records rights transfer earlier than FERPA: 603 CMR 23.00, amended January 27, 2026, transfers records rights to the student at age 14 or entry to ninth grade, whichever comes first, with both student and parent able to exercise rights thereafter. Records must be available within 10 days, third-party access requires specific informed written consent, and temporary records must be destroyed within seven years of departure. That consent requirement is what drives district DPA demands on vendors.

  • The consortium is the practical gate, and it is efficient: The Massachusetts Student Privacy Alliance is administered by The Education Cooperative, itself an educational collaborative, using a multi-state agreement built on the SDPC National DPA. TEC negotiates the master DPA once; each additional district then signs a one-page Exhibit E electronically. TEC supports more than 1,900 districts across ten states with over 2,000 signed agreements. Vendors who refuse are publicly posted as declined, which is a real commercial risk. Sign once and you are pre-cleared statewide.

Source: 201 CMR 17.00 | 603 CMR 23.00 student records | TEC data privacy

3. Governance & Decision-Making

Last verified: August 2026

Authority flows Governor to Secretary of Education (Executive Office of Education, overseeing early education, K-12, and higher education) to the Board of Elementary and Secondary Education to the Commissioner to 395 districts. The nuance that matters: the Governor appoints the Secretary and the Board, but the Board, not the Governor, hires and fires the K-12 Commissioner. That buffers DESE from direct gubernatorial control.

  • Both leaders are new, and both are operators: Commissioner Pedro Martinez started July 1, 2025 after running Chicago Public Schools. Secretary Stephen Zrike took office in March 2026 after six years as Salem superintendent and, before that, as state-appointed receiver of Holyoke. This is an unusually practitioner-heavy pair: neither came from a statehouse policy shop, and both have personally run districts. State-level relationships are still forming.

  • November 2026 election, provisional note: Healey is on the ballot November 3, 2026 with the primary September 1. Handicappers rate the race safe Democratic, so continuity is the base case and Zrike and Martinez are likely to remain. Treat leadership as provisional until January 2027 anyway, but Massachusetts carries the least leadership risk in this batch.

  • Three district types, three money paths: Municipal districts: the school committee adopts a budget and town meeting or city council appropriates it. Under MGL c.71 s.34 the appropriating body votes the total and cannot allocate among accounts, which is the basis for school committee control once the bottom line is set. Academic regional districts: the regional committee adopts by two-thirds, the treasurer certifies each member town's assessment, and two-thirds of member appropriating authorities must approve. Regional vocational technical districts run the same mechanics plus Chapter 74 approval, and are the most equipment-intensive segment in the state.

  • What happens when a town says no: A rejecting town forces the regional committee to reconsider and resubmit, though two-thirds approval can still certify a budget over one town's objection. If no locally approved budget exists by July 1, the Commissioner sets an interim one-twelfth monthly budget. A district in one-twelfth mode cannot make new discretionary commitments, and this happens somewhere in Massachusetts most years.

  • Net school spending is the legal floor: Chapter 70 sets two binding minimums: the required local contribution based on municipal wealth, and net school spending, which is that contribution plus Chapter 70 aid. A district must actually spend at least the net school spending figure or face DESE compliance action. Towns frequently appropriate exactly at the floor and no further, and districts at the floor have essentially zero discretionary capacity, which is where technology budgets die first.

  • Proposition 2.5 sets the ceiling: MGL c.59 s.21C limits levy growth to 2.5% per year plus new growth, under a ceiling of 2.5% of full and fair cash value. A general override permanently raises the limit and needs majority voter approval at the ballot. A debt exclusion temporarily adds debt service for a specific borrowing. A capital outlay expenditure exclusion covers one-year, cash-funded capital, which is the vehicle that matters for a device refresh. Historically capital exclusions pass at 73% and debt exclusions at 84%, while general overrides pass around half the time.

  • Collaboratives, and what they are not: 24 educational collaboratives under MGL c.40 s.4E and 603 CMR 50.00 serve more than 300 member districts, governed by boards that include a Commissioner-appointed voting member after the 2012 accountability reform. They deliver special education programs, 300-plus training sessions and 50 professional learning communities annually, emergency licensure pathways, and student data privacy negotiation. They are not the hardware and software channel: TEC's awarded bid categories are food service, supplies, custodial, fuel, and athletics. Technology purchasing runs through statewide contracts and MHEC.

  • Legislature: The Joint Committee on Education is co-chaired by Rep. Kenneth Gordon (D-Bedford) and Sen. Jason Lewis (D-Winchester). Money runs through House Ways and Means (Rep. Aaron Michlewitz) and Senate Ways and Means (Sen. Michael Rodrigues). For anything touching student data, AI, or cybersecurity, the relevant committee is Advanced Information Technology, the Internet and Cybersecurity, co-chaired by Sen. Michael Moore and Rep. Tricia Farley-Bouvier. Formal sessions for the 194th General Court ended July 31, 2026.

Sources: BESE membership | Regional budget approval guide | Proposition 2.5 primer | MOEC on collaboratives

4. Funding & Budget Rhythm

Last verified: August 2026. FY2027 budget signed July 9, 2026

  • How the formula works: Chapter 70 has three parts. The foundation budget is a per-pupil adequacy calculation built from 11 functional categories, including instructional materials, equipment and technology, adjusted by grade level, a regional wage factor, and student-need increments. The required local contribution is what the municipality must pay, derived from equalized property valuation and aggregate personal income. Chapter 70 aid fills the gap, subject to minimum aid per pupil.

  • FY2027 numbers: Total Chapter 70 aid is $7.658 billion, up 4.03%. Minimum aid landed at $160 per pupil, up from the $75 in the preliminary January figures, which matters because districts built spring budgets on the lower number. Foundation enrollment is 890,681, down 14,626, and target local share is 59%. The special education circuit breaker is $654.6 million, charter reimbursement $200.4 million, rural school aid $16 million, and regional transportation $57.1 million. The total state budget is $63.4 billion, signed with no vetoes.

  • The cliff is the real story: FY2027 is the sixth and final year of the Student Opportunity Act phase-in, with roughly $550 million dedicated to completing it. The Act raised the foundation budget by about $1.5 billion across employee health benefits, special education assumptions, English learner increments, and poverty-concentration increments. After FY2027 that growth stops, and districts have spent six years layering recurring costs onto it. FY2028 is the cliff. The budget revived the Foundation Budget Review Commission to re-examine the formula, reporting by October 2028. That is the most important forward-looking funding item in the state.

  • A second, quieter FY2027 shock: Low-income enrollment fell from 419,861 to 400,805, a 4.5% drop, faster than the overall enrollment decline. Because the low-income increment is the largest Student Opportunity Act lever, that directly reduces Chapter 70 aid for high-poverty districts. It is an under-discussed pressure on Gateway Cities this year.

  • Fair Share money, and the override surge: The 4% surtax on income over $1 million generated $4.053 billion in FY2027 spending, of which education takes $2.303 billion, funding universal school meals, free community college, early education, and circuit breaker supplements. Healey filed a $100 million supplemental school aid request in July 2026 at roughly $112 per pupil; it needs legislative approval and formal sessions have ended, so its status is unresolved. Meanwhile municipalities put 74 override questions to voters in a single year seeking $158 million, the second-largest total since the early 1990s, and roughly half passed. Berkley rejected one and cut teachers; Fairhaven rejected one and reduced services.

  • Calendar, and the timing mismatch that defines the year: The Governor files House 1 or House 2 in late January with preliminary Chapter 70 numbers, House Ways and Means reports in April, Senate in May, conference in June, and the Governor signs in July. FY2027 was signed July 9, which by Massachusetts standards is fast. But districts adopt budgets in the spring on preliminary numbers and reconcile in July. School committee budget development runs January to March, town meetings April to June with most in May, and override questions attach to the annual town election or a special election. That mismatch is a recurring source of spring conservatism.

  • Where technology money lives: Name these the way a business manager would. Operating budget function 2450, Instructional Technology, and function 4400, Maintenance of Equipment, both constrained by the net school spending floor. The municipal capital improvement plan, where devices compete with fire trucks and roofs. The Proposition 2.5 capital outlay expenditure exclusion, a one-year instrument that historically passes 73% of the time and is the realistic vehicle for a district-wide refresh. Debt exclusion for bonded technology inside a building project. Federal E-rate, which DESE explicitly points districts toward. Title IV-A, the one ESSA title with an explicit effective-use-of-technology bucket. And Perkins V plus CTE planning grants for vocational equipment.

Sources: FY2027 Chapter 70 | MMA on the FY2027 budget | MassBudget FY2027 analysis | Override reporting

5. Procurement & Vendor Path

Last verified: August 2026

  • Framework, with a real school district advantage: MGL c.30B, the Uniform Procurement Act, governs supplies and services. Under $10,000 requires only sound business practices. From $10,000 the general local threshold for three written quotes runs to $50,000, but for a municipal or regional school district it runs to $100,000, above which a sealed bid or competitive sealed proposal is required. That doubled headroom is the single most useful procurement fact for a K-12 vendor in Massachusetts. MGL c.149 governs building construction over $150,000, and MGL c.30 s.39M applies if network cabling is bid inside a construction contract rather than as a c.30B supply.

  • State vehicles are the fastest legal path: The Operational Services Division manages more than 100 statewide contracts generating $2.5 billion a year, and municipalities and school districts may buy from them without running their own c.30B procurement. The three that matter for K-12 technology are ITC73 (IT hardware and services), ITS86 (software and service), and ITS60 (cloud solutions), with ITS78 for data and cybersecurity and ITS82 for IT accessibility services, which is increasingly relevant given digital accessibility deadlines. Vendors must register in COMMBUYS, which is free, to hold or bid on a statewide contract.

  • MHEC is the second vehicle worth having: The Massachusetts Higher Education Consortium, founded 1977, explicitly serves K-12 districts alongside higher education and municipalities. Membership is required but free, and MHEC creates, manages, and owns its own publicly bid contracts rather than reselling others'. One caution: MHEC's c.30B compliance mechanism is not spelled out publicly, and Massachusetts districts and their counsel are sensitive on this point, so get the written compliance memo before representing that an MHEC contract satisfies c.30B.

  • Massachusetts is not a piggyback state: Chapter 30B contains no blanket piggyback exemption of the kind most states have. A district relying on a national cooperative such as Sourcewell, Omnia, NCPA, or E&I needs either the statewide contract path, an interlocal agreement under MGL c.40 s.4A, or its own c.30B procurement. Do not assume a national co-op contract closes a Massachusetts deal. The Office of the Inspector General is the enforcement and technical assistance body for c.30B and handled more than 1,100 procurement inquiries in 2025.

  • What a vendor actually signs: In order: the TEC master data privacy agreement plus a per-district Exhibit E; the district's own terms incorporating 201 CMR 17.00 flow-down and c.93H breach notification; if on a statewide contract, the Commonwealth Terms and Conditions and any security schedule; a certificate of non-collusion and tax compliance certification, required on every c.30B award; and FERPA school-official designation language where claimed. District cost for the privacy alliance is $1.00 per student capped at $4,999 a year, which is why nearly everyone participates.

Sources: MGL c.30B s.5 thresholds | Statewide contract index | MHEC | Office of the Inspector General

6. Technology & Digital Learning Posture

Last verified: August 2026

  • Devices: No statewide 1:1 mandate. DESE's stated goal is aspirational rather than regulatory: expand equitable access to technology including sufficient bandwidth, 1:1 devices, and high-quality digital materials. The 2020-2021 device purchases were federally funded and have not been renewed as a recurring program. Current state-adjacent device distribution runs through the Massachusetts Broadband Institute's Connected and Online program, which is a household digital-equity effort rather than a school program. With ESSER liquidation closed, the Student Opportunity Act ramp ending, and 74 override questions in one year, the Massachusetts refresh problem is currently unfunded at both state and local level. The realistic instruments are the municipal capital plan and the Proposition 2.5 capital outlay exclusion.

  • Assessment infrastructure: MCAS is computer-based across grades 3-8 and 10 in ELA and math, science in grades 5, 8, and one high school grade, and civics in grade 8. The current vendor is Cognia, delivering through the MCAS Portal. Device and bandwidth specifications come to districts annually in the Test Administration Manual rather than from a published state standard, so get the current manual rather than working from assumptions.

  • Statewide platforms: Edwin Analytics is DESE's active reporting and longitudinal data tool, spanning early education through workforce outcomes with role-based reports. The state reporting stack is SIMS for students, EPIMS for personnel, SCS for course schedules, the End of Year Financial Report, and GEM$ for all federal and state grant applications. There is no statewide learning management system and no statewide content platform; that choice is entirely district-level, which is a meaningful contrast with states that hold statewide LMS contracts.

  • Cybersecurity sits outside DESE: Massachusetts has no K-12 cybersecurity statute and no DESE cybersecurity mandate. The posture is assembled from three other agencies. The MassCyberCenter at MassTech runs the Cyber Resilient Massachusetts Grant Program, where K-12 districts are explicitly eligible and regional districts may apply separately from local governments, funding up to a third of the cost of managed detection and response and application control services for up to three years. The EOTSS Office of Municipal and School Technology runs a Cybersecurity Awareness Grant Program free to participants, with public school districts explicitly eligible and applications opening each October. And 201 CMR 17.00 functions as the de facto mandate, enforceable by the Attorney General.

  • AI capacity building is a genuine differentiator: Massachusetts is unusually far along here. DESE has published implementation guidance, quick reference guides, an AI Literacy for Educators course with optional certification, a six-session recorded webinar series, and facilitation guides in two formats, and it is now running a policy considerations phase for 2026-27. MassCUE runs the PEAK cohort program on AI knowledge for educators and held a dedicated AI leadership event in August 2026. The proposed graduation framework would add a required AI and digital literacy module for all students.

  • Framing: The primary lens is equity and access, with a fast-growing instructional AI lens layered on top, and compliance handled outside the education agency. DESE's Office of Educational Technology organizes around equitable access, effective uses, and research and innovation, and frames technology skills as workforce readiness rather than wellbeing. Money questions get routed to broadband and E-rate. Security and privacy compliance sits with EOTSS, MassCyberCenter, the Attorney General, and TEC. The practical consequence: there is no DESE technology mandate to sell against and no DESE technology grant to sell into. Persuasion happens at the district and municipal level.

Sources: DESE educational technology | Cyber Resilient Massachusetts grant | Cybersecurity Awareness Grant Program | Edwin Analytics

7. Instructional Priorities & Mandates

Last verified: August 2026

  • Literacy became a statute two months ago: This is the biggest change in Massachusetts instructional policy. Chapter 113 of the Acts of 2026, An Act relative to teacher preparation and student literacy, passed the House 153-0 and the Senate 39-0 and was signed June 26, 2026. It requires evidence-based K-3 reading curriculum adoption by the 2027-28 school year or a waiver, universal literacy screening at least twice yearly, instruction addressing the five research components, restrictions on three-cueing as a substitute for systematic instruction, codified dyslexia screening, literacy training requirements for educator preparation programs, and annual public reporting on curriculum in use. The $25 million Early Literacy Fund transfer was dropped from the final compromise, so the mandate arrives without a dedicated appropriation.

  • The supporting programs are real: Mass Literacy is the statewide PreK-3 initiative, CURATE provides teacher-panel ratings of instructional materials, and Literacy Launch has carried $35 million in state funding across FY2025 and FY2026 plus a $38.4 million five-year federal grant. PRISM grants fund reading materials, literacy screeners, and coaching. In August 2026 the state announced roughly 8,500 early literacy tutoring seats across 338 schools in 102 districts for 2026-27.

  • Computer science: The Digital Literacy and Computer Science standards were adopted in 2016 across four strands. They are a standards framework, not a graduation requirement, and there is no state computer science credit mandate. A DLCS PreK-6 license is now available, expanding elementary licensure. Innovation Career Pathways and Early College are the state's preferred vehicles for technology exposure. The proposed graduation framework's required AI and digital literacy module is the closest thing to a CS mandate on the horizon, and it is not adopted.

  • Accountability and receivership: Massachusetts uses accountability percentiles with classification into categories, the most intensive being Chronically Underperforming, better known as receivership. As of August 2026 Lawrence remains in receivership, in place since November 2011, with a new receiver installed January 29, 2026, and Southbridge remains in receivership since January 2016. Holyoke has exited. Lawrence is a district in transition and worth treating as its own situation.

  • Graduation requirements are the live ambiguity: November 2024's Question 2 eliminated passing MCAS as a diploma requirement. MCAS is still administered for state and federal accountability, but districts currently set their own graduation requirements and there is no statewide competency determination in force. DESE's own graduation page still displays pre-Question 2 score thresholds and should not be quoted as current. Healey's Statewide K-12 Graduation Council released final recommendations June 17, 2026: the MassCore course sequence, new state-designed end-of-course assessments in English, math, and science, a capstone or portfolio, work-based learning milestones through MyCAP, civic readiness, a financial literacy module, and a required AI and digital literacy module.

  • And it is not adopted: BESE discussed the framework June 22-23, 2026 with members raising timing and cost objections, and took no vote. It requires statutory authority, and legislative consideration is unlikely before January 2027. The MTA opposes it, with President Max Page framing it as repackaged high-stakes testing against the 2024 vote. The proposed clock is MassCore alignment for ninth graders entering fall 2027 with full requirements from 2028, and $500,000 in grants is attached. Label it proposed, not adopted: Massachusetts high schools are in an 18 to 30 month period of graduation ambiguity.

  • CTE and the vocational lottery: Chapter 74 programs are governed by 603 CMR 4.00, amended by BESE in May 2025. The vocational admissions lottery is in force, not enjoined and not repealed, effective for students entering fall 2026. When demand exceeds seats, admission is by public lottery, and criteria may only function as weights: attendance, discipline, and demonstrated interest. It remains controversial, with families frustrated that admission now turns largely on chance. Live CTE funding runs through Perkins V and CTE planning grants, plus Skills Capital Grants for equipment.

Sources: Chapter 113 of the Acts of 2026 | Graduation Council recommendations | Receivership districts | 603 CMR 4.00 vocational admissions

8. Landscape & Pressures

Last verified: August 2026

  • Enrollment decline is driving everything: Foundation enrollment fell 14,626 in a single year to 890,681, and statewide enrollment is down more than 5%, roughly 50,000 students, since 2019. Drivers are declining birth rates, housing costs pushing young families out, and post-2020 shifts to private and home schooling. Small rural districts in the Berkshires, Franklin County, and the Cape and Islands are shrinking fastest, which is why FY2027 raised rural school aid. The structural problem is simple: Chapter 70 is enrollment-driven but district costs are not. A district losing 2% of its students does not lose 2% of its buildings, buses, or contracts. Every one of the 74 override questions in 2026 is downstream of that.

  • Workforce and labor: Persistent shortage fields are special education, English learner education, STEM, and school psychology. The state awarded $250,000 in planning grants in August 2026 to help districts build new licensure pathways, and emergency licensure pathways run partly through the collaboratives. On labor: strikes remain illegal under MGL c.150E s.9A and courts impose escalating daily fines, but a wave of strikes ran from 2022 through 2024, most visibly Newton, and right-to-strike legislation has not passed. Check whether a district is in an open contract year before scheduling a strategic conversation, because a contract fight consumes leadership bandwidth for months and freezes discretionary spending.

  • Facilities: The Massachusetts School Building Authority is funded by a dedicated 1% of the state sales tax and runs a Core Program entered through a Statement of Interest, an Accelerated Repair Program, and a heat pump conversion program that reopens in 2027. Construction cost pressure is the dominant story, and it interacts directly with Proposition 2.5: the local share of an MSBA project is almost always funded by a debt exclusion, and debt exclusions pass at 84% historically, adding $777 million in FY2026. That is the one property tax question Massachusetts voters reliably approve.

  • Choice pressure is real but not from vouchers: The Anti-Aid Amendment constitutionally bars public funds for private and sectarian schools, so there is no ESA, voucher, or tax-credit scholarship. The pressure comes from two other places. Charters are capped at 120 statewide with a district spending cap of 9% of net school spending, rising to 18% for the lowest-performing decile, and the 2016 ballot question to lift the cap failed decisively. Inter-district school choice under MGL c.76 s.12B is sharper: the receiving district gets 75% of its per-pupil spending capped at $5,000, and the Treasurer deducts that tuition off the top of the sending district's Chapter 70 aid. A district losing 100 students to choice loses up to $500,000 before it sees a dollar, with no reduction in fixed costs.

  • Federal climate: DESE administers the usual ESSA titles plus IDEA and Perkins, with FY27 applications open in GEM$. ESSER and ARP funds are in wind-down and the liquidation period has closed for most districts, which makes the post-ESSER cliff a live 2026 budget driver and the backdrop for both the override surge and Healey's supplemental request. Specific 2026 federal actions affecting Massachusetts, including any litigation the state has joined, could not be verified in this pass.

10. Key Notes for Engagement

Last verified: August 2026. WORKING DRAFT for Jeremiah's edit. Research-generated engagement judgment, not settled advice

How to think about Massachusetts right now:

  • The money conversation happens at town hall, not the district office: Massachusetts districts do not levy taxes. A school committee can want something badly and still not get it, because town meeting or the city council appropriates the bottom line and Proposition 2.5 caps the levy. Net school spending is the floor, and districts sitting at it have no discretionary capacity. Whether a district is at the floor, whether its town has passed an override recently, and whether it is a regional district assessing multiple towns tells you more than any conversation about need.

  • Learn the capital outlay exclusion: This is the most useful and least understood instrument in the state for technology. It funds one-year, cash, non-bonded capital, needs a two-thirds local body vote plus a ballot majority, and historically passes 73% of the time against roughly 50% for general overrides. With ESSER gone, the Student Opportunity Act ramp ending, and no state refresh program, it is the realistic path for a district-wide device refresh. The timing matters: a question has to be in front of the selectboard by roughly January or February to make a spring ballot.

  • Sign the TEC agreement once and you are cleared statewide: This is the structural opposite of New York. Massachusetts is contractually demanding but statutorily light, and the consortium model front-loads the compliance lift and then makes it reusable. Negotiate the master DPA with The Education Cooperative once, and each district after that signs a one-page Exhibit E. Vendors who refuse get publicly posted as declined. On the security side, expect to attest to a Written Information Security Program under 201 CMR 17.00 regardless of where your company sits.

  • Do not tell anyone the phone ban is law: It passed both chambers in different forms and it is stuck in conference over the social media provisions, not the phone provisions. It survived the July 31 deadline under new joint rules and can still pass in a pop-up session. Getting this wrong in a superintendent meeting is an unforced credibility error, and Massachusetts superintendents are following it closely because their districts are already writing local policies without a state model.

  • The AI window is genuinely open, and the state is helping: Massachusetts is further along than New York or Ohio on AI professional learning: real DESE guidance, an educator course with certification, a webinar series, facilitation guides, and a policy considerations phase running through 2026-27. Districts are writing AI policy this year with state encouragement. The educator-first move is to work inside that DESE frame, especially its advice on bias audits during procurement and on not relying on AI detection tools. This is the one state in the batch where the state's own materials give you a shared vocabulary.

  • Two dated, unfunded mandates create real demand: The literacy statute requires evidence-based K-3 curriculum adoption and twice-yearly universal screening by 2027-28, and its $25 million funding transfer was dropped from the final bill. The proposed graduation framework, if enacted, would require MassCore alignment for ninth graders entering fall 2027 plus capstone or portfolio work, MyCAP work-based learning tracking, financial literacy, and an AI and digital literacy module, with $500,000 attached. One is law and one is not. Both are the classic condition for a fast procurement cycle followed by a funding fight, and both are places to be useful before being commercial.

  • Watch FY2028, not FY2027: FY2027 completes the Student Opportunity Act phase-in. After that growth stops, and districts have spent six years layering recurring costs onto that ramp. Enrollment is down 50,000 since 2019, low-income counts fell 4.5% in one year (which cuts aid to the highest-need districts), and 74 override questions went to voters in a single year. Any multi-year proposal should be structured so a district is not exposed if FY2028 is worse than FY2027, and any conversation that assumes Massachusetts is a wealthy state with money will land badly.
