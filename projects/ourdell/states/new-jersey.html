1. Snapshot

Last verified: August 2026

Leadership & Governance
Governor Mikie Sherrill (D), sworn in January 20, 2026. New Jersey elects governors in odd years, so this is a first-year administration and there is no gubernatorial race in November 2026
Commissioner of Education Dr. Lily Laux, acting from February 4 and confirmed by the Senate February 24, 2026. An outsider hire from the Texas Education Agency, where she led science-of-reading training at scale, with prior work on instructional materials and responsible AI use in schools. Her predecessor was Kevin Dehmer
Stated priorities Student achievement, modernizing and stabilizing the funding formula, online child safety, and preschool expansion
State chief selection Appointed by the Governor with Senate confirmation. Note that the State Board, not the Commissioner, adopts the administrative code at N.J.A.C. Title 6A
State Board of Education Appointed by the Governor with Senate consent on staggered six-year terms. President Kathy Goldenberg of Burlington, vice president Mary G. Bennett of Essex. Tenure runs long, one member since 1990. A slow-moving body, and not where initiatives originate
Attorney General Jennifer Davenport, whose office is carrying New Jersey's case against Meta, which reached trial in August 2026 (see Section 3)
Local control Constrained in an unusual way. Districts have taxing authority within a hard 2 percent levy cap, but their budgets are approved by a state official at the county level rather than by voters (see Sections 4 and 5)
Scale
Districts 693 total local education agencies: 590 operating districts, 84 charters, 3 renaissance schools, and 16 non-operating districts that levy taxes and send students elsewhere. Use 590 for anything per-district
Students / schools 1,357,450 students in 2025-26, down roughly 23,700 or 1.7 percent, across 2,505 schools. That decline follows four years of growth and is the most consequential single number here
Shape of the market 590 operating districts for 1.36 million students means a median district around 1,500 to 2,000, and after Newark there is no dominant district. The opposite of a county-district state, and the central go-to-market fact
Regional layer Two separate structures. 21 County Offices of Education hold regulatory power and no purchasing role; Educational Services Commissions hold purchasing power and no regulatory role (see Section 2)
Largest districts Newark 44,199, Elizabeth 28,454, Jersey City 25,951, Paterson 24,291, Edison 17,032, Trenton 15,964, Toms River 14,454, Woodbridge 14,042
Charters and vo-tech 84 charters serving roughly 64,000 students with NJDOE as sole authorizer, concentrated in Newark, Paterson, Jersey City, Trenton, and Camden. Separately, 21 county vocational-technical districts anchor roughly 77,000 secondary CTE participants
Money & Calendar
Funding formula The School Funding Reform Act of 2008. The seven-year phase-out of adjustment aid under the 2018 S2 law completed with FY2025. FY2027 added a 3 percent floor on aid reductions and a 6 percent ceiling on increases, three-year averaging of income and property valuation, and a shift of special education aid to actual district enrollment
Budget cycle Annual, with a year-round legislature. Fiscal year July 1, with the Appropriations Act due by June 30. The FY2027 budget was signed on time at $60.7 billion
Current state budget K-12 formula aid $12.4 billion, up $372.1 million, across 574 districts with 400 increases and 167 reductions. Total PreK-12 aid $22.5 billion, more than 35 percent of the state budget. Preschool aid $1.38 billion. Extraordinary special education aid held flat at $420 million
Local tax cap A hard 2 percent levy cap with three allowable adjustments: enrollment growth, health care costs above 2 percent capped at the plan average, and pension contributions above 2 percent. Debt service and capital sit outside it. No adjustment for loss of state aid (see Section 5)
Local approval mechanism For roughly 577 of 590 operating districts there is no voter approval. The board adopts after a public hearing and the binding approval is the Executive County Superintendent's. Only 13 districts still hold April elections. Voters otherwise see only a separate question to exceed the cap, or a bond referendum
Quick Yes/No
State AI guidance for schools? Advisory only, and dated. NJDOE's core artifact is a set of questions for consideration from June 2024, and its AI page has not been substantively updated since January 2025. No mandate, no recurring funding. Bills that would change that sit in committee (see Section 3)
Cell phone restriction? YES, statewide, mandatory, K-12, in force this school year, signed January 8, 2026. Critically, district-issued devices are expressly outside it (see Section 3)
Screen time action? YES, and brand new. The Kids' Online Safety package signed August 11, 2026 creates a New Jersey Kids Code, a Social Media Research Center, and a warning-label study. No legal challenge as of mid-August (see Section 3)
Student data privacy law? No comprehensive student-specific statute. Narrow provisions on internet disclosure and pupil records, plus FERPA. The New Jersey Data Privacy Act exempts political subdivisions, so it does not regulate districts, though it reaches vendors
SDPC / standard NDPA state? Yes in practice, no in statute. The New Jersey Student Privacy Alliance is run by the state educational technology association rather than NJDOE, and participation is voluntary
1:1 / state device program? No state device program and no recurring ed-tech fund. The pandemic-era digital divide program has not recurred. The most under-used channel for infrastructure is the school construction grant program (see Section 5)
ESA / voucher program? None, and none close. The functioning alternative is Interdistrict Public School Choice, with 119 participating districts for 2026-27, where choice students bring state funding with them
Collective bargaining Strong, under the Employer-Employee Relations Act administered by PERC. Strikes are illegal by judicial precedent rather than statute, enforced through injunction and contempt

2. What This State Is Driving Right Now

Last verified: August 2026

New Jersey in 2026 is a first-year administration inheriting a funding formula everyone agrees is broken, a hard levy cap with no relief valve, and a health benefits increase that could reach 34 percent. Underneath that, the state has the most AI-fluent superintendent and technology-director corps in the region and no state AI policy at all. The superintendent list and the technology leader list below share almost nothing.

What superintendents are focused on

  • The levy cap squeezed between health benefits and state aid: The number one topic in New Jersey education by a wide margin, appearing in the boards association agenda and testimony, the business officials' testimony, the union's statements, the Commissioner's remarks, and district coverage. The mechanism makes it acute: the cap's health care adjustment is limited to the average plan increase, and the plan increase is what is exploding. The School Employees Health Benefits Commission deferred its vote on active-employee rates to August 19, 2026, with an increase near 34 percent under consideration. Note where the pressure does not reach: debt service and capital sit outside the cap entirely, which is why the school facilities grant channel is the one place in New Jersey where infrastructure money is not competing against the levy. See Sections 5 and 9.

  • A funding formula rewrite that has finally started: The Commissioner has called the 2008 formula outdated and asked for something more predictable and transparent, while deferring structural change out of the current cycle. On August 5, 2026 the administration launched a statewide funding outreach effort with the Hunt Institute, with a steering committee in fall 2026 and near-term recommendations to the Legislature. Every district budget conversation in 2026-27 sits under this. See Section 5.

  • Special education costs that the state formula no longer tracks: Extraordinary special education aid was held flat at $420 million while average per-pupil special education cost rose from $19,519 in FY2009 to $38,984 in FY2026, and reimbursement fell to roughly 52 percent of eligible costs. The boards association notes that median tuition at approved private schools now exceeds the aid threshold, so the extraordinary program no longer covers extraordinary cases. See Section 5.

  • Enrollment reversing after four years of growth: Statewide enrollment fell to 1,357,450 in 2025-26 from 1,381,182, a decline of roughly 23,700 students. Under an enrollment-driven formula that converts directly into aid loss for shrinking districts, and it lands in the same year the formula itself is under review. Expect defensive budgeting and per-pupil cost scrutiny through 2027-28. See Section 9.

  • Consolidation and shared services, pushed from the top and resisted from below: The Governor used the budget address to signal consolidation and named software explicitly among the shared services worth pursuing, and the Commissioner has echoed it. The boards association expressly opposes forced consolidation or regionalization without community support. That collision is live and shapes how any multi-district proposal is received. See Section 4.

What technology leaders are focused on

  • The phone law is in force right now, and district devices are expressly permitted: Every board must have a bell-to-bell policy in place for 2026-27 covering personal internet-enabled devices during school hours, on buses, and at school-sanctioned events. NJDOE's July 2026 guidance states plainly that students may use district-issued technology and sponsored platforms during the school day. That single sentence makes school-managed hardware the compliant instructional channel, and it is the most commercially useful fact in this profile. See Section 3.

  • An AI vacuum that the practitioner community has already outgrown: The state technology conference ran more than 40 AI sessions in January 2026, including building a secure local model, while NJDOE's guidance remains a set of questions from June 2024, its AI page has not been substantively updated since January 2025, and its one-time grants expired January 31, 2026 with no second round. Bills requiring district AI policies and state instructional technology guidelines sit in committee. The clearest strategic opening in the state. See Section 3.

  • Route-to-market risk in the co-op districts rely on: In January 2026 the State Comptroller directed the Educational Services Commission of New Jersey not to proceed with proposed contracts and not to extend existing ones past their March 31, 2026 expiration, having found it had never submitted a contract for review since 2008. No public follow-up has been issued. Because that commission is how many districts buy technology without running their own bid, any co-op route to market needs a contract-validity check. See Section 6.

  • A 72-hour cyber reporting clock that reaches vendors too: New Jersey requires public agencies to report cybersecurity incidents to the state within 72 hours of reasonable belief, and the statute expressly covers government contractors with access to or hosting of an agency's networks, systems, applications, or information. A vendor operating district systems is inside the reporting perimeter. Technology adequacy is also scored inside QSAC rather than treated separately. See Sections 4 and 7.

  • An adaptive assessment stack and a graduation test with fresh cut scores: New Jersey's assessments are now adaptive, delivered through Cambium rather than the Pearson lineage many still assume, and testing calendars for 2026-27 and 2027-28 were published in May 2026. Cut scores for the class of 2027 graduation assessment were approved August 5, 2026. Anyone planning readiness on a legacy platform assumption is planning against the wrong stack. See Section 7.

The regional service agency view

Calibration: closest to Ohio's ESC and ITC model, influential and frequently the contracting party but genuinely bypassable, and clearly not New York's BOCES. The defining asymmetry is that New Jersey splits the regional role in two: county offices hold regulatory power with no purchasing role, and Educational Services Commissions hold purchasing power with no regulatory role. Neither substitutes for the other.

  • County Offices of Education hold a regional veto, not a regional channel: Each of the 21 counties has an Executive County Superintendent who reviews and approves every district budget before adoption, may order changes in proposed expenditures, approves superintendent and administrator contracts, gatekeeps use of banked cap, approves transportation contracts, and administers QSAC monitoring. A district's capacity to spend on anything discretionary is filtered through a state employee at the county level.

  • And that office is running on interims: As of August 2026 not one of the 21 counties has a permanent Executive County Superintendent. Sixteen are interim, four are vacant, and one person is listed as interim in two counties at once. The practical read: county approval is a timing risk rather than a relationship opportunity, and there is no durable county executive to build a multi-year relationship with. Build slack into any procurement calendar that lands near spring budget adoption.

  • Educational Services Commissions are actual school districts, and that is why they can contract: An ESC is established under state law as a public entity providing educational and administrative services, with member districts joining by board resolution. Because an ESC is itself a contracting unit, it can serve as lead agency for a registered cooperative pricing system. The Educational Services Commission of New Jersey is the dominant one and its technology catalog is deep: digital and instructional resources, technology supplies and services, internet access and data transmission, consulting, unified communications, phone systems, device repair, and maintenance management.

  • Why this is not BOCES: New York's BOCES channel is close to mandatory because BOCES Aid makes the same purchase cheaper. New Jersey has no analogous aid mechanism, and a district has at least five lawful paths: state contract without bidding, federal supply schedules, its own bid, informal quotes under the threshold, or any registered co-op. The ESC channel is strong through convenience rather than compulsion, which matters most for districts without a qualified purchasing agent.

  • What this means in practice: Win the ESC contract for market access. Win the district for the decision. Respect the county office for the calendar. A vendor who courts one of the three while ignoring the others will hit a wall.

3. Hot Topics: The Four Questions of 2026

Last verified: August 2026

New Jersey has moved fast on two of these and barely at all on the other two. The phone law and the platform package were both enacted within the last eight months, while AI guidance is two years old and there is still no student data privacy statute. That split is the story: districts here face hard new obligations on devices and platforms, and an open field on everything else.

ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE | Advisory, dated, and unfunded. The practitioner community is well ahead of the state.

  • What exists: NJDOE's core artifact is Questions for Consideration Regarding AI Systems in Education, dated June 6, 2024: twelve prompts, six for district leaders and six for educators, covering policy review, acceptable use, professional development, bias, data privacy, and alignment to district policy. It is framed for consideration rather than compliance, and there is no mandate for district AI policies.

  • The funding, which has ended: The FY2025 budget allocated $1.5 million across two one-time programs: an AI Innovation in Education grant to 10 districts totaling $747,805, restricted to districts with an existing AI curriculum oversight committee, and an AI career pathways grant to two vocational districts totaling $713,872. Both ran through January 31, 2026 with no second round announced.

  • The bills that would change it: Assembly Bill 5184 would require boards to adopt an AI policy and NJDOE to publish a model policy, introduced June 1, 2026. Senate Bill 2862 would require AI instruction and model curricula, introduced January 13, 2026. Assembly Bill 5292 would require NJDOE instructional technology guidelines that boards must adopt, introduced June 18, 2026. None has moved. One AI bill has advanced: transparency policies for AI-enabled surveillance systems passed the Assembly in February 2026.

  • The gap, stated plainly: New Jersey has an AI-experienced Commissioner, a marquee state AI hub at Princeton with no K-12 arm, and a technology leadership community that ran more than 40 AI sessions in January 2026. Against that, the state offers two-year-old questions and no money. Districts are writing AI policy without a framework to align to, which makes policy and professional learning support the highest-value conversation here.

Source: NJDOE AI resources | Questions for Consideration | 2025 AI grant awards | Assembly Bill 5292

SCREEN TIME | Enacted five days before this profile. Brand new, not yet challenged, and separate from the phone law.

  • The package: The Kids' Online Safety package was signed August 11, 2026. The New Jersey Kids Code Act requires online service providers to limit data collection from minors, set default privacy settings to the highest level, safeguard minor-adult interactions, restrict engagement-maximizing features, and give children and parents control over algorithmic recommendations, enforced by the Attorney General and the Department of Health. Companion bills establish a Social Media Research Center at a public university with $500,000 from FY2027 and direct it to study warning-label efficacy within 18 months.

  • Litigation status: No challenge to the New Jersey Kids Code had been filed as of mid-August 2026, though the leading industry group testified against it. Given the pattern in comparable states, treat this as the most volatile item in the profile and re-check it.

  • The Attorney General is already in court: New Jersey sued Meta alongside 41 other attorneys general over harms to youth, and separately sued TikTok. On June 30, 2026 a federal court denied Meta's summary judgment motion in full, letting federal children's privacy and New Jersey consumer fraud claims proceed; jury selection began August 12, 2026. The state also created an Office of Youth Online Mental Health Safety and Awareness on the Governor's first day, funded at $125,000.

  • The older, distinct mandate: Separately, New Jersey was the first state to require K-12 information literacy instruction, signed January 4, 2023, with State Board-adopted standards. It is a curriculum mandate rather than platform regulation, and NJDOE files it under its artificial intelligence section, which tells you the state treats AI literacy and information literacy as the same competency.

Source: Kids' Online Safety package signing | Legislative coverage | Meta litigation | Information literacy law

CELL PHONES / CONNECTED DEVICES | Statewide, mandatory, K-12, in force this school year, with district-issued devices expressly permitted.

  • The law: P.L. 2025 c.195 was signed January 8, 2026, twelve days before the prior governor left office. It requires every board to adopt a bell-to-bell policy prohibiting student non-academic use of personal internet-enabled devices during school hours, on school buses, and at school-sanctioned events, grades K through 12, effective for the 2026-2027 school year. NJDOE released guidelines in January 2026 and frequently asked questions in July 2026.

  • School-issued devices are explicitly outside it: The statute's definition of an internet-enabled device excludes school-provided devices used for educational purposes, and NJDOE's July 2026 guidance is unambiguous: students are allowed to use district-issued technology, such as district-issued laptops, and sponsored platforms during the school day. Covered personal devices include smartwatches, earbuds, and headphones.

  • Exceptions: Personal devices are permitted to fulfill accommodations in an IEP, to support a 504 plan, to implement a student health plan, and for translation services, plus principal authorization for a specified purpose, emergencies and perceived threats, caregiving responsibilities with approval, and legally required use.

  • What the state funded, and what it did not: A Phone-Free Schools Grant opened October 2025 with a $500 minimum per district, eligible for locked pouches, lockers, bins, cabinets, related equipment and software, and staff training. Eighty-six grants were awarded, and districts adopting only an off-and-away or limited-use policy did not qualify. Note what is absent: neither the statute nor the guidance imposes any reporting to NJDOE. Enforcement runs through local policy adoption and county office oversight.

Source: P.L. 2025 c.195 | NJDOE July 2026 FAQs | NJDOE guidance | Phone-Free Schools Grant

STUDENT DATA PRIVACY | No comprehensive student privacy statute, a consumer law that exempts districts, a voluntary alliance, and a hard 72-hour cyber clock.

  • What actually governs: New Jersey has no analogue to a student privacy operator statute. What exists is a narrow provision barring internet disclosure of certain student information without parental consent, the pupil records statute and its regulations, and FERPA. There is no state-level vendor certification regime.

  • The consumer law, and the common error: The New Jersey Data Privacy Act took effect January 15, 2025. Two facts matter. It expressly exempts state entities and political subdivisions, so school districts are not regulated as controllers under it. But it does not exempt FERPA-governed data, so it can reach ed-tech vendors handling New Jersey student data who meet its thresholds. Any claim that districts must comply with it as controllers is wrong, and it circulates widely.

  • The alliance that does the practical work: The New Jersey Student Privacy Alliance is administered by the state educational technology association rather than NJDOE, a business official group, or an ESC, and participates in the national Student Data Privacy Consortium. New Jersey is an alliance state in practice and a non-statutory one in law: participation is voluntary, with no state requirement behind it. Absent that, a data privacy agreement is a district-by-district negotiation signed by the superintendent or business administrator.

  • Cybersecurity reporting is real and it reaches vendors: Every public agency must report a cybersecurity incident to the state Office of Homeland Security and Preparedness within 72 hours of reasonably believing one has occurred. School districts are covered as political subdivisions, and the statute expressly covers government contractors with access to or hosting of an agency's networks, systems, applications, or information. Breach notification law separately requires State Police notification before affected individuals.

Source: Cyber incident reporting law | New Jersey Data Privacy Act | N.J.S.A. 18A:36-35 | Student Data Privacy Consortium

4. Governance & Decision-Making

Last verified: August 2026

Authority flows Governor to Commissioner to the State Board of Education to NJDOE, then through 21 County Offices of Education to roughly 590 operating districts. New Jersey is a strong regulatory state: the State Board adopts N.J.A.C. Title 6A, which regulates instructional program, facilities, personnel, and governance at a level of specificity most states leave to districts. A practice can be legal in principle and still non-compliant in Title 6A detail.

  • The county office is the pressure point: Created in 2007, the Executive County Superintendent reviews and approves every district budget before adoption against efficiency standards, achievement data, audits, maintenance plans, and long-range facilities plans, and may order changes in proposed expenditures. The office also approves superintendent and administrator contracts including salary cap compliance, may disapprove use of banked cap where a district has not implemented available administrative efficiencies, approves transportation contracts, and runs QSAC monitoring. As of August 2026 none of the 21 offices has a permanent occupant: sixteen are interim, four are vacant, and one person covers two counties.

  • QSAC is where technology gets scored: The Quality Single Accountability Continuum is the department's monitoring and district self-evaluation system, framed around capacity building rather than pure compliance, with five District Performance Review components: instruction and program, fiscal management, governance, operations, and personnel. Instructional technology and data live inside instruction and program and inside operations, which means technology adequacy is not a separate conversation in New Jersey; it is scored inside the state's own accountability instrument. A proposal that maps to a QSAC indicator is speaking the state's language.

  • The legislature: New Jersey is a Democratic trifecta: the Senate is 25 to 15 and the Assembly is 57 to 23 after the November 2025 election. Senate Education is chaired by Vin Gopal, who also carried the 2024 literacy screening law. Assembly Education is chaired by Verlina Reynolds-Jackson, a change from the prior session. Money runs through Senate Budget and Appropriations under Paul Sarlo and Assembly Budget under Eliana Pintor Marin. The most active recent K-12 technology bills are the AI surveillance transparency bill, the instructional technology guidelines bill, and the AI policy mandate bill.

  • Election-year context, provisional note: New Jersey elects governors in odd years, so there is no statewide executive race on November 3, 2026. What is on the ballot is congressional, including a special election to fill the seat the Governor vacated, plus local school board seats. The real provisional element is different from the rest of this region: this is the Sherrill administration's first full year, and it has already opened the biggest structural question in the state by putting the funding formula itself under review. Treat everything about aid distribution beyond FY2027 as unsettled.

  • Governance exceptions: the state-operated era is mostly over, with one exception and one new case: Newark returned to local control in 2018, and NJDOE declared it a high performing district on July 1, 2026 after it met weighted indicators in all five QSAC areas. Superintendent Roger Leon's contract runs to June 2030 and the board president is Hasani K. Council. Paterson returned in 2021 under Superintendent Dr. Laurie W. Newell; Jersey City returned in 2022 under Superintendent Dr. Norma Fernandez. Camden remains state-operated, with NJDOE naming Alfonso Q. Llano Jr. as State Superintendent effective March 1, 2026. The live takeover story is Lakewood: NJDOE filed to initiate full state intervention on January 14, 2026 citing deficiencies in governance, finance, curriculum, transportation, and special education, following a court finding of ongoing constitutional deprivation. The district is contesting it and the matter was still before an administrative law judge as of mid-2026.

  • Consolidation is a live governance agenda: The Governor used the budget address to press shared services and consolidation, naming software among the targets, and the state funds feasibility work through a local efficiency program and a school regionalization program. The boards association opposes forced consolidation or regionalization without community support. Any multi-district proposal lands inside that argument, so know which side of it the district you are visiting sits on.

Sources: NJDOE county offices | QSAC | Newark high performing designation | Lakewood intervention

5. Funding & Budget Rhythm

Last verified: August 2026. FY2027 Appropriations Act signed June 30, 2026

  • The levy cap, and why it is the whole conversation: The 2010 law reduced the prior 4 percent cap to a hard 2 percent and made it permanent. There are three allowable adjustments: enrollment increases, using weighted resident enrollment with tiered multipliers of 0.50, 0.75, and 1.00 as growth rises; health care costs, but only the portion above 2 percent and only up to the average state plan increase; and pension contributions above 2 percent. Debt service and capital sit outside the cap. What the law eliminated matters as much: commissioner waivers for energy, capital, and high-cost special education are gone, and so is the automatic adjustment for loss of state aid. A district that loses aid gets no cap relief by right.

  • Why the health benefits vote is existential: The health care adjustment is capped at the average plan increase, and the plan increase is what is exploding. The commission deferred its vote on active-employee rates from July 27 to August 19, 2026, with an increase near 34 percent under consideration, having approved 11 percent for early retirees and 6 percent for Medicare-eligible retirees effective January 2027. Plan enrollment is projected to fall about 9 percent in 2027 as districts exit, leaving the rest subsidizing the departures. Anyone selling a multi-year commitment here should know where this landed.

  • Banked cap and the separate question: Unused levy authority may be used in any of the next three budget years, oldest first, but the Executive County Superintendent may disapprove it where a district has not implemented available administrative efficiencies. A district may also put a separate question to voters to exceed the adjusted cap, subject to two constraints: it must be in the preliminary budget, and it cannot fund programs necessary to meet the Student Learning Standards. Note also that New Jersey has no flat 2 percent administrative spending cap; what exists is a regional per-pupil administrative cost limit enforced by the county office.

  • The local approval mechanism, stated explicitly: For roughly 577 of 590 operating districts there is no voter approval of the budget. Most districts moved school elections to November in 2012, which eliminated the April budget vote so long as the budget stays within the cap. Only 13 districts still hold April elections. For everyone else the board adopts after a public hearing and the binding approval is the Executive County Superintendent's. This is the most misunderstood fact about New Jersey school finance and the most important one for a multi-year commitment: the buyer's budget is approved by a state official at the county level, not by a local electorate.

  • What the FY2027 budget did: Signed on time at $60.7 billion. K-12 formula aid is $12.4 billion, up $372.1 million, the largest in state history, across 574 districts with 400 increases and 167 reductions, losses capped at 3 percent and gains at 6 percent. Total PreK-12 aid is $22.5 billion. Preschool aid rose 9.3 percent to $1.38 billion. Extraordinary special education aid was held flat at $420 million, high-impact tutoring doubled to $15 million, and school construction received $350 million plus $50 million in capital grants. The three real formula changes were the loss floor and gain ceiling, three-year averaging of income and property valuation, and a shift of special education aid to actual district enrollment.

  • The aid redistribution fight, as context: The 2018 S2 law phased out adjustment aid over seven years, completing with FY2025, reallocating from over-adequacy districts to under-adequacy ones. The phase-out is complete but the losers did not stop losing. In FY2027 the largest gains went to Newark at $60.6 million, Paterson at $37.1 million, and Trenton at $24.0 million, while West New York, Jersey City, and Bridgeton took the largest dollar cuts. That urban gains sit alongside shore and suburban cuts is precisely the political fault line, and the 3 percent floor and 6 percent ceiling are the current de facto stabilization mechanism.

  • Where technology money lives: There is no recurring state educational technology or device appropriation. The Phone-Free Schools Grant was the only recent hardware-adjacent state money. Federal E-rate carries connectivity. The most under-used channel is school facilities: the Regular Operating District grant program funds at least 40 percent of eligible project costs, with the actual share set by each district's aid percentage, and the state construction authority lists technology infrastructure upgrades among eligible project types. In a state with a hard levy cap and no ed-tech fund, an infrastructure project inside a facilities grant is the most reliable path to funded network work.

Sources: Levy cap law | FY2027 budget signing | NJSBA budget analysis | SDA grant program

6. Procurement & Vendor Path

Last verified: August 2026

  • Framework and the current thresholds: The Public School Contracts Law governs. The statutory base figures still published on statute mirror sites are from 1999 and are long superseded; the current amounts took effect July 1, 2025. With a Qualified Purchasing Agent a board's bid threshold is $53,000 with a quotation threshold of $7,950. Without one it is $39,000 and $5,850. Quotation thresholds are 15 percent of the bid threshold. The prior cycle's figures of $44,000 and $29,000 still circulate widely in vendor materials and are wrong. The Qualified Purchasing Agent certification is administered by the Division of Local Government Services, and whether a district's business administrator holds it swings every purchase decision by $14,000, so ask early.

  • State contract is a genuine bypass: A board may purchase goods or services without advertising for bids under a contract entered into on behalf of the state by the Division of Purchase and Property, and may also use federal supply schedules subject to price conditions. The platform is NJSTART, and districts join the state cooperative purchasing program by request. This is one of the cleanest paths in the state and it is frequently overlooked in favor of a co-op.

  • Cooperative pricing, and why national co-op awards do not travel: This is where the Section 2 calibration cashes out, and New Jersey is materially more restrictive than most states. A cooperative pricing system must be registered with and approved by the Director of the Division of Local Government Services, the lead agency must be a New Jersey contracting unit that advertises for bids and awards a master contract, an assigned alphanumeric identifier must appear on all bidding documents and purchase orders, bid documents must require uniform pricing, and late-joining members need approval from both the lead agency and the contractor. A national cooperative headquartered elsewhere does not satisfy that structure on its own. Treat any internal claim that a national co-op award covers New Jersey school business as a red flag.

  • What carries the load instead: The route that demonstrably works is national specifications bid by a New Jersey lead agency. The Educational Services Commission of New Jersey runs multi-state cooperative specifications through its own New Jersey-compliant bid, visible in its contract numbering, which is how New Jersey districts get national-scale pricing without violating New Jersey procurement law. Its technology catalog covers digital and instructional resources, technology supplies and services, internet access and data transmission, technology consulting, unified communications, hybrid phone systems, device repair, and maintenance management, on an annual bid cycle. Other county educational services commissions run smaller versions of the same model.

  • One caution on that route: In January 2026 the State Comptroller directed ESCNJ not to proceed with proposed public contracts and not to extend current contracts beyond their March 31, 2026 expiration until outstanding findings were resolved, having found the commission had never submitted a contract for review since its 2008 creation and had failed to report roughly 43 contracts above the review threshold across three fiscal years. No public follow-up has been issued and the co-op's published bid series has not advanced to the current fiscal year, while the organization continues to run vendor events. Verify contract vintage and validity before relying on a specific award.

  • Vendor compliance is not trivial here: New Jersey imposes real, non-waivable steps: pay-to-play political contribution disclosure and certification, a Business Registration Certificate, ownership disclosure, and at the state contract level a series of certifications covering investigations, investment activities, and affirmative action. On data privacy the operative document is a district-level agreement signed by the superintendent or business administrator, since there is no state template; the voluntary alliance in Section 3 is the closest thing to a shared instrument. The Kids Code adds a new design-code compliance surface for any student-facing product.

7. Technology & Digital Learning Posture

Last verified: August 2026

  • Devices: No statewide 1:1 program and no recurring state device fund. The pandemic-era digital divide grant program does not appear as an active program, and NJDOE frames its educational technology work as capacity and standards support rather than funding: digital literacy across the standards, equitable access to broadband and technology, infrastructure capacity planning, and technology-driven instructional models. The tools it points to are a technology readiness self-assessment and a sustainability recognition program. Post-relief refresh is therefore a local budget problem funded from a levy capped at 2 percent, which is precisely why co-op device and device-repair contracts matter so much here.

  • Assessment, and the platform many people get wrong: New Jersey's assessments are the adaptive Student Learning Assessment, science, the adaptive Graduation Proficiency Assessment, the alternate assessment, English language proficiency, and the national assessment. Delivery runs through the New Jersey Assessments Portal operated by Cambium, not the Pearson lineage many still assume, and adaptive delivery is the material change. Testing calendars for 2026-27 and 2027-28 were published in May 2026. For graduation, the class of 2027 has three pathways: the adaptive assessment in grade 11, with cut scores approved August 5, 2026 at 450 in both subjects; substitute competency tests such as the ACT or SAT; and a grade 12 portfolio appeal. That structure is the state's settlement of the long-running graduation testing fight.

  • Statewide platforms, and what is absent: NJDOE operates a learning management system, but it is a professional learning platform for educators and administrators rather than a student platform, currently hosting literacy implementation coursework. There is no state student learning management system, no statewide digital content library, and no state open educational resources repository. State data reporting runs through the state longitudinal system with defined submission deadlines. A standards and mastery platform is referenced alongside the information literacy mandate.

  • Cybersecurity: New Jersey has a statutory mandatory reporting requirement: public agencies must report cybersecurity incidents to the state Office of Homeland Security and Preparedness within 72 hours of reasonably believing an incident has occurred, and the requirement expressly extends to government contractors with access to or hosting of an agency's networks, systems, applications, or information. School districts are covered as political subdivisions. The state cyber center provides advisory and threat intelligence services to districts. The 2026 session's directly relevant technology governance bill, requiring NJDOE instructional technology guidelines that boards must adopt, remains in committee.

  • AI capacity building sits with the associations: NJDOE's posture is advisory and its materials are two years old. The capacity building is happening at the superintendents association's technology conference, which ran more than 40 AI sessions in January 2026 including secure local model deployment and AI literacy through ethics scenarios, and at the state educational computing cooperative, whose 2026 theme was finding the balance in the classroom. The state's marquee AI hub, anchored at Princeton with state economic development and industry partners, is a higher education, workforce, and library initiative with no K-12 arm.

  • Framing: New Jersey frames educational technology through a compliance and equity lens rather than an instructional transformation lens. Technology is scored inside QSAC rather than promoted as its own initiative. The department's ed-tech page leads with equitable access and infrastructure readiness, and its flagship tool is a readiness self-assessment. The state learning management system serves professional learning, not student instruction. AI guidance is questions rather than standards, and the live 2026 bill would require districts to adopt a policy rather than do anything instructional. The state tells districts what they must not get wrong and leaves what to build to them. Anchor conversations here in QSAC indicators, budget-cycle timing, and equity of access, not in transformation narratives the state has not authorized.

Sources: NJDOE assessment | New Jersey Assessments Portal | NJDOE educational technology | Class of 2027 graduation requirements

8. Instructional Priorities & Mandates

Last verified: August 2026

  • Literacy is screening and professional development, not curriculum: The 2024 literacy law requires at least two literacy screenings annually for every student in kindergarten through grade three, with results shared with parents, plus annual training for staff and administrators in kindergarten through grade six against a no-cost state program. It does not mandate a curriculum. Two grant streams support implementation: a broad program funding 305 districts at $1.95 million, and a targeted program at $1.97 million for high-need schools where at least 70 percent of students are not meeting proficiency. The distinction matters commercially: assessment and data tooling has a mandate behind it, core reading curriculum does not.

  • Computer science is a standards area, delivered through regional hubs: Computer science and design thinking standards were adopted in 2020 and sit as one of nine content areas in the state learning standards. Capacity is delivered through three regional computer science hubs at Montclair State, The College of New Jersey, and Stockton, offering free K-12 professional development, technical assistance, classroom resources, and lending libraries stocked with microcontrollers and robots. That lending-library model is unusual and worth knowing, because it means some districts have hands-on hardware exposure without owning it.

  • New Jersey mandates more instruction than any state in the region: Climate change is integrated across all standards, with more than $10 million allocated since 2023, four regional learning collaboratives, and 32 districts funded in one recent year reaching more than 14,500 students. Information literacy is a first-in-the-nation K-12 requirement. On top of those sit mental health, financial literacy, Amistad, Holocaust, LGBTQ-inclusive curriculum, Asian American history, and, newly, cursive from 2026-27. For a district curriculum office this volume of mandate is itself the workload, and a real constraint on appetite for anything additional.

  • Accountability runs on three layers: The School Performance Reports, released for 2024-25 in May 2026 in a redesigned format with interactive dashboards and five years of trend data across achievement, college and career preparation, graduation, climate including chronic absenteeism, staff retention, and per-pupil spending; the federal plan; and QSAC as the distinctive state instrument. QSAC is the one that changes district behavior, because it is a monitoring cycle with consequences rather than a public report.

  • CTE and workforce: Roughly 77,000 secondary students participate across 16 career clusters and 79 pathways aligned to construction and energy, finance, health care, technology, manufacturing, and transportation and logistics. Twenty-one county vocational-technical school districts anchor the system, and a record 367 students earned associate degrees before high school graduation in July 2026. This is the most natural technology adjacency in New Jersey that is not about devices, and the county vocational districts are institutionally distinct buyers worth approaching separately.

  • Preschool expansion is a facilities story as much as an instructional one: Expansion continues on a phased plan using a mixed delivery model across district, private provider, and Head Start settings, with an annual expansion funding opportunity and districts identified for future years. Preschool Education Aid rose 9.3 percent to $1.38 billion in FY2027. The practical consequence is that expansion consumes classroom space and drives facilities decisions in exactly the districts with the least slack.

Sources: 2024 literacy law | Computer science standards and hubs | Climate change education | School Performance Reports

9. Landscape & Pressures

Last verified: August 2026

  • Enrollment has turned, and it matters more here than in most states: Statewide enrollment fell to 1,357,450 in 2025-26 from 1,381,182 the prior year, a decline of roughly 23,700 students, reversing four consecutive years of growth. Under an enrollment-driven formula that converts directly into aid loss for shrinking districts, and it lands in the same year the administration opened a formula review. Combined with a hard levy cap, it produces defensive budgeting and per-pupil cost scrutiny across 2026-27 and 2027-28.

  • The 2026-27 budget cycle was visibly painful: Districts reported deficits from $5 million to $100 million at legislative hearings, and the cuts were broad: Camden issued roughly 100 layoff notices, Lakewood 164, Passaic 51, Middletown cut about 40 jobs and closed two elementary schools, and Ocean Gate closed its only school. Toms River clashed openly with NJDOE and was forced into a large tax increase to pass a budget. The business officials association asked the state to raise the surplus cap from 2 percent to 4 percent and to align state and school budget timelines, since districts build budgets in March while the state does not finish until June.

  • Teacher certification has been loosened, and the pipeline runs on alternate route: New Jersey eliminated the performance-based assessment requirement for certification in 2022 and eliminated the basic skills test requirement effective January 1, 2025. Alternate route remains a major supply channel. Note the sequencing for anyone who last looked at New Jersey certification a few years ago: both of the barriers most often cited are gone.

  • Facilities, and the channel nobody uses: The Schools Development Authority operates capital, emergent, and regular operating district grant programs plus school security compliance work. The regular operating district program funds at least 40 percent of eligible project costs with the actual share set by each district's aid percentage, and the authority lists technology infrastructure upgrades among eligible project types. In a state with a hard levy cap and no educational technology fund, this is the most under-exploited funding channel for network and infrastructure work in New Jersey, and almost nobody is talking about it.

  • Choice pressure is charter concentration, not vouchers: New Jersey has no voucher and no education savings account. Interdistrict Public School Choice has 119 participating districts for 2026-27, and choice students bring state funding with them. The real pressure is charter concentration: 84 charters serving roughly 64,000 students, heavily concentrated in Newark, Paterson, Jersey City, Trenton, and Camden. Per-pupil dollars leave the district budget while fixed costs including buildings, transportation, central office, and network and device infrastructure do not scale down proportionally. That is how technology refresh gets deferred in exactly the districts with the greatest need.

  • Property tax politics shape everything: New Jersey carries the nation's highest property taxes and school taxes are the largest component, which makes the levy cap politically untouchable and referenda expensive. Two large direct-relief programs compete for the same state dollars, one of which moved to automatic filing in 2026 with a November 2 deadline. The tension is now explicit, because the funding formula review and the relief programs are running in the same fiscal year.

11. Key Notes for Engagement

Last verified: August 2026. WORKING DRAFT for Jeremiah's edit. Research-generated engagement judgment, not settled advice

How to think about New Jersey right now:

  • Open with the cap, not with the state aid number: The hard 2 percent levy cap with only three adjustments, none of them for lost state aid, is the constraint every superintendent and business administrator is living inside. Pair that with the health benefits increase near 34 percent and you have described their year better than any headline aid figure does. This is the state where showing you understand the mechanism, rather than the total, is the credibility test.

  • The budget is approved by a state official, not by voters, and that changes the calendar: For roughly 577 of 590 districts the binding approval is the Executive County Superintendent's, and every one of those 21 offices is currently led by an interim or vacant. Build slack into any procurement timeline that lands near spring budget adoption, and do not assume a relationship at the county level will move anything.

  • Know the current thresholds and ask about the purchasing agent: The bid threshold is $53,000 with a Qualified Purchasing Agent and $39,000 without, effective July 1, 2025, with quotation thresholds of $7,950 and $5,850. The prior figures are still circulating in vendor material and are wrong by nearly $10,000. Asking early whether a district's business administrator holds the designation tells you immediately which deals need a public bid.

  • Do not assume a national cooperative award travels here: New Jersey requires a registered cooperative pricing system with a New Jersey contracting unit as lead agency and a state-assigned identifier on every document. The route that works is national specifications bid by a New Jersey lead agency, which in practice means an ESC award, a state contract through the Division of Purchase and Property, or a district bid. Verify current ESC contract validity given the pending comptroller matter before relying on a specific award.

  • The phone law made school-issued devices the compliant channel, and the state said so in writing: NJDOE's July 2026 guidance states that students may use district-issued technology and sponsored platforms during the school day, and the statute excludes school-provided devices by definition. That is a clean, quotable, educator-first fact: the restriction is on personal devices, and district-managed hardware is how instruction continues. It is also the most useful thing a technology director can tell a nervous board.

  • AI is the open field, and the state has left it open: Two-year-old questions for consideration, no mandate, no recurring funding, and a bill sitting in committee, against a technology leadership community that ran 40-plus AI sessions in January. Helping districts write policy, plan professional learning, and think through responsible implementation is genuinely needed work here, and it puts Dell in the conversation before the state framework exists rather than after.

  • Point people at the facilities grant channel, because almost nobody does: The regular operating district grant program funds at least 40 percent of eligible project costs and lists technology infrastructure upgrades among eligible project types. In a state with a hard levy cap and no ed-tech fund, that is the most reliable path to funded network and infrastructure work, and it is materially under-used. Knowing it exists is a differentiator with a business administrator.

  • 590 districts means coverage is the strategic problem: After Newark there is no dominant district and the median is around 1,500 to 2,000 students. That makes the ESC contract, the state contract, and the two annual gatherings that reach everyone, Workshop in October and Techspo in January, disproportionately valuable. It also means the 21 county vocational districts are worth treating as a distinct segment, since they are institutionally separate buyers with their own CTE funding.
