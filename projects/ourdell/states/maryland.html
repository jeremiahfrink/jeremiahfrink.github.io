1. Snapshot

Last verified: August 2026

Leadership & Governance
Governor Wes Moore (D), inaugurated January 2023. On the November 3, 2026 ballot in a rematch against Republican Dan Cox. Maryland elects governors in midterm years, so a term begins January 2027 either way
State Superintendent Dr. Carey M. Wright, appointed by the State Board rather than the Governor, on a four-year term running to June 30, 2028. She sits in the Governor's cabinet, which is a political relationship rather than an appointing one
State chief selection The State Board appoints and can decline to reappoint. The Governor has no direct removal power. Outsiders get this wrong constantly, and it determines who a district actually answers to
State Board of Education 14 members appointed by the Governor with Senate consent on four-year terms, including a teacher, a parent, and a student. President Dr. Joshua L. Michael, vice president Dr. James C. Bell, elected July 2026
Accountability and Implementation Board Maryland's second oversight body and its distinguishing structural feature. Seven members on six-year terms, chaired by Isiah "Ike" Leggett, executive director Rachel Hise. It approves or rejects every district's Blueprint implementation plan and decides whether to release or withhold Blueprint funds (see Section 4)
Attorney General Anthony G. Brown (D), the named defendant in the litigation over the Maryland Kids Code (see Section 3)
Local control Weak on money. 24 county-level districts with no taxing authority, fiscally dependent on county government, and no school budget referendum anywhere in the state
Scale
School systems 24, one per county plus Baltimore City. There are no sub-county school districts in Maryland
Students / schools 880,231 students as of September 30, 2025, down 1.3 percent in a year, across roughly 1,420 schools. Nonpublic enrollment is 134,955 and falling from its 2023 peak
Regional layer None. No BOCES, Intermediate Unit, or ESC equivalent. With districts averaging about 36,700 students, the county system is itself the scale a regional agency provides elsewhere (see Section 2)
Largest systems Montgomery 156,050, Prince George's 130,629, Baltimore County 107,983, Anne Arundel 84,256, Baltimore City 76,340, Howard 57,031, Frederick 47,884. The smallest is Kent at 1,699
Charters 45 statewide, 27 in Baltimore City. Local boards are the only authorizers, and charter competition is not a meaningful budget pressure in 22 of 24 systems
Chronic absenteeism 25.2 percent statewide in 2024-25, from Frederick at 16.7 percent to Baltimore City at 46.4 percent
Money & Calendar
Funding formula The Blueprint for Maryland's Future formula: a foundation program plus targeted programs for compensatory education, English learners, special education, and concentration of poverty, phasing in to 2032
Budget cycle Annual. State fiscal year July 1. The 90-day session convenes the second Wednesday of January; 2027 runs January 13 to April 12
Current state budget $70.8 billion total signed April 2026, with about $10.1 billion for K-12, up roughly $370 million. Includes $480.5 million for school construction, $572 million for community schools, and $19.4 million to reduce teacher vacancies. No new taxes
The out-year problem The Blueprint Fund is projected to be depleted by FY2028, with a structural deficit projected around $2.8 to $3.1 billion in FY2028 rising toward $4 to $4.5 billion by FY2031
Local approval mechanism County council or commissioners appropriation. No taxing authority, no school budget referendum, no levy election, no town meeting. Maintenance of effort sets a per-pupil floor enforced by the Comptroller (see Section 5)
District budget rhythm Boards submit budgets at least 45 days before the local tax levy date. County executives may deny or reduce major categories; in most counties the council may restore, but in Baltimore City and Baltimore County it cannot without a charter amendment
Quick Yes/No
State AI guidance for schools? YES, and district policies are mandatory. MSDE guidance was issued February 2026 and the Artificial Intelligence Ready Schools Act requires every system to adopt an aligned policy and designate a central-office AI Coordinator (see Section 3)
Cell phone restriction? YES. The Maryland Phone-Free Schools Act, effective July 1, 2026, requires all 24 boards to adopt bell-to-bell policies by 2027-28, with mandatory exemptions in statute. Most large systems moved ahead of the deadline (see Section 3)
Screen time action? YES, aimed at platforms rather than schools. The Maryland Age-Appropriate Design Code Act has been in force since October 1, 2024 and is not enjoined despite an active challenge (see Section 3)
Student data privacy law? YES. The Maryland Student Data Privacy Act at Md. Code Educ. 4-131 governs vendors as operators, with the Maryland Online Data Privacy Act adding a second layer
SDPC / standard NDPA state? No. No Maryland alliance and no standard agreement. Plan for 24 separate district agreements (see Section 6)
1:1 / state device program? No statewide program and no state device fund. Large systems reached 1:1 independently. Note the maintenance of effort rule letting counties treat technology as a nonrecurring cost, which shapes how they prefer to buy (see Section 7)
ESA / voucher program? No ESA. BOOST, a need-based nonpublic scholarship, is level funded at $9 million and served 2,796 students in 2025-26, averaging about $3,400
Collective bargaining Mandatory and strong under Education Article Title 6, enforced by the Public Employee Relations Board. MSEA statewide, BTU in Baltimore City, MCEA in Montgomery

2. What This State Is Driving Right Now

Last verified: August 2026

Maryland in 2026 is a state executing an enormous, dated, legally binding reform plan into a budget that will not support it after FY2028. Every conversation about money in this state eventually becomes a conversation about the Blueprint cliff. Underneath that, technology leaders are carrying two hard statutory deadlines that no one in the superintendent conversation is talking about. The two lists below barely overlap, which is normal and worth planning around.

What superintendents are focused on

  • The Blueprint out-year cliff, and who absorbs it: This appears in every source: the Governor, MSDE, the Accountability and Implementation Board, the superintendents and boards associations, the counties, the union, and the press. The Blueprint Fund is projected to be depleted by FY2028, shifting costs onto the general fund, with analysts projecting a $2.8 to $3.1 billion structural deficit in FY2028 rising toward $4 to $4.5 billion by FY2031. Whoever wins in November inherits that first. See Section 5.

  • The $60,000 salary floor, and what it cost to hit: The Blueprint's minimum teacher salary took effect July 1, 2026 with no waivers available. Twelve systems met it by the deadline and ten more after. The trade-offs are the story: Cecil County cut 104 positions, 60 of them teachers, to reach a $60,086 starting salary, and Dorchester raised class sizes from 18 to 22. In at least a half dozen systems, discretionary budget is functionally gone for FY2027. See Section 9.

  • Answering to two bosses at once: MSDE sets standards and regulation, but the Accountability and Implementation Board holds the money valve: it approves or rejects each system's Blueprint implementation plan, warns by December 1, and makes funding determinations by February 1. All 24 plans are approved. A superintendent's behavior looks over-determined until you understand that a filed, funding-linked plan governs what they can say yes to. See Section 4.

  • An accountability system being rebuilt while they are judged by it: The COMPASS Act, effective July 1, 2026, alters school quality indicators, caps test scores at 70 percent of the composite rating, and authorizes replacing the star rating system. Separately, 2025-26 was the last MCAP administration; a new assessment system starts in 2026-27 with the redesigned rating targeted for spring 2027 pending federal approval. Reported numbers will move for reasons unrelated to instruction. See Sections 7 and 8.

  • County appropriation politics, with a floor that erodes: Districts cannot levy taxes and depend entirely on county appropriation above a maintenance of effort per-pupil floor with no inflation escalator; a 2026 bill to add one failed. County income tax is capped at 3.30 percent and 16 of 24 counties are already at or above 3.20 percent, leaving property tax the only flexible lever. Baltimore City is the sharpest case: it carries the greatest Blueprint local-share exposure in the state and its council cannot even restore executive budget reductions without a charter amendment. A task force chaired by the Comptroller reports December 1, 2026. See Sections 4 and 5.

What technology leaders are focused on

  • A cybersecurity compliance clock running at all 24 systems at once: Senate Bill 601, effective July 1, 2026, requires every system to designate a cybersecurity point of contact, comply with State minimum standards from fiscal 2027, certify compliance by June 30, 2027 and biennially after, and run a biennial maturity assessment. The fiscal note captures the spread: Prince George's anticipated more than $2.0 million in hardware and software upgrades, several systems expect added staff, and one warned compliance could be overly burdensome. The clearest state-created demand signal in Maryland K-12. See Section 7.

  • An AI statute that names a job and governs procurement: The Artificial Intelligence Ready Schools Act requires each county board to designate a central-office AI Coordinator as noninstructional staff, adopt an aligned AI policy, use MSDE's evaluation rubric when selecting AI tools, and procure consistent with state information technology standards. Maryland is one of very few states where an AI rubric and a named coordinator are statutory, which means a defined buying contact and a defined dossier. See Section 3.

  • A new assessment system arriving in 2026-27: The transition off MCAP and the COMPASS Act redesign together mean new delivery, new reporting, and a new rating methodology inside two school years, with federal approval sought early in 2027. For a technology director this is a readiness cycle: device and browser requirements, rostering, and reporting integration all have to be reconfirmed rather than assumed. See Section 7.

  • Phone policy as an operations and reporting problem: The statute mandates policies at all 24 boards by 2027-28, requires submission to MSDE by September 1, 2027, and requires annual impact reports from July 2028 through 2033 disaggregated by student demographics with exemption tracking by type. One mandatory exemption is explicitly conditioned on the unavailability of a school-issued device for translation, which quietly advantages systems that can guarantee 1:1 school-managed hardware. See Section 3.

  • Refresh economics shaped by a maintenance of effort rule: There is no state device fund. What there is instead is a structural incentive: maintenance of effort lets counties exclude nonrecurring costs including computer labs, technology, start-ups, and books from the permanent local funding base. Counties therefore prefer one-time capital over recurring operating expense, which runs against subscription and device-as-a-service models. Name it early rather than discovering it in a negotiation. See Sections 5 and 7.

The regional service agency view

Calibration: there is no regional layer, and in Maryland that is not a gap in the way it is in Virginia. With 24 districts averaging about 36,700 students, the county system is itself the scale a BOCES or Intermediate Unit provides elsewhere. What substitutes for the regional layer commercially is one contracting consortium, MEEC, which every district belongs to.

  • What does not exist: No BOCES, no Intermediate Unit, no ESC, no regional educational service agency. MSDE's only quasi-regional instrument is the Expert Review Teams it deploys with the Accountability and Implementation Board, which is an accountability mechanism rather than a service or purchasing agency, and which was suspended for 2026-27 in the 2026 Blueprint revisions.

  • MEEC is the substitute, and it is complete: The Maryland Education Enterprise Consortium is hosted by the University System of Maryland, covers K-20 and public libraries, and has more than 200 participating institutions. All 24 public school districts are members, a clean sweep and the single most important procurement fact in this profile. Its categories cover hardware, software including learning management systems, and services including IT security and consulting. One caution rides with it: Maryland's minority business enterprise regime is stricter than most states, certification runs only through the state transportation department, and K-12 award decisions name minority contractor utilization as a statutory factor, so the subcontracting plan is part of the bid. See Section 6.

  • The boards association is not a purchasing cooperative: MABE's partnership services are a curated vendor-referral program where districts contract directly with the partner. It is useful and it is not a contracting vehicle. Confusing the two is a common and costly error in this state. The Baltimore Regional Cooperative Purchasing Committee is a county-government cooperative oriented to energy and commodities, not instructional technology.

  • Where the absence genuinely bites: The six smallest systems, Kent at 1,699 students through Caroline at 5,598, have no regional agency to lean on for capacity, and they are precisely the systems that legislative analysts flag as most exposed to Blueprint local-share costs. In a BOCES state those districts would have somewhere to go. In Maryland they do not, which makes them both harder to serve and more genuinely underserved.

3. Hot Topics: The Four Questions of 2026

Last verified: August 2026

Maryland has acted on all four, and unusually it has acted on three of them in a single legislative session. The 2026 session produced an AI statute, a phone statute, and a cybersecurity mandate, all signed within six weeks of each other. That density is the story: Maryland districts are absorbing three new compliance regimes simultaneously in the same school year.

ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE | Mandatory. A statute, a guidance document, a named coordinator role, and a procurement rubric, all live this school year.

  • The law: The Artificial Intelligence Ready Schools Act, Senate Bill 720, became Chapter 634, signed May 26, 2026 and effective June 1, 2026, passing 41-0 and 129-8. MSDE must maintain an online AI guidance platform and publish a rubric and assessment tools districts use to evaluate AI products. It creates the Maryland AI Education Collaborative, which must recommend a standards-update process by January 1, 2027 and reports annually from December 2027, and requires AI literacy in workforce preparation and computer science standards by June 1, 2027.

  • What each district must do: Designate a coordinator on the productive and ethical use of AI, employed at central office as noninstructional staff. Adopt an AI policy aligned with MSDE's guidance within 120 days of its release, which puts policies due in fall 2026. Use MSDE's rubric when selecting AI tools, and procure consistent with state information technology standards. Because the statute sets 120 days from release rather than a calendar date, ask a district which operative date MSDE gave them.

  • The guidance itself: Artificial Intelligence Guidance for Maryland K-12 Schools, with a Local Action Planning Guide, was presented to the State Board on February 24, 2026. It runs across eight domains from human-centered learning and equity through data privacy, academic integrity, professional learning, operational use, and procurement and vendor governance. Two provisions matter commercially: it rejects reliance on unreliable AI-detection tools, and it requires documented human review for high-stakes decisions.

  • What that means on the ground: Tool selection stays local; MSDE sets the guardrails. Districts are visibly moving: Montgomery on AI for teacher support with monitored student use, Prince George's on approved pilots, Frederick with a local AI advisory group, Baltimore County launching at three schools. The practical consequence is that an AI-adjacent offering needs a rubric-mapped privacy, bias, and human-review dossier, and the buying contact is now a defined role rather than a guess.

Source: Senate Bill 720 as enacted | MSDE AI guidance | Fiscal note | Fall deadline coverage

SCREEN TIME | In effect and not enjoined. Aimed at commercial platforms, not at schools, and under active First Amendment challenge.

  • The law: The Maryland Age-Appropriate Design Code Act, commonly the Maryland Kids Code, took effect October 1, 2024. It reaches for-profit entities above revenue or user thresholds offering online products reasonably likely to be accessed by minors under 18. It requires processing personal information consistent with the best interests of the child, bans dark patterns, geolocation tracking, and automated profiling with narrow exceptions, requires privacy-protective defaults, and bans selling or using under-18 data for targeted advertising.

  • The compliance step that arrived in 2026: Data protection impact assessments became required beginning April 2026. Enforcement runs through the Attorney General's Consumer Protection Division, at $2,500 per affected child for a negligent violation and $7,500 for an intentional one, with a cure period.

  • The litigation, and what it did not do: NetChoice sued the Attorney General in federal district court. The court denied the motion to dismiss on November 24, 2025, letting First Amendment, vagueness, and preemption claims proceed, and NetChoice amended in April 2026 to add a Commerce Clause claim. Critically, the court took no action to block the law. It remains in force, and anyone describing the Kids Code as enjoined is wrong.

  • Keep this separate from the phone law: The Kids Code regulates commercial platforms. It imposes no obligation on school systems and does not govern school-issued devices. The phone restriction below is a separate statute with an entirely different compliance path, and Maryland's youth mental health work runs elsewhere again, mainly through the Consortium on Coordinated Community Supports.

Source: Kids Code overview | Order denying the motion to dismiss | Online safety law tracker | MSDE news

CELL PHONES / CONNECTED DEVICES | Statewide, mandatory, bell to bell, with statutory exemptions and a reporting regime running to 2033.

  • The law: House Bill 525, the Joanne C. Benson Maryland Phone-Free Schools Act, became Chapter 596, signed May 26, 2026, effective July 1, 2026, passing 135-1 and 41-0. The state does not itself ban phones: it requires each of the 24 county boards to adopt a restriction policy, leaving storage a local decision. Scope is the academic day between first and last bell, including lunch, recess, and passing periods.

  • The exemptions are mandatory, not optional: Policies may not prohibit use for IEP or 504 accommodations, monitoring a documented health issue, language translation for multilingual students when a school-issued device is unavailable, or principal-approved caregiving. Note the translation clause: it is conditioned on the unavailability of a school device, which structurally favors systems that can guarantee school-managed hardware.

  • The deadlines that create work: Policies must be implemented by 2027-28 and submitted to MSDE by September 1, 2027. MSDE publishes the compiled policies by December 1, 2028, and annual impact reports run from July 2028 through 2033, disaggregated by demographics with exemption tracking by type.

  • Large systems have already moved: Montgomery went full bell to bell for all students in 2026-27, a year ahead of the mandate, with enforcement framed as conference-based rather than punitive. Frederick adopted bell to bell with storage for fall 2026. Baltimore City requires devices off, away, and secured from arrival to dismissal. Howard calls its all-day prohibition the second strictest in the state. Anne Arundel and Baltimore County vary by grade level. The statute governs personal devices; district-issued instructional devices are outside it.

Source: House Bill 525 as enacted | Fiscal note with the exemption list | Legislative coverage | Montgomery implementation

STUDENT DATA PRIVACY | A mature vendor statute, a second consumer layer, a new cybersecurity mandate, and no statewide agreement to sign.

  • The core statute: The Maryland Student Data Privacy Act at Md. Code Educ. 4-131, enacted 2015 and amended since, binds operators, meaning entities providing internet-based services to schools under contract. Operators may not knowingly run targeted advertising on student data, build non-educational profiles, sell student information, or disclose covered information outside enumerated exceptions, and must use reasonable security and delete covered information on a school's request. Covered information is expansive and explicitly includes biometrics, photographs, voice recordings, and persistent identifiers such as cookies and device IDs.

  • The second layer: The Maryland Online Data Privacy Act took effect October 1, 2025. It does not regulate districts, which are government entities, but it independently constrains ed-tech vendors meeting its thresholds and bans selling or using under-18 personal data for targeted advertising where the controller knew or should have known. The age line is 18, not 13, and it stacks on top of 4-131.

  • The new cybersecurity obligation: Senate Bill 601 of 2026, Chapter 35, effective July 1, 2026, requires each local school system to designate a cybersecurity point of contact, comply with State minimum cybersecurity standards, certify compliance by June 30, 2027 and biennially after, and conduct a biennial maturity assessment. It builds on the 2022 Local Cybersecurity Support Act, which already covered local school systems and required incident reporting to three destinations including the State Security Operations Center. Separately, 2026 legislation voids unconscionable vendor terms in school contracts, which is worth a contracting review.

  • What is absent, and it matters: Maryland is not a Student Data Privacy Consortium state. There is no Maryland alliance, no standard agreement, and no Exhibit E style subscription. Signing authority sits with the local superintendent or a designated officer at each of the 24 boards, and the operative document is a district-specific agreement incorporating 4-131. Plan for 24 separate reviews, which is materially more work per district than in Pennsylvania or Virginia even though Maryland has fewer districts.

Source: Md. Code Educ. 4-131 | Maryland Online Data Privacy Act | Senate Bill 601 as enacted | Senate Bill 601 fiscal note

4. Governance & Decision-Making

Last verified: August 2026

Authority flows Governor to the State Board of Education to MSDE and the State Superintendent, then to 24 county-level systems. It is a short chain with one unusual feature: a second oversight body runs alongside it. Understanding the split between MSDE and the Accountability and Implementation Board is the single most useful thing to know about how Maryland districts behave.

  • The State Board appoints the chief, not the Governor: Fourteen members are appointed by the Governor with Senate consent to four-year terms, including a certified teacher selected by an election among Maryland teachers, a parent from a list submitted by the Maryland PTA, and a high school student. Reappointments also require Senate confirmation. The Board, not the Governor, appoints the State Superintendent to a four-year term beginning July 1. Dr. Carey Wright's term runs to June 30, 2028, which means the office does not automatically turn over with the November election.

  • The Accountability and Implementation Board holds the money valve: Created in 2021, codified at Education Article 5-401 through 5-414 with a sunset of June 30, 2032, the Board has seven members on six-year terms and a chair jointly designated by the Governor, Senate President, and House Speaker. It requires all 24 systems to submit Blueprint implementation plans annually and can approve or reject them, and since fiscal 2026 it decides whether to release or withhold Blueprint funds, issuing warnings by December 1 and determinations by February 1. It is not subordinate to MSDE or the State Board; they are peers. The consequence for anyone proposing something to a district: a proposal that maps onto a commitment already in the approved plan is far easier to say yes to.

  • The legislature: The General Assembly meets 90 days from the second Wednesday of January; the 2027 session runs January 13 to April 12. Education policy runs through Senate Education, Energy and the Environment under Brian J. Feldman, and House Ways and Means under Jheanelle K. Wilkins, which holds both education finance and primary and secondary programs. Money runs through Senate Budget and Taxation under Guy Guzzone and House Appropriations under Ben Barnes. Sen. Katie Fry Hester is the most active legislator on K-12 technology and cybersecurity, carrying both the cybersecurity mandate and the AI statute in 2026.

  • Election-year context, provisional note: On November 3, 2026 Maryland elects a Governor and the entire General Assembly, plus three statewide constitutional questions covering funding for arbitrated collective bargaining agreements, judicial disabilities appointments, and state court review of congressional redistricting. Treat the education posture as provisional: the second half of 2026 is a low-decision window in Annapolis, and the 2027 session convening January 13 is where a new or returning administration's approach surfaces. Note that the State Superintendent's term runs past the election regardless of outcome.

  • Fiscal dependence is the structural fact: County boards submit budgets at least 45 days before the local tax levy date. County executives may deny or reduce major budget categories in writing with reasons; in most executive-council counties the council may restore those reductions, but in Baltimore City and Baltimore County the council cannot restore unless voters have approved a charter amendment granting that power. There is no school budget referendum anywhere in Maryland, so the budget audience is a county executive and council rather than an electorate. Two local quirks are worth carrying: in Prince George's the County Executive nominates the superintendent, and in Montgomery the weighted real property tax rate may not exceed the prior year's without a unanimous Council vote, which explains a great deal about how Montgomery school funding fights unfold.

  • Baltimore City is the governance exception: City Schools is governed by a 12-member Board of School Commissioners: nine appointed by the Mayor from a slate vetted by a Community Panel, two elected citywide every four years, and one student commissioner. The Governor no longer appoints members, which retired the joint arrangement dating to the 1997 city-state partnership. A 2026 bill that would have repealed the Community Panel and added Senate confirmation passed the Senate 40-0 and died in the House, so the current structure stands, and the two elected seats are on the November 3, 2026 ballot. The chief executive carries the title CEO: Dr. Jermaine Dawson, named in April 2026 from Philadelphia, succeeding Dr. Sonja Santelises. Enrollment is 76,340. Treat it as a stabilization account: a first-year CEO, the steepest enrollment decline in the state, 46.4 percent chronic absenteeism, a $48 million federal loss, the greatest Blueprint local-share exposure of any jurisdiction, and a council that cannot restore budget cuts.

5. Funding & Budget Rhythm

Last verified: August 2026. FY2027 budget in effect since July 1, 2026

  • How the formula works: The Blueprint for Maryland's Future replaced the prior funding structure with a foundation program plus targeted programs for compensatory education, English learners, special education, and concentration of poverty, phased in on a multi-year schedule to 2032. Enrollment for funding purposes is the greater of prior-year enrollment or a three-year moving average with 2020-21 excluded. The five pillars are early childhood, high-quality teachers and leaders, college and career readiness, resources for students who need more, and governance and accountability.

  • The local approval mechanism, stated explicitly: County council or county commissioners appropriation. School systems have no taxing authority, and Maryland has no school budget referendum, no levy election, and no town meeting. Maintenance of effort at Education Article 5-235 sets the floor: a county must appropriate at least the prior year's highest local appropriation per pupil multiplied by current enrollment, or the local share of major education aid. Because it is a per-pupil floor, enrollment growth raises the obligation and decline lowers it. A county whose education effort falls below the statewide five-year average must raise its per-pupil amount by the lesser of its own wealth growth, statewide wealth growth, or 2.5 percent. Enforcement has teeth: missing it without a waiver means state income tax revenue is withheld and redirected to the county board.

  • The exclusion that shapes technology buying: Counties may exclude certain nonrecurring supplemental costs from the maintenance of effort base, and the statute names computer labs, technology, new program start-ups, and books among them. The consequence is a standing incentive for counties to fund devices and infrastructure as one-time capital rather than as recurring operating expense, because recurring spending permanently raises the floor. This is the most commercially important sentence in Maryland school finance and it is almost never said out loud.

  • The FY2027 budget, and the year after it: The enacted budget signed in April 2026 totals $70.8 billion with roughly $10.1 billion for K-12, up about $370 million and nearly 17 percent since 2023. It carries $480.5 million for school construction, $572 million for community schools, $10.9 million for literacy and math coaches, and $19.4 million to reduce teacher vacancies, and closed a $1.5 billion gap with no new taxes. It did not close the structural problem: the Blueprint Fund is projected to be depleted by FY2028, with structural deficits projected around $2.8 to $3.1 billion in FY2028 and $4 to $4.5 billion by FY2031.

  • What the 2026 session did to the Blueprint: The Blueprint revisions signed May 26, 2026 and effective July 1, 2026 extend the compensatory education enrollment calculation through FY2028, make Concentration of Poverty Grant uses permanent by repealing their termination date, alter initial teacher certificate qualifications, suspend the Expert Review Team program for 2026-27, extend the National Board Certification timeline for principals, extend dual enrollment authority, and require a new poverty methodology by the end of 2026. The prior year's changes had already delayed teacher collaborative time by three years while preserving community schools funding and per-pupil amounts for high-need students.

  • Where the counties actually are: Counties appropriated $8.85 billion for schools in fiscal 2026, at least $1.4 billion above required levels. But county income tax is capped at 3.30 percent and 16 of 24 counties sit at or above 3.20 percent, leaving property tax as the only flexible lever, while special education at roughly $1 billion a year and transportation at roughly $500 million sit largely outside state formulas. County finance officers in August 2026 were describing state cost shifts and warning of a reckoning. The Task Force to Modernize County and Municipal Revenue Structures, chaired by the Comptroller, reports December 1, 2026.

  • Where technology money lives: There is no dedicated state educational technology fund, no state device program, and no school-specific broadband program. Technology rides the district operating budget, the county capital program, federal programs, and E-rate. The one state channel with real dollars is school construction, with specific rules: cabling and pathways inside a project are eligible construction work, but equipment with a median useful life under 15 years is not, which excludes most active network gear, displays, and endpoints. See Section 9.

6. Procurement & Vendor Path

Last verified: August 2026

  • Framework: Maryland local school systems are not governed by the State General Procurement Law in most respects. The controlling provision is Education Article 5-112, requiring competitive bidding when the cost of any building, improvement, supplies, or equipment exceeds $100,000. Its exceptions matter: books and other materials of instruction, emergency repairs, and intergovernmental purchases where the lead agency followed public bidding, which is the explicit statutory basis for piggybacking here. Award goes to the responsible bidder providing best value, considering delivery, competency, minority contractor utilization plans, and price. Best value, not lowest price, which opens the door to evaluated criteria.

  • The operative thresholds are lower than the statute: Every large board is stricter than $100,000. Montgomery requires three quotes from $7,500 and a formal solicitation plus board approval at $25,000, including for cooperative purchases, along with a bridge letter and pricing at or below the original contract. Prince George's requires three written quotes from $15,000 and a formal solicitation at $50,000. For a technology vendor the practical number in the two largest systems is $25,000 and $50,000, not $100,000, and above those you are in a public solicitation and a public board vote.

  • What carries the load in a state with no regional layer: This is where the Section 2 calibration cashes out. With no BOCES, Intermediate Unit, or ESC, there is no aggregation intermediary and no regional gatekeeper. What substitutes is a single consortium, the Maryland Education Enterprise Consortium, hosted by the University System of Maryland, of which all 24 districts are members. Because 5-112's intergovernmental purchase exception exempts a district riding a properly bid contract from its own competitive requirement, a MEEC contract converts a six-month solicitation into a purchase order. National cooperatives are usable under the same exception, but MEEC beats them in practice because it is a Maryland public agency contract that district counsel already recognize.

  • Minority business enterprise requirements are not optional: Maryland's regime is stricter than most states and materially shapes bids. The statewide aspirational goal is 29 percent, certification runs only through the Maryland Department of Transportation, and only MDOT-certified firms count. Education Article 5-112 pulls this into K-12 award decisions by naming minority contractor utilization plans as an award factor. Prince George's is strictest: 29 percent on state-funded projects with 8 percent African American-owned and 11 percent women-owned subgoals, 30 percent on locally funded projects with a 20 percent county-based subgoal, plus a 5 percent evaluation preference for county firms on contracts over $100,000. Montgomery's target is 10 percent. A prime without an MDOT-certified subcontracting plan will lose Prince George's.

  • Registration and advertising: State of Maryland business registration and good standing is a baseline requirement. eMaryland Marketplace Advantage is the state eProcurement system where state, county, school, and university solicitations post public notices, and 5-112 names it as an acceptable advertising venue for county board solicitations, so registration there is functionally mandatory for anyone tracking Maryland K-12 opportunities. Add MEEC vendor status and the district-specific supplier portals that Montgomery and Prince George's each operate.

  • The privacy and contracting layer: There is no statewide data privacy agreement template and no state signatory. Signing authority sits with the local superintendent or a designated officer at each of the 24 boards, and the operative document is a district-specific agreement incorporating the obligations at Education Article 4-131. From 2026 forward, expect two additions to the review: an AI evaluation rubric applied under the Artificial Intelligence Ready Schools Act, and closer scrutiny of vendor terms following 2026 legislation voiding unconscionable contract provisions in school contracts.

7. Technology & Digital Learning Posture

Last verified: August 2026

  • Devices: No statewide 1:1 program and no dedicated state device fund. Maryland's large county systems reached 1:1 independently, largely with local capital and federal relief money, and refresh is now a local operating decision. The state lever is indirect and important: maintenance of effort permits counties to exclude nonrecurring technology spending from the permanent funding base, which pushes counties toward one-time capital purchases and away from recurring subscription models. Federal relief reimbursements were also clawed back in 2025, which froze technology device purchases among other things in several systems.

  • Cybersecurity is the state's clearest technology mandate: The mechanics are in Section 3. What matters here is the operational shape: a designated point of contact, compliance with State minimum standards beginning in fiscal 2027, certification by June 30, 2027 and biennially after, and a biennial maturity assessment, layered on 2022 incident-reporting duties to the local emergency manager, the State Security Operations Center, and the Maryland Joint Operations Center. The Department of Information Technology's February 2026 policy suite is scoped to the executive branch, so districts should confirm which standards they are measured against. This is a dated compliance window running at all 24 systems simultaneously.

  • Assessment, and a transition already underway: MCAP has been delivered on Pearson's TestNav platform, covering English language arts, mathematics, social studies, and end-of-course exams in American Government and Life Science that count for 20 percent of the final course grade. Maryland has no exit exam; graduation is set by county board policy and State Board regulation. The 2025-26 administration was the last under MCAP, with a new assessment system from 2026-27 and a redesigned rating system targeted for spring 2027 pending federal approval. July 2026 results showed 51.7 percent proficient in English language arts and 27.1 percent in mathematics, with gains under one point and persistent gaps, including 14.6 percent for students with disabilities, unchanged.

  • Statewide platforms: Maryland does run a statewide learning management system: MSDE eConnect is a Canvas instance operated by the Digital Learning and School Library Media Branch and used for student and educator online programs, professional learning, and MSDE-published content, including its AI resources for educators. The same branch publishes Digital Learning Standards for both students and educators. The Maryland Longitudinal Data System Center links K-12, postsecondary, and workforce data and maintains a standing Cybersecurity Subcommittee, which is itself a signal of posture.

  • Broadband: The Office of Statewide Broadband sits in the Department of Housing and Community Development and runs the Connect Maryland initiative with grant programs and a digital equity coalition. There is no school-specific state connectivity program, so school connectivity is effectively an E-rate and local matter.

  • AI capacity building: MSDE publishes educator AI resources inside its Canvas instance and the Artificial Intelligence Ready Schools Act funds three new state positions and a $500,000 contingent appropriation. The practitioner conversation, though, is happening at MEEC: its 2026 member conference keynote was an AI panel and its session slate paired AI adoption with cybersecurity modernization, accessibility compliance, disaster recovery, and data governance. That pairing tells you where Maryland's technology leadership locates AI, which is as a governance and risk question first.

  • Framing: Maryland frames educational technology primarily as a compliance and infrastructure domain, secondarily as instructional delivery, and barely at all as an equity or wellbeing agenda. The evidence is consistent: the 2026 technology legislation is a cybersecurity mandate with certification deadlines and an AI statute with a procurement rubric; MSDE's technology unit is housed with library media and produces standards and a platform rather than a funded program; there is no device fund and no school broadband program; and the most important technology institution in the state is a procurement consortium hosted by a university system. The translation: leading with instructional transformation will be received politely and deprioritized. Leading with the June 30, 2027 certification obligation, total cost against a per-pupil floor, and a MEEC contract vehicle matches how the state organizes decisions.

Sources: MCAP results | Senate Bill 601 as enacted | Digital Learning and School Library Media Branch | Maryland Longitudinal Data System

8. Instructional Priorities & Mandates

Last verified: August 2026

  • Literacy is the most prescriptive instructional mandate in the state: The statutory floor is the Ready to Read Act of 2019, but the operative instrument is the Comprehensive PreK-3 Literacy Policy adopted by the State Board in October 2024, phased so districts feel a new obligation each year. In 2025-26 systems began administering MSDE-approved universal screeners three times a year. In 2026-27 Student Reading Improvement Plans are required within 30 days of identification, developed with parents. In 2027-28 a grade 3 retention provision takes effect, with good-cause exemptions and mandatory parent consent. Instruction must align to the science of reading and must not include three-cueing or leveled readers. Note the mechanism: State Board policy operationalizing statute, not standalone legislation, which makes it faster to change than a law.

  • Mathematics is the visible weakness without a comparable policy: Statewide proficiency sits at 27.1 percent, with elementary math at 35.8 percent and secondary far lower. The state's response has been instructional coaching, tools, and resources rather than a named policy parallel to the literacy framework. The literacy playbook exists and mathematics has not yet received it, which is a live opening in a district conversation.

  • The career ladder is where the money and the mandate coincide: Four levels, with Level 3 requiring National Board Certification or a master's degree and paying $10,000 more, or $17,000 in low-performing schools, and Level 4 adding $5,000 to $15,000 by role. All 24 systems have approved ladders through Level 4, and Maryland is first nationally in new National Board Certifications, up more than 400 percent from baseline. The demand this creates is for coaching, observation, micro-credentialing, professional development tracking, and evidence portfolios.

  • College and career readiness by the end of grade 10: Students must meet a college and career readiness standard in English language arts, mathematics, and science by the end of grade 10, with first data expected in fall 2026. Those who meet it access post-CCR pathways at no cost, including dual enrollment, industry credentials, and competitive college preparation; those who do not enter a support pathway in grades 11 and 12. Districts have operated both since 2023.

  • Computer science: the requirement is to offer, not to require: Education Article 4-111.4 requires each county board to ensure every public high school offers at least one high-quality computer science course, effective from 2021-22, and to work toward incorporating it in elementary and middle schools and increasing enrollment among underrepresented groups. It is an offering mandate, not a graduation requirement. The Artificial Intelligence Ready Schools Act layers on top, requiring AI literacy in workforce preparation and computer science standards by June 1, 2027.

  • CTE with a hard statutory target: Education Article 21-204 requires the state to reach a goal of 45 percent of high school students completing the high school level of a registered apprenticeship or another industry-recognized occupational credential before graduation by the 2030-31 school year, with intermediate goals each year and annual progress reports. That is a specific, dated, measurable number, and it is the most natural technology adjacency in the state that is not about devices: IT and cyber pathways, industry credentials, and work-based learning.

  • Community schools and prekindergarten, briefly: More than half of Maryland's schools are community schools, funded at $572 million in FY2027 through concentration of poverty grants paying for a coordinator and a health practitioner plus a sliding per-pupil grant. Prekindergarten is free full-day for families at or below 300 percent of the federal poverty level through a mixed model where the private provider share rises to at least 50 percent from 2026-27, which means a district's prekindergarten seats are partly not its own classrooms and complicates any districtwide platform or device deployment at that level.

Sources: PreK-3 Literacy Policy | Md. Code Educ. 4-111.4 on computer science | Md. Code Educ. 21-204 on CTE goals | Blueprint college and career readiness

9. Landscape & Pressures

Last verified: August 2026

  • Enrollment is roughly flat statewide and sharply divergent underneath: 880,231 students as of September 30, 2025, down 1.3 percent in a year. The divergence is what matters: Frederick, Charles, and Harford are growing while Baltimore City and several Eastern Shore and Western Maryland systems shrink. Because maintenance of effort is a per-pupil floor, declining-enrollment counties see their required dollar appropriation fall automatically, compounding fixed-cost problems in exactly the small systems least able to absorb them. Nonpublic enrollment of 134,955 is declining from its 2023 peak.

  • The teacher workforce improved sharply, and the salary floor was expensive: Statewide vacancies fell to 886.2 in 2025-26 from 1,619.0, a 45.3 percent decrease, with attrition down to 14.9 percent from 17.6 percent. Do not open with a shortage narrative; it is out of date. What is current is the cost of the $60,000 floor: no waivers were available, twelve systems met it by the July 1 deadline and ten more after, several by cutting positions and raising class sizes.

  • Facilities, where the state money is and what it will and will not buy: The Interagency Commission on School Construction assesses nearly 1,400 schools, with an average facility condition index of 52.26 percent, meaning useful life about halfway depleted. The Built to Learn Act authorizes up to $2.2 billion in revenue bonds, $1.131 billion issued, supporting 52 projects worth $3.608 billion, and Baltimore City runs the $1.1 billion 21st Century School Buildings Program. On technology, state regulation makes E-rate eligible special construction broadband infrastructure eligible and treats IT equipment as eligible only if it meets a 15-year median useful life test, which cabling and pathways can satisfy and switches, access points, displays, and endpoints generally cannot. Get that distinction right in front of a facilities director.

  • School choice is not the pressure it is elsewhere: Maryland has 45 charter schools statewide, 27 of them in Baltimore City, and local boards are the only authorizers, with no independent or statewide authorizer. Against 880,231 students, charter competition is not a meaningful budget pressure in 22 of 24 systems. BOOST, the nonpublic scholarship program, is level funded at $9 million and served 2,796 students in 2025-26. Maryland superintendents are not defending market share; they are managing a compliance and capacity load, and that changes what they will listen to.

  • Federal exposure runs through the county, not through Title I: Maryland's revenue base is unusually sensitive to federal employment and contracting, so the transmission mechanism into a school budget is volatile county income tax rather than a direct formula cut. Federal relief reimbursements were rescinded in 2025, hitting the largest systems hardest and freezing mental health clinicians, technology devices, building maintenance, and educator training. Baltimore City cut programs after losing $48 million, and county leaders in August 2026 were describing state cost shifts alongside softening revenue.

11. Key Notes for Engagement

Last verified: August 2026. WORKING DRAFT for Jeremiah's edit. Research-generated engagement judgment, not settled advice

How to think about Maryland right now:

  • Every money conversation ends at FY2028, so start there: The Blueprint Fund is projected to be depleted by FY2028 with a structural deficit around $3 billion behind it, and superintendents, boards, county executives, and the union are all planning against the same cliff. A conversation that acknowledges it and talks about total cost of ownership across it will land. One that assumes FY2027 conditions persist will not.

  • There are two hard dated obligations, and they are the openings: Cybersecurity certification is due June 30, 2027 at all 24 systems, and district AI policies aligned to MSDE guidance are due this fall along with a named central-office AI Coordinator. These are the only state-created, calendar-bound technology demands in Maryland. Helping a district think through either is educator-first work that sits exactly where the mandate is.

  • MEEC is the single highest-leverage contracting action in Maryland: All 24 districts are members, and Education Article 5-112's intergovernmental purchase exception means riding a MEEC contract exempts a district from its own competitive bid requirement, turning a six-month solicitation into a purchase order. A national cooperative award does not carry the same weight with district counsel here.

  • Understand the maintenance of effort exclusion before proposing a subscription: Counties can exclude nonrecurring technology spending from the permanent funding base but not recurring spending, which gives every county a structural reason to prefer one-time capital over subscriptions and device-as-a-service. Naming that mechanic yourself, and structuring around it, is a credibility marker with a CFO.

  • Build the minority business enterprise plan before the bid, not during it: Certification runs exclusively through the Maryland Department of Transportation, the statewide goal is 29 percent, and Prince George's layers county subgoals and an evaluation preference on top. Education Article 5-112 makes minority contractor utilization an award factor in K-12 specifically.

  • Respect the second boss: Anything proposed to a Maryland district gets measured against its approved Blueprint implementation plan, because the Accountability and Implementation Board can withhold money over it. Ask which pillar commitment a proposal supports. If it maps to the plan, the district can say yes. If not, it competes against a mandate, and mandates win.

  • Two Marylands, and the smaller one is genuinely underserved: Six systems with fewer than 6,000 students have no regional agency to lean on and are the ones analysts flag as most exposed to Blueprint local-share costs. At the other end, Montgomery, Prince George's, Baltimore County, and Baltimore City each run full CIO organizations and their own procurement offices. A two-track presence, a large-system anchor plus a real partnership on the Eastern Shore or in Western Maryland, matches where the need is.

  • Baltimore City is a stabilization account, and worth the patience: A first-year CEO, a governance structure that just survived an attempted overhaul, elected board seats on the November ballot, the steepest enrollment decline in the state, 46.4 percent chronic absenteeism, and a council that cannot restore budget cuts. It is also the jurisdiction with the largest facilities program and the deepest need. Approach as a multi-year partnership rather than a transaction.
