1. Snapshot

Last verified: August 16, 2026

Leadership & Governance
Governor Abigail Spanberger (D), sworn in January 17, 2026. Virginia governors serve a single four-year term and cannot succeed themselves, so this administration runs to January 2030
Secretary of Education Dr. Jeffery Smith. Former Hampton City Schools superintendent, 2015 to 2023. Oversees early childhood through higher education. A second deputy secretary was added in July 2026 (see Section 5)
Superintendent of Public Instruction Jenna Conway, announced January 13, 2026. Previously VDOE Chief of Early Learning and Specialized Populations. Verified in seat August 16, 2026
State chief selection Appointed by the Governor. Virginia is one of the states where the executive controls the education agency directly
State Board of Education 9 members, all appointed by the Governor. President Mashea Ashton, elected January 8, 2026. Spanberger appointed four members on March 18, 2026. Sets the Standards of Quality prescriptions, SOL standards, and accreditation
Attorney General Jay Jones (D), sworn in January 17, 2026. Relevant here because his office is defending the social media law on appeal, now captioned NetChoice v. Jones (see Section 4)
Local control Strong on policy, weak on money. 131 school divisions with elected or appointed boards, but divisions have no independent taxing authority and are fiscally dependent on city or county government
Scale
School divisions 131 divisions, plus regional Governor's Schools and regional CTE and special education programs
Students / schools 1.209 million students in fall 2025, down 1.2% in one year. Roughly 1,800 public schools. Projected to fall to 1.172 million by fall 2030
Regional layer Eight Superintendent's Regions. These are VDOE administrative and convening geography, not service agencies. Virginia has no BOCES or Intermediate Unit equivalent with purchasing power (see Section 2)
Largest divisions Fairfax County (~178K), Prince William, Loudoun, Virginia Beach, Chesterfield, Henrico. Fairfax alone is roughly 15% of statewide enrollment
Money & Calendar
Funding formula Standards of Quality (SOQ): staffing-ratio based, with the state and local split set by each division's Local Composite Index. Redesign funded in the 2026-2028 budget and a contractor RFP directed, though no RFP had been issued as of August 16, 2026 (see Section 3)
Budget cycle Biennial, even years. State fiscal year July 1 to June 30. The 2026-2028 biennial budget passed June 29, 2026 after a months-long impasse and took effect July 1, 2026
Current state budget Roughly $2 billion in new K-12 money over the biennium: the state share of 4% salary increases in each year, $449.8 million for rebenchmarking, $148.4 million for the special education add-on, school construction grants reported at $519 million, and $2 million for the AI pilot
Legislative session Annual, convening the second Wednesday of January. 60 days in even years, 45 days in odd years. The 2027 short session convenes January 13, 2027
Local approval mechanism City council or county board of supervisors appropriation. Divisions cannot levy taxes and there is no public vote on the school budget itself. New in 2026: any locality may hold a referendum on a 1% local sales tax dedicated to school capital (see Sections 3 and 6)
District budget rhythm Division budgets built January to March, adopted April to May, local appropriations final by roughly June 30. In 2026 that ran ahead of the state budget by three months
Quick Yes/No
State AI guidance for schools? YES, and now mandatory. 2024 guidelines remain the only published state framework. SB394 and HB1186 require new statewide VDOE guidance and aligned division policies, effective July 1, 2026, but set no publication deadline, and the guidance was not out as of August 16, 2026 (see Sections 3 and 4)
Cell phone restriction? YES. Statewide bell-to-bell restriction in statute, all grades, including lunch and passing periods. SB108, effective July 1, 2026 (see Section 4)
Screen time action? YES, but blocked. SB854 limits under-16 users to one hour per platform per day. Enjoined February 27, 2026; the Fourth Circuit denied a stay April 10, 2026 and a renewed motion was pending (see Section 4)
Student data privacy law? YES. A SOPIPA-style vendor statute at Va. Code 22.1-289.01, plus required division data security plans under 22.1-20.2 and a VDOE chief data security officer (see Section 4)
SDPC / standard NDPA state? YES. The Virginia Student Privacy Alliance runs the Student Data Privacy Consortium model. Divisions expect the Virginia standard DPA, extended to other divisions by Exhibit E
1:1 / state device program? No statewide mandate and no state device fund. Most divisions are 1:1 post-pandemic. The VPSA educational technology notes are the only recurring state technology funding stream (see Section 6)
ESA / voucher program? No private-school ESA. A limited tax-credit scholarship exists (Education Improvement Scholarships Tax Credits). Choice pressure on division budgets is materially lower than in neighboring states
Collective bargaining Permitted since 2021, but only where the local board affirmatively opts in by resolution. Active in several Northern Virginia and urban divisions, absent in most of the state

2. Where the Focus Is

Last verified: August 16, 2026

Virginia in 2026 is a state absorbing a full change of administration on top of four simultaneous rebuilds: school capital, accountability and assessment together, the funding formula, and AI policy. The two lists below are ranked by attention consumed rather than by dollar size, and they overlap on almost nothing, which is normal and worth planning around.

What superintendents are focused on

  • Whether the November 3 referendum passes, and what the capital plan looks like if it does not: More than 40 localities placed a 1% school capital sales tax on the November 3, 2026 ballot before the August 14 deadline. This is the largest change to school capital capacity in a generation, and it arrives in the same year the state suspended its own Literary Fund construction loans. Boards and supervisors are running public education campaigns now. See Section 3.

  • Ratings arriving this fall from a system still being rebuilt: Second-year School Performance and Support Framework results land in fall 2026 under a changed growth methodology and, pending final Board review on August 26 and 27, renamed performance categories. SOL cut scores begin a four-year climb this school year, and the Board rejected its own department's proposal to slow that climb. Divisions are being judged by a system that is changing while it judges them. See Section 3.

  • A budget that arrived three months after theirs did: The 2026-2028 biennial budget was not adopted until June 29, 2026, after an impasse over data center tax treatment that came close to a partial state shutdown. Divisions built and adopted their own budgets January through May without knowing state numbers. The result was good on paper, roughly $2 billion in new money, but the 4% raises require a local match many localities had already budgeted past. See Section 6.

  • Enrollment decline, and the two Virginias it is producing: Membership fell to 1.209 million in fall 2025 and is projected to reach 1.172 million by fall 2030, with growth in exurban Northern Virginia and the Richmond metro against decline in Southwest and Southside. No state initiative sits behind this one, which is exactly why it consumes superintendents privately rather than publicly. See Section 10.

  • Federal exposure, budgeted defensively: Title funds and IDEA continuity remain the stated worry of the superintendents association, and the state budget carries a Federal Uncertainty Contingency Fund. Division CFOs are holding reserves rather than committing them, though Virginia's new Ed-Flex authority gives them more room than most states to move federal money. See Sections 6 and 10.

What technology leaders are focused on

  • The SOL assessment system is being competitively rebid for the first time since 2005: VDOE issued RFP 124939 on July 27, 2026 to replace the vendor for all summative SOL assessments, with proposals due in mid-September 2026 and a new system targeted for 2027-28. For a technology director this means new device specifications, a new secure browser, new rostering and integration work, and a readiness cycle landing inside the next two budget years. Nothing else on this list has that blast radius. See Sections 3 and 8.

  • Writing AI policy against guidance that does not exist yet, with the DPA as the only gate that does: Every division must adopt an AI policy consistent with forthcoming VDOE guidance under SB394 and HB1186, effective July 1, 2026. The guidance had not been published as of August 16, 2026 and no pilot application window had opened, so tool vetting is running through the Virginia standard data privacy agreement because that is the only instrument in place. See Sections 3, 4, and 7.

  • Refresh economics with no state device money, and a new capital stream that cannot buy devices: ESSER-funded fleets bought in 2020 and 2021 are five to six years old and out of warranty. There is no state 1:1 program and no refresh fund, the new 1% sales tax is restricted to construction and renovation, and the only recurring state technology money is the VPSA educational technology notes. Refresh therefore competes inside the local capital plan against roofs and buses. See Sections 6 and 8.

  • Statewide platform continuity after the Canvas breach: Canvas is the statewide LMS and GoOpenVA the state OER repository. VDOE's technology page still shows funding secured through June 30, 2026, a date now passed, while the 2026-27 professional learning calendar continues to feature both. Instructure disclosed a breach affecting names, email addresses, student ID numbers, and user messages between April 25 and May 12, 2026, and Roanoke City is reconsidering the platform. See Section 8.

  • Cybersecurity capacity as the federal grant runs out: Hanover County lost divisionwide connectivity across 24 schools in March 2026 and prepared for at least a week without technology. Va. Code 22.1-20.2 gives divisions a model plan and an advisory office rather than a mandate with an audit, and the State and Local Cybersecurity Grant Program was reauthorized in February 2026 without new appropriation and lapses September 30, 2026. See Section 8.

The regional service agency layer

Virginia has no regional service agency with purchasing power, so this profile carries no third lens. What fills the role commercially is a combination rather than an institution: eVA registration, VITA statewide contracts, and national cooperatives carry purchasing; the Virginia Student Privacy Alliance carries the data privacy agreement; and the superintendents association carries the convening and professional learning a BOCES or an Intermediate Unit would run elsewhere. In Northern Virginia, Fairfax County's cooperative solicitations are the closest thing to a regional vehicle. Sections 7 and 11 carry the detail.

3. What the State Is Driving

Last verified: August 16, 2026

These are the initiatives the Governor, the Board of Education, VDOE, and the General Assembly are actively pushing, ranked by how much they change what a division actually does. Three of the five are behind schedule, contested, or unfunded in some material way, and saying so is not a criticism of the state; it is what divisions are experiencing.

SCHOOL CONSTRUCTION AND THE CAPITAL SQUEEZE | The largest change to school capital in a generation, on the ballot in more than 40 localities, while the state's own loan program sits switched off.

  • The scale of the problem: The Commission on School Construction and Modernization put replacement cost for Virginia's aging buildings at close to $25 billion in 2021, with roughly half at least 50 years old, 22% not ADA compliant, 40% at or above capacity, and 5% short of broadband capacity. No current published source establishes how fast that backlog grows each year, and HB544 requires a refreshed statewide estimate, so November 2026 is when that number becomes knowable.

  • The new local instrument, and its limits: The 2026-2028 budget amended Va. Code 58.1-605.1 so that any county or city, not just the nine previously authorized, may hold a referendum on an additional local sales tax of up to 1% for school capital. More than 40 localities acted before the August 14, 2026 deadline, per reporting as of August 7; no certified statewide count had been published as of August 16. Revenue must fund new construction or major renovation including related financing costs, cannot repay debt on projects begun or finished before the tax, sunsets no later than 20 years out, and sits in a locality fund rather than a division fund. Planning District 8 localities may also direct it to transit.

  • The track record, stated accurately: Nine localities held these referenda before 2026 and all nine now levy the tax, but not every one passed first time: Pittsylvania's failed by 23 votes in 2021 before passing in 2022. Together the nine have raised about $119 million, Danville roughly $30 million in three years and Henry County nearly $28 million since 2020. Yields track the retail base, so Danville generates far more per student than adjacent Pittsylvania, which is the equity argument to expect.

  • What the state is adding, and what it quietly removed: The Governor's office puts construction grants at $519 million while the House conference summary shows $347 million for the grant program, so confirm which figure a division is planning against. The FY26 construction grant round closed July 27, 2026 with $209.3 million available and awards pending. In the same budget the Literary Fund loan program was suspended and $172 million redirected to teacher retirement, closing the state's low-interest loan channel. HB544 made the Commission permanent and set two deadlines: a VDOE statewide facility inventory by September 1, 2026 and a 10-year capital roadmap by November 1, 2026.

Source: Va. Code 58.1-605.1 | Cardinal News referendum coverage | VDOE construction assistance | VDOE Literary Fund loans | HB544

ACCOUNTABILITY AND ASSESSMENT, REBUILT TOGETHER | Contested inside the state itself. In June the Board of Education voted down its own department's proposal to slow the climb in proficiency standards, 7 to 0 with one abstention.

  • Why these are now one initiative: 2026 legislation requires accountability and testing to be reevaluated together rather than separately. VDOE cited that requirement, JLARC's December 2025 review, and listening tour findings of confusion about too many changes at once when it proposed slowing the cut score phase-in. The Board rejected the proposal on June 25, 2026.

  • The framework in year two: The School Performance and Support Framework replaced accreditation as the performance system. First results, published December 10, 2025 on 2024-25 data, rated 425 schools Distinguished, 774 On Track, 399 Off Track, and 213 Needs Intensive Support out of 1,811. Accreditation, now a separate compliance measure, ran 94% fully accredited with 113 schools conditional on documentation grounds rather than operational failure. JLARC called the framework an improvement and recommended renaming the labels, setting thresholds by school level, and capping the readiness indicator; the Board took the rename and the growth methodology first, with final review August 26 and 27, 2026.

  • The bar rises this school year: Higher cut scores in grades 3-8 reading and mathematics and in four end-of-course tests begin a four-year phase-in in 2026-27, benchmarked to NAEP, with a temporary performance level through 2028-29. Neither VDOE nor the Board has published a projected size of the resulting drop, so divisions should expect a visible decline in spring 2027 reporting that reflects the scale rather than instruction. Growth measurement returns to value tables for 2025-26 under ESSA Amendment IX, approved June 25, 2026.

  • The measuring instrument is being replaced too: RFP 124939, issued July 27, 2026, is the first competitive procurement of the SOL assessment vendor since 2005. The current contract runs about $46.5 million a year against an estimated $30 million to $38 million for the replacement, with proposals due mid-September 2026 and operation targeted for 2027-28. Separately, 2025 legislation effective 2026-27 makes SOLs worth 10% of a final course grade, moves testing to the last two weeks of the year, and converts the scale from 600 points to 100. The Executive Order 4 after-action report on framework year one had not been published as of August 16, 2026. Section 8 carries the readiness detail.

Source: VDOE assessment RFP | School Performance and Support Framework | JLARC accountability review | VDOE cut scores | June 2026 Board summary

THE SOQ FUNDING FORMULA REDESIGN | Funded at last, and still without a deadline, a contractor, or a scheduled meeting.

  • What JLARC found, and why it still governs the argument: The 2023 JLARC review found divisions in other states receive about 14% more per student than Virginia divisions, roughly $1,900. The formula indicated about $10.7 billion was needed in FY21 while divisions spent $17.3 billion, and it recognized 113,500 positions against 171,400 employed. Depending on the benchmark, divisions were estimated to need 6% to more than 30% more. JLARC did not recommend scrapping the Local Composite Index; its criticism was the swings between biennia and the imprecision against modern revenue capacity methods.

  • What the budget actually did: Item 123 of the 2026-2028 budget provides $1 million in FY2027 and $250,000 in FY2028 for the Joint Subcommittee on Elementary and Secondary Education Funding and directs the Superintendent of Public Instruction to issue an RFP for a contractor to develop a new formula, subject to approval by a majority of House members and a majority of Senate members of the subcommittee. A further $300,000 a year supports VDOE finance staffing. The paragraph sets no report deadline and no delivery date.

  • Why it is behind, in plain terms: The equivalent 2025 appropriation was line-item vetoed in March 2025 and not overridden. The Joint Subcommittee has not met publicly since December 9, 2024, and the paragraph that created it still carries a November 1, 2024 reporting deadline. No RFP had appeared as of August 16, 2026, and the approval gate cannot clear until the subcommittee meets. The funding profile, front-loaded in FY2027, points at the 2028 session and the 2028-2030 budget.

  • What is moving instead: The legislature is reforming the formula incrementally rather than waiting: $449.8 million for rebenchmarking, the support cap funded to the prevailing ratio of 27.89 positions per 1,000 students, $148.4 million for the special education add-on plus $10 million for intensive support needs, and an at-risk increase with a $98.4 million one-time supplement. The superintendents association is asking for technical fixes to the existing SOQ rather than a new architecture: a division average rather than a linear weighted average, central office positions inside the formula, and a three-year average Local Composite Index.

Source: Budget Item 123 | JLARC K-12 funding formula study | Joint Subcommittee materials | Direct Aid Item 125

THE VIRGINIA LITERACY ACT AT FULL STRETCH | The one initiative the new administration has promised not to change, now reaching grade 8 at roughly half the staffing intensity it has in the early grades.

  • What it requires: The Virginia Literacy Act sits inside the Standards of Quality at Va. Code 22.1-253.13:1 and requires science-based reading instruction K-8, VDOE-approved core and intervention programs, a state literacy screener, and a Student Reading Plan developed and overseen by a reading specialist for every student with a substantial deficiency. K-3 phased in during 2024-25 and grades 4-8 during 2025-26.

  • What the screener actually shows: On the fall 2025 VALLSS screener, the share of K-3 students at low risk ran 36.5% in kindergarten, 45.7% in grade 1, 40.3% in grade 2, and 31.4% in grade 3, and grade 3 was the only grade where high risk rose year over year. In grades 4-8 the screener reaches only students who fell short on the reading SOL, about 157,756 at mid-year, roughly half well below on both spelling and word reading, with gains concentrated in grades 4 and 5 and thinnest in grade 8.

  • Where the money did not follow the mandate: The middle grades reading specialist standard is one per 1,100 students against one per 550 in K-3, and the budget language that funded it removed the flexibility to count reading coaches toward the ratio. The grades 6-8 coaching and screener build ran on one-time federal relief money that has since expired. The 2026-2028 budget adds permission to spend early reading intervention money on specialists rather than a new literacy line, and that appropriation declines from $49.3 million in FY2027 to $49.0 million in FY2028.

  • Where it stands politically: Superintendent Conway, whose background is early learning, told an April 2026 listening session that the state will stay the course on literacy. Executive Order 4 created a 24-member advisory work group, first met April 16, 2026 and running through December 31, 2027, focused on middle grades reading plans and the VAConnects data platform; it had published no recommendations as of August 16, 2026. Published criticism from teachers concentrates on the pace and depth of training and on scripted materials, not on the reading science.

Source: VDOE Virginia Literacy Act | VALLSS screener reports | Executive Order Four | Direct Aid Item 125

THE AI MANDATE AND THE $2 MILLION PILOT | In force since July 1, 2026, and waiting on guidance the statute never required by any date. Section 4 carries the statutory mechanics.

  • Why the state pushed it: Virginia was an early mover, with 2024 guidelines under Executive Order 30 emphasizing responsible use, teacher training, and AI literacy. Those guidelines are advisory, remain the only published state framework, and do not meet the checklist the new statute sets. The 2026 legislation passed with near unanimity, 95-0 in the House and 38-1 in the Senate on the conference reports, and its patrons framed it around protecting student data from commercial reuse rather than around restricting classroom practice.

  • What it means locally right now: Divisions must adopt policies consistent with guidance that does not yet exist, so the duty is real and its trigger has not fired. The large divisions are not waiting. Loudoun adopted a generative AI policy in June 2025. Fairfax acted on July 16, 2026, banning generative AI use by elementary students, applying guardrails rather than a ban in secondary, and ordering an inventory of every generative AI tool on division devices by January 14, 2027. Richmond took an AI systems policy through second read in May 2026, and Chesterfield and Virginia Beach are drafting. Each will face a conformance review when the state guidance lands.

  • The pilot, and who it is aimed at: The AIS Innovation in Education Pilot Program is funded at $2 million for instruction, tutoring, student engagement, operational efficiency, and teacher support, with the statute directing VDOE to prioritize high-poverty, rural, and under-resourced divisions and conditioning participation on educator training and an evaluation plan. No application window had opened as of August 16, 2026, and the first report to the education committees is due December 1, 2026, which is what will shape the 2027 session.

  • Where capacity building is actually happening: It is associational rather than governmental. The superintendents association ran AI Part II: Into the Classroom for all divisions through June 30, 2026, when the materials transferred to the association and VDOE. The state technology association runs regional AI in Action summits from August 26 through October 8, 2026. The most developed AI professional learning asset in Virginia sits with the associations, not the agency.

Source: Va. Code 22.1-20.2:1 | SB394 bill text | Williams Mullen summary | VASS AI Part II | VSTE AI in Action

4. The Four National Questions

Last verified: August 16, 2026

These are the four questions every state is answering, and the point of this section is comparability. Where a topic is also a state initiative, which artificial intelligence is, Section 3 explains why the state pushed it and what divisions are doing meanwhile, and this section carries the statute, dates, scope, and compliance obligations. Virginia has acted on all four within two years, and the pattern is distinctive: broad bipartisan majorities on the school-facing measures and a courtroom loss on the platform-facing one.

ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE | In statute since July 1, 2026. The guidance divisions must align to is not out, and the statute sets no deadline for it. Section 3 carries why the state pushed it.

  • The statute: Va. Code 22.1-20.2:1, created by HB1186 (Rasoul) and SB394 (Pekarsky), 2026 Acts of Assembly chapters 937 and 943, signed April 13, 2026 and effective July 1, 2026. VDOE must compile information on current AI use in public schools and publish guidance for safe, ethical, and equitable use in instructional settings. Each school board must then establish, implement, and enforce policies consistent with that guidance.

  • What the guidance must cover: Student data privacy including FERPA and COPPA compliance, teacher training, transparency about instructional AI use, data privacy agreements prohibiting use of student data to train external models, bias protocols and limits on AI in high-stakes decisions, accessibility, alignment to the Standards of Learning, guardrails favoring division-managed platforms in division-managed environments, and educator access to student AI interaction transcripts.

  • The dates that exist, and the one that does not: The only dates in the section are an annual December 1 report to the House Education and Senate Education and Health committees and a July 1, 2030 expiration of the pilot. There is no publication deadline for the guidance and no adoption deadline for school boards, because the local duty is triggered by the guidance existing. The duty sits with the Department rather than the Board of Education, so no Board vote is required to publish. From the drafting history: a House version assigned the guidance to the Board and barred requiring a student to interact with a chatbot to complete an assignment, and neither survived conference.

  • What did not pass, and what returns: Most Virginia AI legislation was carried to 2027, including companion bills on chatbots and minors and a broad AI accountability act. Expect those back in January 2027 alongside the first pilot report, which lands just before session.

Source: Va. Code 22.1-20.2:1 | SB394 bill text | Williams Mullen summary | VDOE Technology in Education

SCREEN TIME | First-in-nation usage-limit law, enjoined since February 2026, unenforceable today, and before a Fourth Circuit that has already refused one stay.

  • The law: SB854, enacted in 2025 and effective January 1, 2026, amended the Consumer Data Protection Act at Va. Code 59.1-577.1 to require social media platforms to determine whether a user is under 16 and to default those accounts to one hour per day per platform. A parent can raise or lower the cap through verifiable consent. Penalties run to $7,000 per violation.

  • Where the litigation actually stands: NetChoice sued November 17, 2025. Judge Patricia Tolliver Giles of the Eastern District of Virginia granted a preliminary injunction on February 27, 2026, finding the law likely fails First Amendment scrutiny. Virginia appealed March 3, 2026, and the Fourth Circuit denied a stay on April 10, 2026 on the procedural ground that the Commonwealth had not first asked the district court. Briefing closed in mid-May, a renewed stay motion was filed in July 2026, and no ruling had issued as of August 16. The case is captioned NetChoice v. Jones, No. 26-1252, and does not appear on the September 2026 argument calendar, which points to the October or December session. The law remains on the books and unenforceable.

  • The companion measures that did survive: HB1486 (Rasoul) and SB568 (Sturtevant), signed in April 2026, require instruction on the addictive potential of electronic devices, which is a curriculum obligation rather than a platform one. SB245 bars schools from using social media as their only channel for communicating with students about extracurricular activities.

  • Why divisions still care: This is a platform-side law and creates no division compliance work. It matters because it fixes where Virginia politics sit, with bipartisan appetite for limiting youth screen exposure framed around mental health, and because it is the frame parents bring to conversations about school devices.

Source: Va. Code 59.1-577.1 | NetChoice case page | Fourth Circuit docket

CELL PHONES / CONNECTED DEVICES | Bell to bell, all grades, in statute since July 1, 2026. School-issued devices are expressly outside it.

  • Three waves, ending in statute: Executive Order 33 in July 2024 directed VDOE to develop cell-phone-free education guidance. VDOE final guidance, shaped by nearly 6,000 public comments and 21 stakeholder convenings, defined bell to bell as phones off and stored from first bell to dismissal including lunch and passing periods, with division policies required by January 2025. SB108 (Pekarsky), signed in April 2026 and effective July 1, 2026, then put it in statute for every division and all grades.

  • Scope is broader than phones: The restriction reaches personal smart devices generally: smart watches, earbuds, smart glasses, and other internet-connected devices. Exemptions cover IEP and 504 accommodations, health monitoring, and other documented accommodations. What SB108 changed in practice was the high school lunch period, which many divisions had previously left open.

  • School-issued devices are exempt, and year one evidence is favorable: The statute governs student-owned devices. Division-issued laptops, Chromebooks, and tablets are outside its scope, which large divisions have stated plainly in their own policies, so the law makes the district-managed fleet the only sanctioned screen in the building. Fairfax moved in the other direction in July 2026 for its youngest students, banning division-issued tablets and laptops in pre-kindergarten and kindergarten with accommodation exceptions. VDOE reports that Regional Teachers of the Year in 2024, 2025, and 2026 described students as more focused and present, with better attendance and fewer conflicts driven by online activity. Divisions are still solving storage, enforcement, and parent communication, and no state funding for storage has been identified.

Source: SB108 bill text | VDOE cell phone-free guidance | FCPS policy and SB108 summary

STUDENT DATA PRIVACY | Mature statute plus a working consortium. The data privacy agreement is the gate, and it is a reusable one.

  • Three legal layers: Va. Code 22.1-289.01 is Virginia's SOPIPA analogue: a vendor providing school services or school-issued technology under contract must maintain a comprehensive information security program, limit collection, use, and disclosure to authorized educational purposes, refrain from targeted advertising and from selling student data, and flow those obligations down to subcontractors. Va. Code 22.1-20.2 requires VDOE, with VITA, to maintain and at least annually update a model data security plan and to designate a chief data security officer to support divisions. Breach notification to parents is required as soon as practicable.

  • How it works in practice: Virginia runs on the Student Data Privacy Consortium model. The Virginia Student Privacy Alliance coordinates divisions, and most expect a vendor to sign the Virginia standard Data Privacy Agreement. Signing once and then extending to additional divisions through Exhibit E is the efficient path in, and it is the closest thing Virginia has to a shared regional service.

  • Where the burden actually sits: Lighter than New York's Education Law 2-d regime: no named data protection officer as a statutory decision gate, no per-contract public posting requirement, and no mandated security framework at the division level. The obligations attach through the contract rather than through a regulation, which is why the data privacy agreement conversation is the whole conversation.

  • What the Canvas breach changed: Instructure disclosed a breach of Canvas, Virginia's statewide learning management system, involving names, email addresses, student ID numbers, and messages among users between April 25 and May 12, 2026. Because Canvas is a state-provided platform rather than one division's vendor choice, this landed as a statewide question, and it intersects directly with the notification duty in 22.1-289.01. Expect privacy agreement review and AI tool review to merge into one process during 2026-27, since the AI statute requires the forthcoming guidance to address privacy in AI systems. See Section 8.

Source: Va. Code 22.1-289.01 | Va. Code 22.1-20.2 | Virginia SDPC alliance | Roanoke response coverage

Sources: VDOE cell phone-free education hub | Virginia LIS bill tracking | VDOE Technology in Education | Virginia Student Privacy Alliance

5. Governance & Decision-Making

Last verified: August 16, 2026

Authority flows Governor to Secretary of Education (early childhood through higher education) to the Board of Education (policy, standards, accreditation) to VDOE (administration, led by the Superintendent of Public Instruction) to 131 school divisions. Unlike New York or Massachusetts, the Governor appoints both the Secretary and the entire Board, and the Board appoints no one above the Superintendent. Executive control of the education agency here is direct, which is why a change of administration changes VDOE quickly.

  • New leadership, now settled: Secretary Jeffery Smith is a career superintendent, known in Virginia as the dean of superintendents. Superintendent Jenna Conway was promoted from within VDOE and was named to the Education Commission of the States in May 2026. This is a practitioner-heavy pair rather than a statehouse policy team, and the stated priorities are early literacy, mathematics, teacher pipeline, school construction, and assessment modernization. The Secretariat added a second deputy secretary, Dr. Abby Edwards, in July 2026, alongside Deputy Secretary Connor Andrews and Assistant Secretary Kate Maxlow, a former division curriculum leader.

  • The Board, and the seat to watch: Nine members, all gubernatorial appointees, with Mashea Ashton elected president on January 8, 2026. Spanberger appointed Kimberly Bridges, Beth Rhinehart, William Robinson, and Jamelle Wilson on March 18, 2026, after the legislature removed roughly 40 Youngkin appointees across state boards. The composition leans toward leadership development, higher education, and workforce rather than technology or procurement. William Robinson's term expired June 30, 2026 and he serves until a successor is appointed, so a reappointment or replacement is live.

  • Election-year context, provisional note: Virginia is the exception in this batch. Because Virginia elects its governor in odd years, there is no statewide leadership change on November 3, 2026, and this administration runs to January 2030. Treat Virginia leadership as stable rather than provisional. What is on the November 3 ballot is local and material: more than 40 localities are asking voters to approve a 1% sales tax for school construction. Expect the capital picture in many divisions to look different in January 2027 than it does today.

  • Fiscal dependence is the structural fact: Divisions cannot levy taxes and depend entirely on city or county appropriation. There is no public vote on the school budget itself, which distinguishes Virginia from New York's May budget vote and from town meeting states. County boards of supervisors and city councils are therefore real actors in any significant division purchase, and the Local Composite Index, which sets each division's state and local split, is a term worth knowing in every budget conversation.

  • The regional layer, stated plainly: Virginia has no regional service agency with purchasing power. The eight Superintendent's Regions are VDOE convening and reporting geography: they organize regional superintendent meetings, structured the Commonwealth Listening Tour that reached more than 2,200 participants by the end of April 2026, and are the unit VDOE uses to report teacher vacancy data. Regional Governor's Schools and regional CTE centers are shared programs, not shared procurement. Division-level engagement matters more here than in any BOCES, Intermediate Unit, or ESC state.

  • Legislature: House Education and Senate Education and Health handle policy; money runs through House Appropriations and Senate Finance and Appropriations. Del. Sam Rasoul (D-Roanoke) is the most active K-12 technology legislator, carrying HB1186 on AI and HB1486 on device addiction instruction, alongside assessment flexibility work with Sen. David Suetterlein (R-Roanoke County). Sen. Stella Pekarsky (D-Fairfax) carried both SB394 on AI and SB108 on cell phones. Sen. Schuyler VanValkenburg (D-Henrico) has been the lead voice on assessment modernization. The Joint Subcommittee on Elementary and Secondary Education Funding, chaired by Sen. Mamie Locke, is where the formula rewrite happens, and it has not met publicly since December 2024.

6. Funding & Budget Rhythm

Last verified: August 16, 2026. 2026-2028 biennial budget in effect since July 1, 2026

  • How the formula works: The Standards of Quality formula funds prescribed staffing ratios rather than a per-pupil amount, split between state and locality by each division's Local Composite Index, a wealth measure. JLARC's 2023 study found Virginia funds below the 50-state average and below what its own benchmarks say is needed. Divisions spend billions above what the formula generates, with localities carrying the difference, which is why the Local Composite Index is the most contested number in Virginia school finance. See Section 3 for the redesign.

  • The local approval mechanism, stated explicitly: City council or county board of supervisors appropriation. Divisions have no taxing authority and Virginia has no budget referendum, no levy election, and no town meeting. A school board adopts a request; the appropriating body decides. The budget audience is therefore a small number of elected local officials rather than an electorate, and the money conversation happens at the county administration building. The one exception is capital: the new 1% sales tax does go to voters, which is why November 3 matters.

  • A very late budget, and what it bought: The 2026-2028 budget passed June 29, 2026 after an impasse over data center tax treatment that brought Virginia close to its first partial government shutdown, with the General Assembly adopting the Governor's amendments unanimously. It carries roughly $2 billion in new K-12 money: the state share of 4% salary increases in each year, up from 2% in the introduced budget; $449.8 million for rebenchmarking; $148.4 million more than doubling the flexible special education add-on plus $10 million for intensive support needs; construction grants reported at $519 million; $60 million more for infrastructure and operations per-pupil funding; and $2 million for the AI pilot. A temporary fee on data center electricity usage, capped at $600 million a year and expiring July 1, 2028, is a significant part of how it was paid for.

  • The timing mismatch that defined the year: Divisions build budgets January through March and adopt April through May. In 2026 they did that without state numbers, and many localities had already set local contributions before the 4% raise figure landed, so some employees will not see the full increase because the local match was not budgeted. The gap between the headline number and the local match is the more useful question in any budget conversation this fall.

  • The support cap, funded rather than repealed: The 2009 cap on state funding for support positions was funded up to the prevailing statewide ratio of 27.89 positions per 1,000 students beginning in FY2026, at a cost of about $222.9 million, and that ratio carries into the 2026-2028 budget. The mechanism remains in budget language rather than being repealed, so it has to be re-funded every biennium.

  • The new capital instrument, and its limits: The budget amended Va. Code 58.1-605.1 so that any county or city may hold a referendum on an additional local sales tax of up to 1% dedicated to school capital, and more than 40 localities acted before the August 14, 2026 deadline. Read the restrictions carefully: revenue must fund new construction or major renovation including related financing costs, it cannot repay debt on projects that began or were completed before the tax took effect, it sunsets no later than 20 years out, and the money sits in a locality fund rather than a division fund. It can pay for infrastructure inside a building project. It cannot buy devices. See Section 3.

  • Construction money in, loan money out: The FY26 School Construction Assistance Program round closed July 27, 2026 with $209.3 million available and awards pending in mid-August. The program awards 10%, 20%, or 30% of approved project cost depending on a division's Local Composite Index and fiscal stress ranking, and $618.5 million has been awarded since FY22. In the same budget the Literary Fund loan program was suspended and $172 million redirected to teacher retirement, so the state's low-interest construction loan channel is currently closed.

  • Federal money, and unusual new room to move it: The state budget created a Federal Uncertainty Contingency Fund. Separately, and less well known, the U.S. Department of Education approved Virginia under the Education Flexibility Partnership Act on January 16, 2026, running through the 2029-30 school year. VDOE can now approve certain federal waivers itself: divisions may consolidate or reallocate federal funds, extend spending deadlines, and seek relief from some Title I program and administrative requirements, including grants covering technology. Standards, assessment, accountability, funding distribution, Title I eligibility, equitable private school participation, and parental involvement requirements cannot be waived.

  • Where technology money lives: There is no dedicated state edtech fund. Technology rides the division general fund, the local capital improvement plan, federal programs including Title IV-A and E-rate, and the VPSA educational technology notes, a bond-funded annual grant program historically sized around SOL testing infrastructure and the one recurring, predictable state technology stream worth knowing by name. Note the structural consequence: with refresh competing inside a local capital plan against roofs and buses, and with the new sales tax stream restricted to construction, device refresh has no dedicated home.

7. Procurement & Vendor Path

Last verified: August 16, 2026

  • Framework: The Virginia Public Procurement Act governs. School divisions may buy from state contracts, run their own solicitations, or use cooperative contracts the Act authorizes. Divisions set their own small-purchase thresholds by local policy within the Act, so the practical first question is always what the division's own purchasing policy says rather than what the statute permits.

  • What carries the purchasing load, since no regional layer does: This is the through-line from Section 2. Virginia has no BOCES, Intermediate Unit, or ESC that contracts on a division's behalf, changes the net cost of a purchase, or hosts the student information system. Purchasing is carried by three things instead: state vehicles, national cooperatives, and, in Northern Virginia, one large division's solicitations that others ride. There is no regional aid incentive to route a purchase through and no regional entity that can sign for a group of divisions, so coverage planning here is division by division.

  • State vehicles: eVA is Virginia's mandatory statewide e-procurement portal, so vendor registration there is the baseline and non-negotiable. VITA, the Virginia IT Agency, holds statewide technology contracts and master agreements that divisions can ride without their own solicitation, including statewide E-rate contracts, and VITA has been actively refreshing those master agreements.

  • Cooperatives, which matter more here than in most states: Because Virginia has no regional purchasing agency, national cooperatives carry more weight than they do in New York or Massachusetts. Divisions commonly use VASS and OMNIA, Sourcewell, TIPS, and NCPA. In Northern Virginia, many divisions buy through Fairfax County's cooperative solicitations, which is the closest thing the state has to a regional vehicle. Which vehicle a specific division prefers is worth establishing early, because it determines how long a purchase takes.

  • The compliance layer: For anything touching student data, the Virginia standard data privacy agreement through the Virginia Student Privacy Alliance is effectively part of the procurement package, and 22.1-289.01 obligations attach through that contract. From July 1, 2026 forward, AI tool review criteria layer on top as divisions adopt policies under SB394 and HB1186, and those criteria will firm up once VDOE guidance publishes. In the interim the data privacy agreement is doing the work of both reviews.

  • One live procurement worth tracking: RFP 124939 for the SOL assessment system, issued July 27, 2026 with proposals due in mid-September, is the largest K-12 technology procurement in the state. The current contract runs about $46.5 million a year and the replacement is estimated at $30 million to $38 million. It will reset device, browser, network, and rostering requirements for every division when the new system goes live in 2027-28. Even for vendors not bidding, the resulting technical specifications are the thing to watch.

8. Technology & Digital Learning Posture

Last verified: August 16, 2026

  • Devices: No statewide 1:1 mandate, no device standard, no state device fund. Divisions built 1:1 fleets independently, accelerated by ESSER money that has now expired. The 2020 and 2021 purchases are five to six years old and out of warranty, and refresh is a live budget question in most divisions with no state program to lean on. The realistic instruments are the division general fund, the local capital improvement plan, and the VPSA educational technology notes.

  • Assessment infrastructure, and the biggest technology story in the state: SOL assessments are delivered digitally, and grades 3-8 reading and mathematics also carry growth assessments purchased from a state-approved vendor list. That whole stack is being replaced. VDOE issued RFP 124939 on July 27, 2026 for a vendor to administer all summative SOL assessments, the first competitive procurement since 2005, with proposals due mid-September 2026, the current contract concluding at the end of 2027, and a modernized system targeted for 2027-28. Stated requirements include more technology-enhanced items, alignment to the higher standards, stronger accessibility for English learners and students with disabilities, faster turnaround of results, and released practice items. Nothing published so far specifies an adaptive or through-year design, so treat the delivery model as open until the award. For a technology director this is a two-year readiness cycle: device specifications, secure browser, bandwidth, and rostering integration all reset.

  • Statewide platforms, and a funding line that has lapsed on the page: Canvas is the statewide learning management system, giving every public division its own fully supported instance at no cost, aligned deliberately with the platform used by the Virginia Community College System so students see continuity into postsecondary. GoOpenVA is the state open educational resources repository. VDOE's technology page still displays funding secured through June 30, 2026 and had not been updated as of August 16, 2026, while the 2026-27 professional learning calendar continues to feature both platforms. No renewal has been announced and no successor term published, so the current contract term is a question to put to VDOE or a division technology director directly.

  • The Canvas breach, and why it matters here: Instructure disclosed a cybersecurity incident affecting Canvas between April 25 and May 12, 2026, involving names, email addresses, student ID numbers, and messages among users, with the company stating it found no evidence that passwords, dates of birth, government identifiers, or financial information were involved. Because Canvas is Virginia's statewide LMS rather than one division's vendor choice, this landed as a statewide question. Roanoke City kept Canvas disconnected until May 13 and is reconsidering the platform, noting it had deliberately kept Canvas separated from its student information system. VDOE did not answer press questions about how many divisions use Canvas or how the platform was vetted and funded, so divisions made independent calls on when to reconnect. Class action litigation is under way.

  • Cybersecurity, and a federal grant running out: Va. Code 22.1-20.2 requires VDOE, working with VITA, to maintain and at least annually update a model data security plan and to designate a chief data security officer to support divisions on request. VDOE publishes the Model Security Plan and Best Practice Guidance in Cybersecurity for K-12 Schools, though the posted document carries no revision date and the page does not name the current officer. This is a model and an advisory office, not a mandate with an audit, so capacity across 131 divisions is uneven and thinnest in small rural divisions. The recent record is not theoretical: Hanover County lost divisionwide connectivity across 24 schools and roughly 17,000 students in March 2026 and prepared for at least a week of offline instruction, following disruptions at Manassas City, Mecklenburg, and Halifax in fall 2025 and two divisions caught in the Canvas incident. Public education holds a seat on the Virginia Cybersecurity Planning Committee, which administers State and Local Cybersecurity Grant Program funds, but that program was reauthorized in February 2026 without a new appropriation and lapses September 30, 2026.

  • AI capacity building: VDOE ran generative AI teacher training with community colleges in fall 2024 and offers scholarships for Google AI Essentials and related certificates. The $2 million pilot adds funded experimentation capacity, prioritizing rural and high-poverty divisions, though no application window had opened as of August 16, 2026. The larger effort is associational, as Section 3 sets out. VDOE's Digital Learning Unit began a monthly webinar series for educational technology leaders in July 2026 covering the Digital Learning Integration standards, AI, internet safety, and GoOpenVA, with monthly office hours.

  • Framing: Virginia frames educational technology through an instructional-quality and student-wellbeing lens rather than a compliance one. The near-unanimous AI votes and the bipartisan phone and screen time laws mean technology in schools is not a partisan fault line here, unlike several neighboring states. The privacy regime is real but contract-shaped rather than regulation-shaped, and there is no state cybersecurity mandate with an audit behind it. The live debate is about implementation capacity and money, not about whether technology belongs in classrooms.

9. Instructional Priorities & Mandates

Last verified: August 16, 2026

  • Literacy remains the dominant instructional initiative: The Virginia Literacy Act requires science-of-reading instruction, screening, and division literacy plans through grade 8, with K-3 phased in during 2024-25 and grades 4-8 during 2025-26. Executive Order 4 empowers a 24-member advisory work group, convened April 16, 2026 and running to December 31, 2027, to strengthen Student Reading Plans in the middle grades, update plan templates, and improve the VAConnects data platform. Section 3 carries the funding and capacity picture.

  • Mathematics is the growth area, and it has no literacy-style infrastructure behind it: Executive Order 4 directs VDOE to strengthen mathematics instruction, and the Mathematics Advisory Task Force expanded from 13 members to 25 in April 2026 with monthly meetings. The concrete mandate is access rather than instruction: under 2025 legislation at Va. Code 22.1-207.9, divisions must have policies in place for 2026-27 automatically enrolling grade 5-8 students who score in the statewide upper quartile on the mathematics SOL into advanced or accelerated mathematics, with parental opt-out, multiple additional entry pathways, annual parent notification, and annual disaggregated reporting. Note the asymmetry with literacy: mathematics has no mandated universal screener, no state-approved curriculum list, and no specialist staffing ratio, and its task force sits in budget language rather than in the Standards of Quality.

  • Assessment redesign is a live instructional question, not just a technical one: VDOE expanded the Innovative Assessment Workgroup from 17 members to 31 under Executive Order 4, with monthly meetings from March 2026, and its recommendations shaped the RFP. Cut scores rise beginning in 2026-27 on a four-year phase-in with a temporary performance level through 2028-29, so proficiency thresholds move before the new system arrives. From 2026-27, SOLs also count for 10% of a final course grade, testing moves to the last two weeks of the year to free roughly a month of instruction and permit retakes, and the score scale converts from 600 points to 100. Divisions have described the communication around these changes as thin.

  • Accountability: The Framework replaced the old accreditation model and reported first results in December 2025 on 2024-25 data, with more than 350 schools identified for additional support and 80 for comprehensive support. Accreditation now covers compliance with operating standards while the Framework carries performance across Mastery, Growth, Readiness, and Graduation. The Board takes final review of the label rename and related changes on August 26 and 27, 2026. Divisions are watching closely because this system is still being adjusted while they are being judged by it. Section 3 carries the detail.

  • Computer science: Virginia was the first state to mandate K-12 computer science standards, adopted in 2016, and computer science is integrated across grade levels with dedicated high school courses. Virginia also maintains separate Digital Learning Integration Standards of Learning, which is the framework VDOE's Digital Learning Unit organizes its educator professional learning around.

  • CTE and workforce: Strong CTE and lab school investment, and a bipartisan growth area. 2026 legislation expands apprenticeship access for high school students and broadens CTE instructor eligibility, including SB592 aligning CTE course offerings with emerging industry and workforce needs. High-quality work-based learning is on the superintendents association's fall conference program, and the Commonwealth has paired an AI career launch pad with industry partners.

10. Landscape & Pressures

Last verified: August 16, 2026

  • Enrollment is declining, and the projection says it continues: Fall 2025 membership was 1.209 million, down 1.2% from fall 2024. The Weldon Cooper Center projects a further 3% decline, roughly 36,800 students, to 1.172 million by fall 2030. The driver is births: children born in 2024 trailed the 2007 peak by more than 14,000. The decline sits in grades K-5 and 9-12, together 97.5% of the statewide loss, while middle grades are nearly flat. It diverges sharply by region: growth in exurban Northern Virginia and parts of the Richmond metro against decline in Southwest and Southside and several urban cores. Consolidation is politically dormant but fiscally looming downstate. Fall 2026 membership is a September 30 count and will not be published until late 2026 at the earliest.

  • The teacher workforce story has genuinely improved: The statewide teacher vacancy rate fell to 2.5% for 2025-26, from 3.4% in 2024-25 and 3.9% in 2023-24, a third consecutive annual decline. Eighteen divisions and all Governor's Schools reported zero vacancies. Regional variation is real: Southwest at 1.1%, Northern Virginia at 1.2%, Western Virginia at 1.3%, while Central Virginia sat at 4.1%. Teacher salaries rose roughly 18% between FY2022 and FY2026, and Virginia joined the Interstate Teacher Mobility Compact in May 2026 to speed licensure for teachers arriving from other states. Note that the release date has drifted, from late September in 2024 to December in 2025, so the 2026-27 figure could land any time between late September and December 2026.

  • Facilities are the pressure that just got a new release valve: Section 3 carries the capital picture. What belongs here is the consequence for technology: the state's own building survey found 5% of school buildings short of broadband capacity, which puts network capacity inside the facility condition conversation in Virginia rather than only inside the technology budget. Aging buildings mean aging infrastructure, and network and cabling work inside an approved construction project is the most reliable way infrastructure gets funded here, though no state guidance confirms that reading of the sales tax restriction, so it is a question for a division CFO.

  • Choice pressure is low, which is unusual for the region: Virginia has no ESA and no voucher program draining division budgets. The Education Improvement Scholarships Tax Credits program is limited in scale. Public school choice remains constrained and inter-division open enrollment is limited. The competitive pressure that exists comes from homeschooling and private school growth in some regions rather than from a state program. Compared with Ohio's roughly $1 billion a year in universal vouchers, this is a materially different budget conversation.

  • Federal climate: Federal funding uncertainty is an explicit planning factor rather than a background worry. The state budget created a Federal Uncertainty Contingency Fund, and Executive Order 5 established an Economic Resiliency Task Force covering federal workforce reductions and funding cuts, with the Secretary of Education a member. The superintendents association has been explicit that Title funds and IDEA continuity are its central concern. No withholding or delay of Virginia's Title or IDEA formula funds is documented in current sources, and Virginia gained Ed-Flex waiver authority in January 2026 through 2029-30 (see Section 6). The sharper exposures are narrower: the cybersecurity grant program lapsing September 30, 2026, and Virginia's unusually large federal workforce, which reaches schools through family stability and enrollment rather than through the education budget.

12. Key Notes for Engagement

Last verified: August 16, 2026. WORKING DRAFT for Jeremiah's edit. Research-generated engagement judgment, not settled advice

How to think about Virginia right now:

  • Everything is new at once, and that is still the opening line: New governor, new secretary, new superintendent, four new board members, a new AI law, a new phone law, a budget three months late, accountability redesign, assessment replacement, cut scores rising, and funding formula redesign. Division leaders are managing more simultaneous state-driven change than at any point in a decade, and VDOE's own listening tour surfaced policy fatigue as a theme. Opening with how are you absorbing all of this lands because it is true.

  • The November 3 sales tax referendum is the most consequential thing on the horizon: More than 40 localities are asking voters for a 1% sales tax dedicated to school capital. Know whether the locality you are visiting has one on the ballot, because it changes the capital conversation entirely in January. Know the limits too: it funds construction and renovation and the financing costs attached to them, it cannot repay debt on projects already begun, and it cannot buy devices. Where it helps is network and cabling inside a building project. And do not say every one of these referenda has passed, because Pittsylvania's failed in 2021 before passing the next year. Getting that distinction right in front of a CFO is a credibility marker.

  • The assessment rebid is the technology story of the next two years: VDOE is replacing the SOL assessment vendor for the first time since 2005, with proposals due mid-September 2026 and a new system targeted for 2027-28. Every division faces a readiness cycle: device specifications, secure browser, bandwidth, and rostering. Technology directors know this is coming and have almost no detail yet, and nothing published so far even settles whether the new system is adaptive. Tracking the RFP and helping think through readiness planning is worth more right now than any product conversation.

  • SY2026-27 is AI policy-writing year, and the state guidance has no deadline: Every division must adopt a policy consistent with VDOE guidance that had not published as of August 2026, and the statute sets no date by which it must. That is worth saying out loud to a superintendent, because it changes the posture from waiting to acting. The educator-first move is helping divisions think through policy, professional learning, and responsible implementation, not pitching AI products into an unsettled compliance environment. The pilot's rural and high-poverty priority points engagement toward divisions that rarely get vendor attention, and the real capacity building is running through VASS and VSTE rather than the agency.

  • Budget empathy means understanding the appropriation, not the formula: Divisions do not levy taxes and there is no budget referendum. A superintendent can want something and still not get it, because the county board of supervisors or city council appropriates. JLARC says the state underfunds, localities carry the gap, and in 2026 divisions adopted budgets three months before the state told them their numbers, leaving some 4% raises unmatched locally. Ask about that gap rather than the headline number. Respecting the Local Composite Index math and local appropriation politics separates Dell from vendors who assume money exists.

  • The data privacy agreement is the door, and it opens more than one division: Signing the Virginia standard privacy agreement through the Virginia Student Privacy Alliance and being visibly fluent in 22.1-289.01 obligations is table stakes, and Exhibit E makes it reusable statewide. Since the Canvas breach, divisions are asking sharper questions about incident response and notification timing, and the duty here is notification as soon as practicable. Because the state AI guidance is late, the privacy agreement is currently carrying the AI tool review too, which makes fluency in it worth more than usual.

  • There is no regional shortcut, so plan coverage on two tracks: Virginia has no BOCES, no Intermediate Unit, no ESC, and the eight Superintendent's Regions convene but do not contract. That makes the few genuine statewide channels more valuable: VSTE in December for technology leaders, VASS in October and January for superintendents, VASBO for business officials, and the Virginia Student Privacy Alliance for privacy. In Northern Virginia, Fairfax's cooperative solicitations are the nearest thing to a regional vehicle. Split the coverage itself: Fairfax and Northern Virginia set patterns others follow, while the administration's attention, the pilot money, and the sharpest facilities and enrollment pressure tilt toward Southwest and Southside. A Northern Virginia lighthouse plus a downstate partnership mirrors the state's own posture.

  • Workforce pathways are the most natural non-device conversation in the state: CTE, lab schools, apprenticeship expansion, IT and cyber academies, and industry credentials are a bipartisan growth area with money and legislative attention behind them, and work-based learning is on the superintendents association's fall program. Cybersecurity capacity is the adjacent one: divisions have lived through real incidents, the state offers a model plan rather than a mandate, and the federal grant pass-through lapses September 30, 2026. Capacity-building conversations land better here than compliance-relief conversations, because there is no state cybersecurity mandate to sell against.
