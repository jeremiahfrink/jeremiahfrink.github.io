1. Snapshot

Last verified: August 2026

Leadership & Governance
Governor Josh Shapiro (D), first term. On the November 3, 2026 ballot against Republican nominee Stacy Garrity, the sitting State Treasurer. Pennsylvania governors serve four-year terms on the midterm cycle
Secretary of Education Dr. Carrie Rowe, confirmed by the Senate December 9, 2025. A career western Pennsylvania superintendent, and not acting. Khalid Mumin resigned in 2025; Angela Fitterer served as acting secretary and stayed on as Executive Deputy Secretary
K-12 lead at PDE Amy Lena, Deputy Secretary for Elementary and Secondary Education. Fitterer, having run the department, is a more consequential internal actor than her title suggests
State chief selection Appointed by the Governor with Senate confirmation. The department follows the administration directly
State Board of Education 21 members split into a Council of Basic Education and a Council of Higher Education. 17 gubernatorial appointees on six-year terms plus the four legislative education chairs as voting members. Chair Karen Farmer White; Basic Education chair Dr. A. Lee Williams. The Governor names all three chairs, who serve at his pleasure
Attorney General Dave Sunday (R), elected 2024. Relevant here because he filed suit against TikTok on August 11, 2026 and remains in litigation against Meta (see Section 3)
Local control Strong on both policy and money. 500 districts with elected nine-member boards and independent taxing authority. Philadelphia is the sole exception (see Section 4)
Scale
School districts 500, from about 200 students to more than 100,000. Plus 29 Intermediate Units, 84 career and technical centers, and 136 comprehensive high schools offering CTE
Charters 160 brick-and-mortar and 14 cyber charter schools, together about 135,000 students. Cyber enrollment is near 60,000, up about 57 percent since 2020
Students / schools More than 1.7 million public school students. District enrollment was 1,486,147 in 2024-25, down about 5 percent since 2019-20, with eight of ten districts losing students
Regional layer 29 Intermediate Units. A real service and purchasing layer that owns the state's dominant technology bid vehicle, but not a New York style BOCES (see Section 2)
Largest districts Philadelphia, roughly 120,000 in district-run schools, then Pittsburgh at about 19,500, Central Bucks, Allentown, Reading, Hazleton Area. Philadelphia is six times the second district and everything below it is a 12,000 to 20,000 student mid-market
Per-pupil spending $23,870 in 2024-25, roughly $4,000 above the national average
Money & Calendar
Funding formula Basic Education Funding plus an adequacy supplement adopted after the 2023 Commonwealth Court ruling in William Penn School District v. PDE. The seven-year, $5.4 billion adequacy phase-in is in year three
Budget cycle Annual. State fiscal year July 1 to June 30. The FY2026-27 budget was signed July 12, 2026 at roughly $50.8 billion, about twelve days late
Late budget risk Structural, not occasional. The FY2025-26 budget was signed November 12, 2025, more than 130 days late. State subsidies stop entirely during an impasse and districts borrowed to cover payroll
Current state budget $11.8 billion for K-12, up about $670 million: $565 million more for adequacy, $58 million more in Basic Education Funding, about $47 million more for special education, $125 million for facility improvement, $120 million for safety and mental health, and $10 million each for CTE and student teacher stipends
Local tax cap The Act 1 index. The 2026-27 base index is 3.5 percent, down from 4.0 percent and from a record 5.3 percent in 2024-25. Exceeding it needs a PDE-approved exception or a voter referendum
Legislative session Two-year sessions, year-round. The General Assembly reconvened September 28, 2026 after summer recess
Local approval mechanism The school board adopts the budget. There is no public vote on a school budget. The only referendum trigger is a tax increase above the Act 1 adjusted index that does not qualify for an exception
Quick Yes/No
State AI guidance for schools? No. PDE has advisory web resources, a multi-agency AI Literacy Toolkit, and an educator AI endorsement framework, but no guidance document, no district policy requirement, and no AI pilot appropriation. A Penn State report to PDE is pending (see Section 3)
Cell phone restriction? No statewide restriction. The state funded pouches through the school safety grant program instead. Two bell-to-bell bills have each passed one chamber and neither is law (see Section 3)
Screen time action? No statute. The SAFECHAT Act on AI companion chatbots passed the Senate 49-1 in March 2026 and awaits the House. The Attorney General sued TikTok on August 11, 2026 (see Section 3)
Student data privacy law? No student-specific statute. FERPA plus the Act 151 of 2022 breach notification law govern. Senate Bill 378 was heard in committee in March 2026 and is not law
SDPC / standard NDPA state? Yes, and it is the operationally important answer. The Pennsylvania SDPC alliance is administered by Lancaster-Lebanon Intermediate Unit 13, with a Pennsylvania standard NDPA and an Exhibit E subscription that extends one signed agreement to other districts
1:1 / state device program? No mandate and no state device fund. Devices ride local revenue and capital. Connectivity leans on federal E-rate, now under FCC review (see Section 7)
ESA / voucher program? No ESA and no voucher. The EITC and OSTC business tax-credit programs awarded 101,751 scholarships averaging $2,776 in 2023-24, with roughly 69,000 applicants turned away on caps. Lifeline and PASS were never enacted
Collective bargaining Yes, and distinctive. Act 195 of 1970 gives Pennsylvania teachers the right to strike, one of the few states where that is true. Act 88 of 1992 limits timing and duration so strikes must end in time for a 180-day school year

2. What This State Is Driving Right Now

Last verified: August 2026

Pennsylvania in 2026 is a state where the biggest money stories are not about state programs at all. They are about money leaving districts (cyber charter tuition), money arriving unpredictably (a seven-year adequacy phase-in a Senate majority is openly questioning), and money that may stop arriving from Washington (E-rate). The state has acted less than its neighbors on all four hot topics, so district leaders here are writing their own policy rather than implementing someone else's. The superintendent list and the technology leader list below share almost nothing.

What superintendents are focused on

  • Cyber charter tuition, still the number one budget line: It appears in every source: PDE, PSBA, PASBO, the teachers union, the Auditor General, both budgets, and every press outlet. Districts pay cyber tuition derived from their own per-student spending, with the same formula applied to online and brick-and-mortar schools. Act 47 of 2025 let districts deduct operations and maintenance, student activities, and tax collection costs first, worth $175 to $178 million statewide and $12.1 million to Pittsburgh alone. A partial win. The statewide flat rate the associations wanted did not pass. See Sections 5 and 9.

  • Special education cost growth, rising fastest: One in five Pennsylvania students is now in special education, more than 347,000 and up 28 percent in a decade, while costs more than doubled and the combined state and federal share fell from 32 percent to 24 percent of expenditures. This is also the mechanism districts use to raise taxes: the special education exception is the workhorse of the Act 1 referendum exception process, accounting for 79 percent of approved exception dollars in 2026-27. See Section 5.

  • Whether the adequacy formula survives the next legislature: The seven-year, $5.4 billion adequacy commitment is three years and roughly $1.62 billion in, which is about 30 percent of the way through. Senate Majority Leader Joe Pittman has said publicly that the formula "created another world of haves and have-nots among the 500 school districts." Advocates warn that changing it restarts the litigation. Both chambers are in play in November. See Sections 4 and 5.

  • Building a budget against a state budget that may not arrive: A Pennsylvania-specific reflex. The prior year's budget landed 130-plus days late, subsidies stop entirely during an impasse because there is no continuing appropriation authority, and districts took short-term loans to make payroll. The FY2026-27 budget arrived close to on time, but the habit of deferring discretionary purchasing until the budget lands is now institutional. See Section 5.

  • Student mental health, and a phone policy the state did not give them: Mental health has been the top instructional challenge in PSBA's annual survey for five straight years, cited by 68.2 percent of responding districts. Meanwhile the bell-to-bell bills each passed one chamber and neither became law, so 500 boards are writing their own phone policies while PDE prepares implementation guidance for a mandate that does not yet exist. See Section 3.

What technology leaders are focused on

  • The E-rate wind-down review, which would hit every district at once: In June 2026 the FCC announced it would consider winding E-rate down entirely or restricting it to rural schools. Pennsylvania schools and libraries have saved $672 million over the past decade; IU13's 22-district region alone accounts for $42.3 million and the School District of Lancaster for $7.2 million at a 90 percent discount rate. Penn Manor's chief information officer called the prospect "catastrophic for all of us." Nothing else on this list threatens the recurring technology budget of every district simultaneously. See Section 7.

  • Refresh economics with no state device money and a 3.5 percent index: Fleets bought with federal relief money in 2020 and 2021 are four to six years old and out of warranty. There is no state 1:1 program, no device fund, and no dedicated technology line in PDE's grants index. Refresh competes inside local revenue capped at a 3.5 percent Act 1 index while special education and health insurance costs accelerate. See Sections 5 and 7.

  • AI adoption that has outrun AI policy: More than half of surveyed districts have integrated AI into classrooms and 80 percent have provided training, but only 70 percent have adopted a usage policy, and there is no state guidance to align to. Penn State completed a statewide listening tour in July 2026 and will deliver recommendations to PDE. The window to shape that framework is open now. See Section 3.

  • Cybersecurity demand with no mandate and no money behind it: Pennsylvania does not require districts to maintain a cyber plan, report incidents to the state, or carry insurance. The school safety apparatus that carries real money is scoped to threat assessment, physical security, and behavioral health. Yet cybersecurity is a named theme at the state technology conference, which tells you practitioner demand is running ahead of state structure. See Section 7.

  • Structured literacy screening, where the state has not finished its own work: Act 135 of 2024 requires structured literacy and screening, and districts must report reading deficiencies and teacher training. PDE's universal screener resources remain under review and have not been posted, so districts are selecting screeners without the approved list. This is the most reliable instructional door-opener in the state precisely because it is funded, dated, and incomplete. See Section 8.

The regional service agency view

Calibration: a strong contracting rail, a weak decision channel. Pennsylvania's 29 Intermediate Units are a genuine purchasing and services layer, and they own the technology bid vehicle most districts use. They are not New York's BOCES: BOCES Aid makes the same purchase cheaper, and Pennsylvania has no equivalent premium, so using an Intermediate Unit is a convenience rather than a financial advantage. They are not Ohio's ITCs either, because there is no statewide hosted data layer. Sell to districts, contract through the Intermediate Units.

  • What the Intermediate Units actually do: 29 regional agencies created by the state, delivering special education services, professional learning, administrative services, and purchasing cooperatives. PAIU reports at least $59 million saved through health insurance consortia, $16 million through energy purchasing, and special education transportation for more than 13,000 students from over 400 school entities.

  • PEPPM, the single most important technology contracting fact in the state: PEPPM has been operated since 1982 by the Central Susquehanna Intermediate Unit, a political subdivision with statutory authority to bid and award shareable contracts. It holds more than two million products under contract and serves buyers inside and outside Pennsylvania. Its sibling, the Keystone Purchasing Network, covers facilities, energy, transportation, and security. A technology vendor without a PEPPM listing is harder to buy from.

  • Technology capability varies enormously by Intermediate Unit: CSIU runs a full stack including its own student information system, financial system, and payroll. Montgomery County IU administers PowerSchool regionally for more than 200 schools. Berks County IU, a $187 million agency serving 70,000 students, advertises no technology services at all. Assume nothing regionally without checking which IU a district sits under.

  • Where the IUs do hold the pen: student data privacy: IU13 administers Pennsylvania's Student Data Privacy Consortium alliance, publishes a Pennsylvania standard NDPA, and operates the Exhibit E mechanism that lets a district subscribe to an already-signed agreement rather than negotiate its own. In a state with no student privacy statute, this contractual layer is the actual compliance regime. See Sections 3 and 6.

3. Hot Topics: The Four Questions of 2026

Last verified: August 2026

Pennsylvania is the least legislated state in this region on all four questions. On three of the four, the action sits in bills that have passed one chamber, in the Attorney General's office, or in a contractual regime the Intermediate Units built themselves. That absence is the story.

ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE | No statewide guidance, no mandate, and adoption running well ahead of policy.

  • Where it stands: PDE maintains advisory web resources, an AI and Digital Media Literacy hub and an Implementing AI in Schools page, both written in "schools should" language covering data authority, federal privacy compliance, human review of high-impact decisions, accessibility, and policy development. There are no templates, no deadlines, and no compliance mechanism. Pennsylvania does not appear on national trackers of states with released K-12 AI guidance.

  • What does exist: A multi-agency AI Literacy Toolkit launched February 27, 2026 across six departments, covering AI companion bots for parents, classroom practice, educator professional development, student mental health, and scam awareness. Separately, a June 2025 AI Program Endorsement framework creates a credential of up to 12 credits across six competency domains. That endorsement is the most formal AI artifact PDE has produced, and it is a credentialing document rather than classroom guidance.

  • What is coming: Penn State completed a statewide Shaping AI in Education listening tour in July 2026 and will deliver a report recommending how the state should support K-12 AI use, with no publication date announced. A Joint State Government Commission report in January 2026 recommended deploying AI only with empirical evidence of student benefit, guaranteeing equitable access, and training teachers in ethical application.

  • What that means on the ground: More than half of districts responding to PSBA's 2026 survey have integrated AI into classrooms and 80 percent have provided training, but only 70 percent have adopted a usage policy. Capacity building is happening at the Intermediate Unit and university level: IU1 built its August 2026 in-service around classroom and administrative AI, and Penn's Graduate School of Education expanded its Pioneering AI in School Systems program into Pennsylvania districts. Note also that PDE denied a charter application from an AI-led cyber charter, finding the model had no demonstrated record of success.

Source: PDE AI and digital media literacy | AI Literacy Toolkit announcement | Penn State statewide AI tour | PSBA State of Education 2026

SCREEN TIME | No Pennsylvania statute. The action is in the Attorney General's office and in one bill that has passed the Senate.

  • No enacted law: Pennsylvania has no social media age-verification law, no minors' social media law, and no adult-content age-verification law. Senate Bills 603 and 22 remain in committee. A prior-session bill requiring parental consent for under-16 accounts passed the House in 2024 and died in the Senate, and is sometimes still cited as live.

  • The live vehicle is chatbot-shaped: Senate Bill 1090, the SAFECHAT Act, passed the Senate 49-1 on March 17, 2026 and awaits House action. It would require operators to disclose that AI companions are not human, run self-harm and violence protocols, surface crisis information on high-risk language, remind minors periodically that they are talking to a machine, and block sexually explicit material for minors. It carries civil penalties and contains no school-specific requirements and no school carve-out.

  • Enforcement instead of legislation: The Attorney General filed a 65-page suit against TikTok on August 11, 2026 in Allegheny County under the Unfair Trade Practices and Consumer Protection Law, alleging addictive design, a misleading 13-plus app store rating, and false claims about Restricted Mode and Family Pairing. His office also confirms it remains in litigation against Meta. The Department of State separately stood up a 12-member AI Enforcement Task Force with a public complaint channel.

  • Keep the two tracks separate: The screen time track in Pennsylvania is consumer protection aimed at platforms and vendors. The phone policy track below runs through the Public School Code and is aimed at districts. In Pennsylvania law they are unconnected, which is different from several neighboring states where one bill carries both.

Source: Attorney General TikTok filing | SAFECHAT Act summary | Senate passage of SAFECHAT | WHYY coverage

CELL PHONES / CONNECTED DEVICES | No statewide restriction. The state funded pouches, districts largely declined, and two bills are each one chamber short.

  • What the state actually did: Act 55 of 2024 amended the School Safety and Mental Health grant program run by the Commission on Crime and Delinquency, with $100 million appropriated and a $100,000 automatic base grant per district plus $70,000 per Intermediate Unit, career and technical school, and charter. To spend it on phone restrictions a school entity had to adopt a policy prohibiting use during the school day.

  • The uptake number that reset the debate: Of 779 public school entities that applied for the $100 million, only 18 requested money for phone pouches. Districts cited monitoring burden at scale, cost-effectiveness, parent anxiety about emergency contact, and straightforward student workarounds. Roughly 2 percent uptake under voluntary funding is now the empirical case legislators use to argue for a mandate.

  • The two bills, and where they sit: Senate Bill 1014 passed the Senate 46-1 on February 3, 2026 and sits in House Appropriations. House Bill 1814 passed the House 126-75 on June 1, 2026 and has sat in Senate Education since June 5. Both require bell-to-bell policies covering the full school day including lunch, recess, and passing periods, with district discretion over storage and enforcement, and both set the start of 2027-28 as the deadline. Exceptions in both cover documented medical conditions, IEP and 504 requirements, English learner translation, teacher-authorized instructional use, and short principal-granted exceptions.

  • School-issued devices are expressly outside both bills: Both define a mobile device as a personal phone or portable internet-capable device "that is not owned by a school entity or issued by a school entity to a student." District Chromebooks, laptops, and tablets fall outside the restriction in either version. Senate Bill 1014 additionally carves out qualifying medical devices and a dual-use watch with wifi and data disabled. Neither bill was included in the July 2026 budget despite the Governor's stated support, and PDE has been preparing implementation guidance anyway.

Source: Senate Bill 1014 history | House Bill 1814 history | Pouch grant uptake reporting | Budget omission coverage

STUDENT DATA PRIVACY | No student privacy statute. A working contractual regime built and run by an Intermediate Unit does the real work.

  • What the law actually is: Pennsylvania has no student-specific data privacy act. What applies is FERPA and the federal children's online privacy rules, the student records provisions of the Public School Code, and Act 151 of 2022, which amended the Breach of Personal Information Notification Act effective May 2, 2023 to add medical, health insurance, and username and password information. Pennsylvania has no omnibus consumer privacy law.

  • The bill that would change that: Senate Bill 378, carried by Sen. Kristin Phillips-Hill, was considered by the Senate Education Committee on March 23, 2026. Its predecessor would have defined student data, established ownership and correction rights, and set collection protocols for school entities and vendors. It has not been enacted.

  • The regime that matters commercially: Pennsylvania has an active Student Data Privacy Consortium alliance administered by Lancaster-Lebanon Intermediate Unit 13. It publishes a Pennsylvania standard National Data Privacy Agreement and uses Exhibit E, which lets a district subscribe to an already-signed Pennsylvania agreement rather than negotiate its own. Membership runs $275 a year for a single district, $200 per school for groups, and $150 per member school through an Intermediate Unit. The national agreement moved to version 2.2 in November 2025.

  • The practical consequence: This converts a 500-district negotiation into a subscription. For any vendor handling student data, getting a signed Pennsylvania agreement into the IU13-administered registry is the highest-leverage single procurement action available in the state. It is also lighter compliance than New York or Massachusetts, which is a genuine point in Pennsylvania's favor and worth saying out loud.

Source: IU13 SDPC alliance | National Data Privacy Agreement v2.2 | Act 151 of 2022 | Senate Education consideration of SB 378

4. Governance & Decision-Making

Last verified: August 2026

Authority flows Governor to Secretary of Education to PDE, with a State Board that regulates but does not administer, down to 500 districts that are independent taxing bodies. The most useful thing to understand here is that the State Board is not where policy comes from. Watch the General Assembly and the Governor's budget instead.

  • Why the State Board is weaker than its counterparts: Four of its 21 seats are held by the majority and minority chairs of the House and Senate education committees, and every proposed regulation runs a gauntlet of the Independent Regulatory Review Commission plus both committees. The substantive policy of the last decade came from statute rather than regulation: graduation pathways from Act 158 of 2018, structured literacy from Act 135 of 2024, charter accountability from Act 55 of 2024. The Board's recent docket has been arts and health standards, a gifted education review, and accreditor applications. That is a standards body, not a policy driver.

  • A narrowly divided legislature, which explains what does not pass: The House is 102 Democrats to 101 Republicans, the Senate 28 Republicans to 22 Democrats, with a Democratic Governor. Senate Education is chaired by Lynda Schlegel Culver, with Lindsey Williams as minority chair. House Education is chaired by Peter Schweyer of Lehigh, the most consequential K-12 legislator in the state and author of the adequacy formula rewrite, with former Speaker Bryan Cutler as minority chair. That arithmetic is exactly why cyber charter tuition reform and the phone ban both stalled after passing one chamber. There is no distinct education technology lane; technology rides inside school safety, cyber charter, and CTE bills.

  • Election-year context, provisional note: On November 3, 2026 Pennsylvania elects a Governor and Lieutenant Governor, all 203 House seats, and half the Senate, with both chambers genuinely in play. Shapiro won the Democratic primary unopposed and faces State Treasurer Stacy Garrity. There is no statewide education referendum on the ballot. Treat the FY2027-28 budget posture as provisional until January 2027. For account planning, SY2026-27 purchasing decisions are already made and the live window is spring 2027 budget building.

  • Independent taxing authority is the structural fact: Districts levy their own real estate tax, earned income tax, and Act 511 local taxes, and property taxes supply about 42.6 percent of district revenue. The elected board adopts the budget and there is no public vote on it. The buying decision therefore sits with nine elected officials who answer to local taxpayers, the Act 1 index caps what they can raise without a referendum, and a pitch premised on the state paying for something lands badly.

  • Philadelphia is the governance exception, and the exception that matters most: Philadelphia is governed by a nine-member Board of Education appointed by the Mayor, currently Cherelle Parker, the structure that replaced the state-run School Reform Commission in 2018. Superintendent Dr. Tony B. Watlington, Sr. has led since 2022; the board president is Reginald Streater. Critically, Philadelphia is the only Pennsylvania district that cannot raise independent tax revenue, and it is carved out of the homestead exclusion so its share of state property tax relief reduces wage taxes instead. The current picture: a $4.6 billion budget adopted May 2026 with $225 million in cuts against a $300 million deficit, a plan closing 17 schools from 2027-28 while upgrading nearly 170 buildings, City Council fighting the closures, and roughly 50 schools without adequate cooling. The state budget sent $157 million more. Approach it as a stabilization account.

  • Everything below Philadelphia: Pittsburgh is the second district at about 19,500 students, led by Superintendent Dr. Wayne N. Walters with an elected board, and is running its own Future-Ready Facilities Plan consolidation. Below Pittsburgh there is no second metro giant, which means coverage strategy has to be regional and Intermediate Unit mediated rather than built around a top-ten district list.

Sources: PA State Board of Education | House Education Committee | Senate Education Committee | Philadelphia Board of Education

5. Funding & Budget Rhythm

Last verified: August 2026. FY2026-27 budget in effect since July 12, 2026

  • How the formula works: Basic Education Funding is the base, distributed through a student-weighted formula. Layered on top since 2024 is an adequacy supplement delivered through the Ready to Learn Block Grant, created after the Commonwealth Court held in February 2023, in William Penn School District v. Pennsylvania Department of Education, that the funding system was unconstitutionally inequitable. The Basic Education Funding Commission adopted an 8-7 plan in January 2024 committing $5.4 billion over seven years, with 387 of 500 districts below their adequacy targets.

  • Where the adequacy phase-in actually stands: Year one delivered $494 million to 348 districts. Years two and three delivered $565 million each, with year three going to all 500 districts subject to a $50,000 minimum. That is roughly $1.62 billion of $5.4 billion, about 30 percent through year three of seven, so on the current run rate the phase-in finishes late or short. Districts must report spending plans to PDE, and year-one reporting showed $123.3 million into academic performance, $89.6 million into early childhood, $72.7 million into STEM and foundational supports, and $43.4 million into charter tuition.

  • The local approval mechanism, stated explicitly: The school board adopts the budget. Pennsylvania has no budget referendum, no town meeting, and no levy election on the operating budget. The only voter trigger is a tax increase above the Act 1 adjusted index that does not qualify for an exception. The budget audience is therefore nine elected school directors, and the money conversation happens in a board finance committee rather than at a county building or a ballot box.

  • Act 1 and the squeeze every business manager is living in: The base index averages growth in the state average weekly wage and the federal employment cost index for schools, with wealthier districts receiving an adjusted index. It fell from a record 5.3 percent in 2024-25 to 4.0 percent to 3.5 percent for 2026-27 while special education and health insurance costs accelerated. Act 25 of 2011 cut the exceptions to four: grandfathered construction debt, electoral debt, special education, and retirement contributions. For 2026-27 PDE approved exceptions for ten districts totaling $23.1 million, 79 percent of it special education. That exception is the release valve, which is also why special-education-adjacent spending is politically defensible in a way general technology spending is not.

  • The late budget habit, and what it does to purchasing: The FY2026-27 budget was signed July 12, 2026, about twelve days past the constitutional deadline, which in Pennsylvania counts as on time. The prior year was the catastrophe: signed November 12, 2025, more than 130 days late. Pennsylvania has no continuing appropriation authority, so basic and special education subsidies simply stop going out during an impasse, and districts took short-term loans to cover payroll. A district that signs in June may not be able to pay until November. Build that into terms rather than being surprised by it.

  • What the current budget bought: $11.8 billion for K-12, roughly $670 million more year over year: $565 million more for adequacy and tax equity, $58 million more in Basic Education Funding, about $47 million more for special education, $120 million for safety and mental health, $125 million for facility improvement including $25 million for Solar for Schools, $10 million more for CTE, $10 million more for student teacher stipends, and $14.3 million more for free breakfast. Philadelphia received $157 million more. Riding in the code bills: 30 minutes of daily recess for K-5, a required district facilities inventory by the end of 2027, CTE competency exams permitted before twelfth grade, and more cyber charter monitoring. Not in it: the cell phone ban, any voucher, and a proposed $1 billion facilities bond.

  • Where technology money lives: There is no dedicated state educational technology fund and no device line in PDE's grants index. Technology rides the district general fund, the local capital plan, federal programs including Title IV-A, and E-rate, which is now under FCC review. The one state program that can touch infrastructure is the Public School Facility Improvement Grant Program, where internet connectivity is an eligible use. The structural consequence: with no state device money and a 3.5 percent index, refresh has no dedicated home and competes against roofs and buses.

Sources: PSBA budget analysis | Adequacy funding tracking | PDE Act 1 index | Referendum exceptions report

6. Procurement & Vendor Path

Last verified: August 2026

  • Framework: The Public School Code of 1949 governs. Section 807.1 covers purchase of supplies and services; section 751 covers work done under contract, including Pennsylvania's separate-prime construction regime. Formal sealed bidding applies above a statutory amount and a lower band requires written or telephonic quotations, with both figures adjusted annually for inflation effective January 1. Because they move every January, confirm the current figures with the district business office or PASBO rather than working from a number in a deck. Technology purchases get no special statutory treatment; they are supplies and services under 807.1.

  • What the regional layer carries here, and it is a lot: This is where the Section 2 calibration cashes out. Because the Intermediate Units own the dominant bid vehicle, the practical procurement path runs through them even though they hold no authority over a district's decision. PEPPM, operated by the Central Susquehanna Intermediate Unit since 1982, is the default technology bid vehicle: CSIU is a political subdivision with statutory authority to bid and award shareable contracts, holds more than two million products under contract, and markets its awards as piggyback-ready. The Keystone Purchasing Network is its non-technology sibling. Where Virginia's missing regional layer pushes weight onto national cooperatives, Pennsylvania's regional layer absorbs that role itself.

  • COSTARS, the state alternative: COSTARS is administered by the Department of General Services under the Commonwealth Procurement Code and reaches more than 9,500 Pennsylvania buyers across 100-plus pre-negotiated vendor contracts, with school districts explicitly eligible as local public procurement units. So a district has three legal paths: run its own solicitation, ride COSTARS, or buy through an Intermediate Unit cooperative. For technology the cooperative is the path of least resistance. An Intermediate Unit endorsement will not close a deal here. A missing PEPPM listing can lose one.

  • The privacy and DPA compliance layer: Lighter than New York or Massachusetts, and better organized than its statutory basis suggests. There is no student privacy statute, but the Pennsylvania SDPC alliance administered by IU13 is the operative gate for anything touching student data, and Exhibit E makes one signed Pennsylvania agreement reusable across districts. Get into that registry early. Expect AI review criteria layered on district by district rather than by state requirement, since no state framework exists to align to. On registration: state supplier portal registration matters only for Commonwealth agency business, not for school district sales, though PDE master terms flow down to grant-funded purchases.

Sources: Public School Code of 1949 | COSTARS member information | PEPPM | Keystone Purchasing Network

7. Technology & Digital Learning Posture

Last verified: August 2026

  • Devices: No statewide 1:1 mandate, no device standard, no state device fund, and no technology line in PDE's grants index. Fleets bought with federal relief money in 2020 through 2022 are four to six years old with no state replacement mechanism, so refresh comes from local revenue capped at a 3.5 percent index, or from capital and bond. In Philadelphia refresh competes with $225 million in cuts and a July 2026 city controller audit that identified missing equipment.

  • E-rate, and why it is the biggest technology story in the state: In June 2026 the FCC announced it would consider winding E-rate down entirely or restricting it to rural schools, citing screen time concerns. Pennsylvania schools and libraries have drawn $672 million over the past decade. IU13's 22-district region alone accounts for $42.3 million, and the School District of Lancaster for $7.2 million at a 90 percent discount rate. Because Pennsylvania has no state connectivity program and no state broadband program aimed at schools, E-rate is the connectivity budget for most districts rather than a supplement to it. Every technology director in the state is watching this and almost none of them have a plan B.

  • Assessment infrastructure: The PSSA covers grades 3 through 8 in English language arts and mathematics plus science, the Keystone Exams are end-of-course assessments in Algebra I, Literature, and Biology, and the PA Alternate System of Assessment serves students with the most significant cognitive disabilities. Online administration is the norm, delivered through the state's INSIGHT test portal. The FY2026-27 budget added the ability to administer CTE competency exams before twelfth grade, a scheduling change districts will feel this year. Unlike Virginia, Pennsylvania has no assessment vendor rebid in motion, so the assessment stack is a stable planning assumption.

  • Statewide platforms districts get free: The Standards Aligned System portal at pdesas.org is free to Pennsylvania educators and carries standards with a vertical alignment viewer, curriculum frameworks, the Classroom Diagnostic Tools, instructional materials, and the graduation toolkit. Current portal notes include assessment updates for 2026-27, arts and humanities standards under revision, and a new multi-factor authentication requirement. The Pennsylvania Information Management System is the state longitudinal collection. Note what is absent: PDE provides no statewide learning management system and no statewide student information system. Those are district decisions, or in some regions Intermediate Unit decisions.

  • Accountability data is unusually current: The Future Ready PA Index is the public dashboard covering proficiency, growth, attendance, career readiness benchmarks, and graduation rates, and it was already displaying 2025-26 data as of August 2026. That is faster than most states and makes it a good conversational entry point with a superintendent.

  • Cybersecurity, a clean absence: Pennsylvania does not require districts to maintain a cybersecurity plan, report incidents to the state, or carry cyber insurance. The school safety machinery is the School Safety and Security Committee under the Commission on Crime and Delinquency, whose published scope is threat assessment, physical security, behavioral health, and school climate. Whether cybersecurity spending is allowable under the safety and mental health grants depends on an eligible activities list set annually in statute, so check the current-year list before positioning against that fund. The FY2026-27 budget put $10 million into Commonwealth cybersecurity infrastructure, which is state agency money rather than district grants.

  • AI capacity building sits outside PDE: PDE's AI hub is overwhelmingly protective in framing: risk, scams, deepfakes, and companion bots rather than instructional capacity. The instructional work is being done by PAECT, whose Keystone Technology Innovators Summit runs each July, by the Intermediate Units through their professional learning arms, and by universities including Penn, which expanded its Pioneering AI in School Systems program into Pennsylvania districts.

  • Framing: Pennsylvania frames educational technology through a cost and wellbeing lens rather than an instructional transformation one, and the evidence is structural rather than rhetorical. The only statewide technology presence PDE promotes is a digital safety hub. The safety apparatus carrying real money is defined around physical security and behavioral health, with cybersecurity outside it. There is no device fund, no statewide platform, no state 1:1, and no privacy regime to build a compliance market around. The one place technology has a durable institutional home is procurement, through a 1982-vintage bidding cooperative. Leading with instructional transformation will not connect to any state structure here. Leading with refresh economics, staffing scarcity, connectivity risk, and student wellbeing will.

8. Instructional Priorities & Mandates

Last verified: August 2026

  • Structured literacy is the dominant instructional mandate: Act 135 of 2024 requires evidence-based reading instruction grounded in reading science, requires every school entity's professional development plan to include structured literacy training, and requires curriculum materials aligned with the science of reading. PDE must set competencies and training standards, build reading standards into educator preparation, and convene a Reading Leadership Council covering curriculum, intervention, screeners, and professional development. It builds on Act 55 of 2022. PDE issued a structured literacy update in January 2026, and the universal screener resources remain under review and have not been posted, so districts are choosing screeners ahead of the state list. An active, funded, incomplete mandate, and the most reliable instructional entry point in the state. Note the contrast with mathematics, which PSBA's 2026 survey identifies as the academic sore spot, with fewer than half of eighth graders proficient and no state policy comparable to the literacy act.

  • Computer science: the standards do not exist in the form people assume: Pennsylvania has no standalone K-12 computer science standards and no computer science graduation requirement, and computer science is not among the twelve subject areas in PDE's curriculum index. The nearest analogue is Technology and Engineering, which points to the STEELS standards for science, technology and engineering, and environmental literacy and sustainability, adopted in July 2022 and implemented July 1, 2025. PDE's STEM messaging is economic rather than standards-based, citing roughly 13,000 open computing jobs a month at an average salary above $101,000.

  • PAsmart is the live grant program to know: PAsmart is active and funded into FY2026-27, offering planning grants up to $75,000 and innovation grants up to $500,000 with a performance period to June 30, 2027. More than $80 million has gone out through more than 500 grants reaching over 1,250 schools since 2018-19. In a state with no dedicated technology fund, this is the closest thing to one, and it is competitive rather than formula-driven.

  • Accountability and graduation: Accountability runs through the state's federal plan with comprehensive and targeted support designations, surfaced on the Future Ready PA Index. Graduation has been pathway-based since the class of 2023: students meet the requirement through one of five pathways, with alternatives for those who do not demonstrate proficiency on the three Keystone Exams. Allowing CTE competency exams before twelfth grade is this year's practical change.

  • CTE and workforce, where the new money is: More than 80 career and technical centers operate under a modernized career clusters framework with industry-recognized credentials. The FY2026-27 budget added $10 million for CTE inside a $193 million annual workforce investment, on top of roughly 60 percent growth since 2023. This is the most natural technology adjacency in the state that is not about devices: workforce pathways, IT and cyber academies, and industry credentials.

  • Special education, which is an instructional issue and a fiscal one at once: Technical assistance runs through PaTTAN, with offices in Norristown, Harrisburg, and Pittsburgh delivering summer institutes and on-site support. The instructional load is growing fast: one in five students, more than 347,000, up 28 percent in a decade. The fiscal consequence shows up twice elsewhere in this profile, in the Act 1 exception process and in cyber charter special education tuition.

9. Landscape & Pressures

Last verified: August 2026

  • Enrollment is declining and the decline is uneven: District enrollment was 1,486,147 in 2024-25, down about 5 percent since 2019-20, with eight of ten districts losing students. The divergence is regional: growth in the Philadelphia collar counties and the Lehigh Valley against decline across western and northern Pennsylvania. The consequence shows up in the closure record rather than in a consolidation debate: Philadelphia closing 17 schools from 2027-28, Pittsburgh consolidating. Pennsylvania still has 500 districts and no consolidation legislation has advanced.

  • Cyber charters are the biggest single transfer out of district budgets: Roughly 60,000 students across 14 cyber charter schools, up about 57 percent since 2020. Districts pay tuition derived from their own per-student spending, with the identical formula applied to online and brick-and-mortar schools despite very different overhead. The Auditor General's February 2025 audit of five cyber charters found revenue rising from $473 million in 2019-20 to $898 million in 2022-23, reserves up sharply, and one operator spending $196 million on 21 buildings for an online school. What passed in response was oversight, not a flat rate: Act 55 of 2024 added trustee governance rules, enrollment-accuracy audits, advertising disclosure, and weekly wellness checks, plus annual residency verification, and Act 47 of 2025 added the tuition deductions. A proposed $8,000 flat statewide rate, projected to save districts $616 million a year, passed the House 104-98 in June 2025 and died in the Senate. Every district CFO knows that number.

  • The teacher pipeline, and a stipend program with more demand than money: Pennsylvania issued more emergency permits than normal certifications in 2021-22 and the workforce has been broadly stagnant since 2018. Shortage geography is extreme rather than uniform: Philadelphia needs roughly 3,577 additional teachers against a 41 percent supply decline over a decade, while Forest County issued zero teaching certificates in 2024 while needing five. The student teacher stipend program grew from $10 million in 2023 to $40 million in FY2026-27, but funded 4,199 recipients against 7,793 applicants over two years. Note also that Pennsylvania teachers can legally strike under Act 195 of 1970, constrained but not eliminated by Act 88 of 1992, one of the most consequential structural differences between this state and its neighbors.

  • Facilities, with a suspended state program: PlanCon remains suspended: PDE is not accepting applications for PlanCon 2.0 or the maintenance program for 2026-27, though existing projects continue through reimbursement. The substitutes are the Public School Facility Improvement Grant Program, with a $500,000 minimum project cost, a $5 million maximum grant, and internet connectivity among eligible uses, plus the rolling Solar for Schools program. The FY2026-27 budget requires every district to complete a facilities inventory by the end of 2027, a useful hook: that inventory is when infrastructure conditions get written down.

  • Choice pressure comes from cyber charters, not vouchers: Pennsylvania has no ESA and no voucher. EITC and OSTC are business tax-credit scholarship programs; in 2023-24 they funded 101,751 scholarships averaging $2,776, with roughly 69,000 applicants denied on caps. Lifeline and PASS were never enacted, and the state's decision on the new federal tax-credit scholarship has been deferred pending federal guidance. The competitive pressure that actually moves district budgets here is cyber charter enrollment, not private school choice. That is a different conversation from Ohio's roughly $1 billion a year in universal vouchers. Underneath all of it sits the number PSBA put on the squeeze in March 2026, surveying 230 districts: a mandated cost gap above $4.1 billion, with pension, charter tuition, and special education costs rising $6.1 billion over a decade while state revenue rose $2.0 billion.

Sources: PSBA State of Education 2026 | Cyber charter reform reporting | Teacher pipeline reporting | PlanCon status and successor programs

11. Key Notes for Engagement

Last verified: August 2026. WORKING DRAFT for Jeremiah's edit. Research-generated engagement judgment, not settled advice

How to think about Pennsylvania right now:

  • The state has done less than its neighbors, and that changes the conversation: No AI guidance, no phone law, no student privacy statute, no screen time statute, no device money, no statewide platform. Districts here are writing their own policy rather than implementing the state's, which makes this a place where helping a district think, rather than helping it comply, is the higher-value offer. The one place the state has acted hard, structured literacy, is also where it has not finished its own homework.

  • Lead with money mechanics, because that is the language: Independent taxing authority, a 3.5 percent Act 1 index, the special education exception as the release valve, a $4.1 billion mandated cost gap, and cyber charter tuition as the top line. A business manager who hears that you know the index fell from 5.3 percent to 3.5 percent in two years while special education grew 28 percent will take the next meeting. This is the state where the CFO and superintendent conversations sit closest together.

  • Treat the budget impasse habit as a commercial fact, not a news story: State subsidies stop entirely during an impasse and there is no continuing appropriation authority. Last year districts borrowed to make payroll, and they defer discretionary purchasing from July until the budget lands. Flexible terms and an honest acknowledgment of that cycle are worth more here than a discount.

  • E-rate is the technology conversation to open with: The FCC's June 2026 review of winding E-rate down or restricting it to rural schools is the largest simultaneous threat to Pennsylvania district technology budgets, and the one thing every technology director is worried about that no vendor is discussing with them. Knowing what a district actually draws, and what its Intermediate Unit consortium draws, makes the conversation concrete rather than sympathetic.

  • Two procurement actions change access statewide, and a third question decides the regional motion: Get a Pennsylvania standard NDPA into the IU13 privacy registry, which converts a 500-district negotiation into a subscription, and get a PEPPM listing, which is how districts actually buy technology without running their own bid. Neither closes a deal; missing either can lose one. Then check which Intermediate Unit a district sits under, because capability varies enormously: CSIU runs a full technology stack, Montgomery County IU administers PowerSchool regionally, and Berks County IU advertises no technology services despite a $187 million budget.

  • AI is a policy-writing conversation, not a product conversation, and the window is now: Half of districts are using AI without a policy, there is no state framework, and the Penn State report to PDE is the likely vehicle for one. Helping districts think through policy, professional learning, and responsible implementation is the educator-first move, and it puts Dell in the room while the framework is being written rather than after.

  • Two Pennsylvanias, and neither is a top-ten district list: Philadelphia is a stabilization account: closures, cuts, no taxing authority, and an audit that identified missing equipment. Everything below Pittsburgh is a 12,000 to 20,000 student mid-market spread across 500 districts, which means coverage has to be regional and Intermediate Unit mediated. The rural and small-district end is where the teacher pipeline and facilities pressure are worst and where vendor attention is thinnest.
