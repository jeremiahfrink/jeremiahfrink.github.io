1. Snapshot

Last verified: August 17, 2026

Leadership & Governance
Governor Ned Lamont (D), in office since January 2019. On the ballot November 3, 2026; won the August 11 primary and faces Sen. Ryan Fazio (R-Greenwich). Raters had the race Solid Democratic as of late 2025
Commissioner of Education Charlene M. Russell-Tucker, verified in seat August 2026. The Commissioner also serves as Secretary of the State Board
State chief selection Appointed by the Governor on the recommendation of the State Board, under Conn. Gen. Stat. 10-3a, confirmed by either house rather than both under 4-7. The term is coterminous with the Governor's, so the commissionership is on the November ballot indirectly
State Board of Education 14 seats including two nonvoting students, all appointed by the Governor under 10-1. The chair seat is unfilled: Vice Chairperson Erin D. Benham has presided through 2026 and the members page lists no chair (see Section 5)
Secretary of Education None. No cabinet post sits above the Commissioner. Early childhood is a separate agency, the Office of Early Childhood
Local control Strong on spending discretion, weak on revenue. Boards have no taxing authority, but the town appropriates a bottom line and the board spends it at its own discretion under 10-222 (see Section 6)
Scale
Districts Roughly 200 operating districts across 169 towns, including 17 regional districts, about 20 charters, RESC-operated magnets, and the state-run technical system. No official count is published, so state the basis when you quote one
Students / schools Fall 2025 membership fell below 500,000 for the first time in roughly two decades, to approximately 498,000, on analysis of CSDE EdSight data. CSDE issued no enrollment release for 2025-26, so confirm the October 1 count on EdSight. Roughly 1,000 public schools
Regional layer Six Regional Educational Service Centers: CREC, ACES, C.E.S., EASTCONN, EdAdvance, and LEARN. Substantial on services, schools, and special education, thin on technology purchasing (see Sections 2 and 7)
Largest districts Bridgeport, Waterbury, New Haven, Hartford, Stamford. Hartford has lost roughly one student in four since 2011 and is no longer the largest
State-operated district The Connecticut Technical Education and Career System runs 17 technical high schools plus a technical education center, roughly 11,200 full-time students, under its own board
Money & Calendar
Funding formula Education Cost Sharing: a foundation of $11,525 per student, unchanged since 2013, with weights of 30% for low income, an additional 15% for concentrated poverty, and 25% for multilingual learners, split by a wealth measure weighting property 70% and income 30%
Budget cycle Biennial, adopted in odd years, adjusted in even-year short sessions. State fiscal year July 1 to June 30. The 2026 session ran February 4 to May 6 and the FY2027 adjustments were signed May 26, 2026
Current state budget The formula was left untouched and two supplemental grants layered on top: Supplemental Education Aid, on a simulated $13,087 foundation, and District Relief and Compensatory Use Learning Aid, guaranteeing every town at least 4% of its ECS grant. Roughly $173 million to districts, plus $12 million for free breakfast and $150.6 million for school construction
Legislative session Odd years January to early June, even years February to early May. The 2027 long session convenes in January and writes the next biennial budget
Local approval mechanism Town appropriation, with the mechanism set by each town charter: board of finance, town meeting, referendum, town or city council, or representative town meeting. Regional district budgets go to simultaneous referendum in every member town. Failed budgets and re-votes are a routine spring event (see Section 6)
District budget rhythm Superintendent builds December to January, board adopts and transmits to the town at least two months before the annual meeting, town review February to March, adoption April to May with re-votes where budgets fail
Quick Yes/No
State AI guidance for schools? YES, and advisory only. Issued by the Commission for Educational Technology inside Administrative Services, not by CSDE. It carries no publication date, its latest statutory anchor is 2024, and it advises districts against writing a standalone AI policy (see Sections 3 and 4)
Cell phone restriction? NO, not in law. Bell-to-bell bills died in the Senate in both 2025 and 2026. The State Board issued an advisory position statement on August 5, 2026 recommending bell to bell in all grade bands, with no deadline and no reporting (see Section 4)
Screen time action? YES on platforms, and the state has now turned the lens on school technology too. Consumer statutes reach platforms from 2024 and Public Act 26-15 adds parental consent for algorithmic feeds from January 1, 2028. The August 2026 guidance asks districts to audit their own ed-tech program (see Section 4)
Student data privacy law? YES, and among the strictest in the Northeast. Conn. Gen. Stat. 10-234aa through 10-234dd, from Public Act 16-189. Non-conforming contracts are void, not voidable (see Section 4)
SDPC / standard NDPA state? NO. Connecticut is not a Student Data Privacy Consortium alliance state. The state runs its own instrument, the Model Terms of Service Addendum, and its own registry, the Connecticut Educational Software Hub
1:1 / state device program? No statewide mandate, device standard, or refresh fund. The substitute is aggregated pricing rather than money: a statewide computer reverse auction and a statewide student information system solicitation, both run by Administrative Services (see Sections 3 and 7)
ESA / voucher program? None, and none close. The choice pressure is interdistrict magnets, Open Choice, and about 20 charters, all inside the public system and shaped by the Sheff settlement
Collective bargaining Mandatory and strong under the Teacher Negotiation Act. Strikes are prohibited and disputes go to binding arbitration

2. Where the Focus Is

Last verified: August 17, 2026

Connecticut in 2026 is a state that has put off its hardest question. Its school funding formula starts from a set amount per student, and that amount has not changed since 2013. In 2026 the state declared the formula fully funded for the first time in its history, which is true and also means fully funded at a thirteen-year-old number. Rather than reopen it, the legislature added two one-time grants on top and created a commission that reports in January. Almost everything a superintendent is worried about points at that date. Almost nothing a technology director is worried about does. Both lists are ranked by how much attention the item actually consumes, not by dollar size.

What superintendents are focused on

  • The per-student amount has not moved since 2013, and this year's fix runs out: Rather than change the formula, the 2026 legislature added about $173 million through two new one-time grants. Keeping that money flowing in 2028 would take another $152.2 million, and the budget contains no language saying how that would be distributed or where it would come from. In plain terms, superintendents built this year's budgets on money with no successor. See Section 3.

  • Special education costs the state promises to share and then does not fully pay: When a single student's special education costs run very high, the state is supposed to reimburse 85% to 91% of the amount above a set threshold. But the reimbursement is capped by however much money the legislature put in the account, so when that runs short every district gets a smaller percentage. In 2025-26 districts actually collected between 66.8% and 71.5%. The account was left flat for this year against an estimated $55.6 million shortfall. This is the largest cost a superintendent cannot control. See Section 3.

  • Mandate load, which is the emotional center of gravity rather than the largest number: The superintendents association's published 2026 priorities lead with the profession and a two-year moratorium on unfunded mandates, and put money second. The session then delivered partial relief, including extended zero-emission bus deadlines, alongside roughly twenty-five new public acts.

  • Fewer students, the same fixed costs, and a spending floor that is sinking: Enrollment fell below 500,000 for the first time in about two decades. State law normally stops a town from spending less on schools than it did the year before. But one of the eight exceptions lets a town reduce that floor when enrollment drops, using a five-year lookback, and towns are using it. So the protection districts rely on is quietly sinking across much of the state. See Sections 6 and 10.

  • The state arriving inside distressed districts, and a new statutory route for it: Bridgeport has been under formal State Board action since January 2025 with monthly public reporting. Hartford is under a state financial audit in which the state's preliminary analysis found a surplus while the district projected a large multi-year deficit. The FY2027 implementer created a fiscal intervention process. See Section 3.

What technology leaders are focused on

  • Two statewide technology procurements that have gone quiet, and both reset local planning: The Department of Administrative Services issued a statewide student information system solicitation, 25PSX0293, which closed February 27, 2026, and planned a statewide reverse auction for staff and student computers in March 2026 with vendor commitments to post to CTsource in April. No award and no posted pricing had surfaced as of August 17, 2026, and the Commission's own page still describes both in the future tense. Districts are budgeting without knowing whether state pricing is coming. See Sections 7 and 8.

  • A privacy law with unusual teeth, and a compliance gap nobody has closed: If a contract touching student data does not contain ten specific provisions the law requires, the contract is void. Not voidable, void. The school board must also post every such contract publicly within five business days. The state's own tracking found that of 6,710 software products in use across 75 districts, 563 were compliant. Every technology director in Connecticut is working that gap. See Sections 4 and 7.

  • Refresh economics with no state device money and a shrinking local envelope: There is no state 1:1 program, no device standard, and no refresh fund. The pandemic-era Everybody Learns initiative is defunct, and the federal digital equity program terminated in May 2025 taking roughly $29 million Connecticut had expected. Refresh now competes against staff inside a town appropriation that the Minimum Budget Requirement no longer protects. See Sections 6 and 8.

  • Guidance that turned the screen time question toward the ed-tech stack itself: The State Board's August 5, 2026 position statement is not only about phones. It asks districts to audit their ed-tech program and weigh the costs of digital tools against their benefits, and asks that codes of conduct address unapproved AI use. The teachers association endorsed it. A technology director here this fall is defending the software footprint, not expanding it. See Sections 3 and 4.

  • Real scar tissue on security and no compliance framework to hang a budget request on: The PowerSchool breach reached about 70% of Connecticut districts and New Haven lost $6 million to a business email compromise. Yet there is no district cybersecurity plan requirement, no reporting clock, and no certification: the 2026 cybersecurity bill died in committee. The de facto security layer is the Connecticut Education Network at UConn. See Section 8.

What the regional service agency layer is focused on

Connecticut has a regional layer, so this profile carries the third lens, but the calibration matters more than the existence. The six Regional Educational Service Centers are large, solvent, statutory bodies whose center of gravity is operating schools rather than channeling purchases. CREC alone ran roughly $437 million through its books in FY2025, about $229 million of it in 16 interdistrict magnet schools serving more than 9,000 students, and took a new executive director in January 2026. Their focus is magnet enrollment and the magnet funding formula, special education programs and outplacement alternatives, and statewide professional learning including a K-12 AI micro-credential.

What they are not focused on is technology procurement. The six run one shared cooperative purchasing program rather than six competing ones, membership costs $100 a year, and its visible bid activity is paper, food service, custodial, and facilities with a thin technology edge. Critically, no state aid incentive exists: Conn. Gen. Stat. 10-66j pays the RESC by a fixed formula unrelated to what member districts buy, so a district that buys a laptop through a RESC and one that buys it direct receive identical state aid. There is no Connecticut analogue to New York's BOCES Aid. The right calibration is the Massachusetts collaborative pattern, strong on services and weak on technology purchasing, with a Connecticut amplifier: these are also enormous operators of schools in their own right. Sections 5 and 7 carry the consequences.

3. What the State Is Driving

Last verified: August 17, 2026

These are the initiatives the Governor, CSDE, the State Board, and the General Assembly are actively pushing, ranked by how much they change what a district actually does. The pattern in Connecticut is distinctive and worth naming: the biggest item is a study, the second is enacted but underfunded, and the fourth is being run by a state agency that is not the education agency.

THE BLUE RIBBON COMMISSION AND THE FROZEN FOUNDATION | The largest thing happening in Connecticut education, and it binds nothing until January 15, 2027.

  • What the Governor asked for: The February 4, 2026 State of the State announced a Blue-Ribbon Commission on K-12 Education Funding and Accountability with three charges: an updated funding strategy including a review of the foundation grant, building on the 2025 special education reforms, and consolidating overhead functions across what he counted as 207 school districts. The consolidation framing is back-office sharing, not district mergers, the politically survivable version after the 2019 regionalization fight.

  • Who is running it and when it reports: Established by executive order in April 2026, members appointed May 18, first meeting June 4. Chaired by Natalie Wagner, the Governor's Deputy Chief of Staff, with Commissioner Russell-Tucker as vice chair; that a governor's staffer chairs it rather than the Commissioner tells you where education policy sits here. The report is due January 15, 2027, after the election and just before the long session that writes the next biennial budget.

  • What the 2026 session did instead: It left the formula and the $11,525 foundation untouched and layered two new grants on top. Supplemental Education Aid pays each town the greater of what it would gain if the foundation were $13,087 or a 2% increase, and District Relief and Compensatory Use Learning Aid tops every town up to at least 4% of its ECS grant. Roughly $173 million to districts. The aid does not count toward Minimum Budget Requirement calculations for 2027-28.

  • Why districts are nervous rather than grateful: Sustaining this in FY2028 requires a $152.2 million increase, equivalent to raising the foundation to $13,087, and the budget contains no language directing that distribution or adjusting the formula. Meanwhile the eight-year phase-in concluded in FY2026, the one year the formula was fully funded as written, and overfunded towns are held harmless with reductions now postponed across 2028 to 2034. Connecticut is funding schools through annual supplements bolted to a frozen formula, which is precisely the condition the commission exists to fix.

Source: Blue Ribbon Commission | Commission appointments | 2026 State of the State | School and State Finance Project, revised FY2027 budget | School and State Finance Project, ECS formula

SPECIAL EDUCATION COST CONTAINMENT | The Governor's stated top priority. Reformed in 2025, flat funded in 2026, and the rate-setting machinery does not switch on until 2027 and 2028.

  • What 2025 built: Public Acts 25-67, 25-93, and the biennial budget set the excess cost grant at $221,119,782 for each of FY2026 and FY2027, making a $40 million increase permanent; created the Special Education and Expansion Development grant at $30 million a year paid directly to districts; added a $9.9 million competitive incentive for in-district programs; froze private provider rates for a year; capped due process hearings at four days; and raised the developmental delay diagnosis age from five to eight.

  • Why districts still feel short: The excess cost grant is capped by appropriation rather than by entitlement. A district absorbs everything up to 4.5 times its own prior-year per-student expenditure for a given student, then should receive 85% to 91% above that, but proration cut actual reimbursement to between 66.8% and 71.5% in 2025-26 against total district requests of about $317 million. FY2027 was flat funded against an estimated $55.6 million shortfall.

  • The dates that have not arrived yet: CSDE must publish transportation billing standards by January 1, 2027, individual service rates by December 31, 2027, and rate schedules for direct special education services by January 1, 2028. The cost controls districts were promised are two budget years away, which is why the relief has not been felt.

  • The review nobody has funded: WestEd's independent review of CSDE's special education systems, presented to the State Board January 14, 2026 and covering roughly 94,000 students with disabilities, found four gaps: vision, infrastructure including the usability of the state special education data system, instructional impact against persistent workforce shortages, and dispute resolution. The Commissioner said implementation would be thoughtfully paced and phased; the concrete action so far is hiring an additional special education attorney. No dollar figure and no timeline are attached.

Source: 2025 special education funding changes | Excess cost grant analysis | School and State Finance Project, revised FY2027 budget | CSDE on the WestEd special education review | CT Mirror on the commission kickoff

THE STATE INSIDE DISTRESSED DISTRICTS | Escalating and now institutionalized. Bridgeport is a live case, Hartford is a public dispute over its own arithmetic, and 2026 added a statutory route.

  • Bridgeport, the formal case: The State Board opened state-led action in January 2025 with the Commissioner leading. A technical assistance team covering finance and human resources followed in February 2025, a forensic audit was released July 24, 2025, a bus service restoration agreement followed in August, and governance training for the local board was added. Monthly updates have gone to the State Board since, published in four languages.

  • Hartford, the arithmetic fight: In April 2026 the State Board's preliminary analysis reported a modest surplus in the current year while the superintendent projected a large two-year deficit, and published figures elsewhere ranged widely. A state financial audit is running and a consulting review attributed the gap to district decision-making and enrollment. The honest framing for a meeting is that the state and the district disagree and the audit is not finished. Do not walk in holding a number.

  • The new instrument: The FY2027 budget implementer created a fiscal intervention process and gave superintendents referendum notification authority. Timing points at Hartford, though no source connects them explicitly.

  • Why this matters beyond two cities: It changes who the buyer is. In a district under state-led action or audit the purchasing conversation includes CSDE and a technical assistance team, timelines slip, and procurement scrutiny rises. It also signals the state's posture in the next downturn: intervention rather than bailout.

Source: Bridgeport intervention | CSDE Bridgeport update, May 2026 | WFSB on the Hartford surplus dispute | Pullman and Comley 2026 session review

THE STATE AS TECHNOLOGY BUYER, IN PLACE OF A TECHNOLOGY FUND | Connecticut's answer to having no state edtech money is aggregated pricing, run out of Administrative Services rather than the education agency. Two 2026 procurements, neither with a published result.

  • Why the state moved: There is no dedicated state edtech fund, no 1:1 program, and no refresh money. The Commission for Educational Technology identified roughly $25 million in annual statewide school spending on software and computers and concluded the leverage available was purchasing aggregation, not appropriation.

  • The student information system solicitation: 25PSX0293, issued by DAS Procurement, posted February 2 with responses due February 27, 2026, for a cloud-based K-12 student information system covering implementation, integrations, and support. The stated purpose is competitive transparent pricing for schools that collectively spend more than $10 million a year here. Read the intent carefully: this is a price transparency vehicle districts may opt into, not a mandated migration. No award had been published as of August 17, 2026.

  • The computer reverse auction: DAS planned a reverse auction for staff and student computers in March 2026, built on a common device specification developed with district leaders, with manufacturer commitments to post to CTsource in April. The Commission's page still reads in the future tense and no results, vendors, or pricing had been published as of August 17, 2026. Whatever eventually posts becomes the reference point every district is shown.

  • The third leg, which is already live: The Connecticut Educational Software Hub, built on LearnPlatform and run by the Commission, is the state's vendor compliance registry, and the Model Terms of Service Addendum is the state's contract instrument. Section 4 carries the statutory mechanics and Section 7 carries the vendor path.

Source: Commission technology sustainability initiative | 2025 Commission annual report | Statewide SIS solicitation 25PSX0293 | CTsource | Commission student data privacy initiative

PHONES, SCREENS, AND THE LIMITS OF STATE AUTHORITY | The Governor asked for a bell-to-bell ban in the State of the State. The Senate killed it for a second straight year, and the State Board answered with advisory guidance on August 5, 2026. Section 4 carries the mechanics.

  • Why the state pushed it: Lamont asked for no phones bell to bell in any of our schools in the 2026 State of the State, alongside restricting minors' social media access, and the Education Committee co-chair carried it as a personal priority. CSDE's own survey of 140 districts found bell-to-bell restrictions in about 70% of PreK-5 schools and 71% of middle schools but only 31% of high schools. The high school number is the entire policy problem.

  • Why it failed, twice: HB 7009 in 2025 would have required every board to adopt a smartphone policy using CSDE guidance; it passed the House and died in the Senate. HB 5035 in 2026 was a genuine bell-to-bell prohibition; it passed the House 117-31 on April 28, 2026 and the Senate never called it before the May 6 adjournment. The stated objection both times was local control and a single rule spanning kindergarten through grade 12. Expect it back in 2027.

  • What the state did instead, and how far it reaches: The State Board adopted a position statement on August 5, 2026 strongly recommending that policies restrict personal technology from the beginning to the end of the school day across all grade bands, with required exceptions for individualized education programs, Section 504 plans, health plans, and documented needs. It is advisory. There is no adoption deadline and no reporting requirement, and the local board still decides.

  • The part that reaches beyond phones, and reaches you: The same document separates personal technology from purposeful technology, sets a developmental progression for school-provided screens that runs adult-guided in elementary, shared responsibility in middle, and student-managed in high school, asks districts to audit their ed-tech program and weigh costs against benefits, and asks that codes of conduct address unapproved AI use. It also preserves the principle that no student is obligated to own a personal device to meet educational needs.

4. The Four National Questions

Last verified: August 17, 2026

These are the four questions every state is answering, and the point of this section is comparability. Connecticut's pattern is unusual and worth stating plainly: it is among the strictest states in the country on student data privacy and among the loosest on everything else. It has a statute that voids contracts, and no phone law, no AI mandate, and no district cybersecurity requirement. Where a topic is also a state initiative, Section 3 explains why the state pushed it and this section carries the statute, dates, scope, and compliance obligations.

ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE | Advisory guidance from an agency that is not the education agency, and one binding curricular duty that arrived unfunded on July 1, 2026. Section 3 carries the state's technology posture.

  • What the guidance is, and who issued it: The state's only AI-for-schools guidance comes from the Commission for Educational Technology, inside Administrative Services rather than CSDE. It is advisory throughout, and its central recommendation is that districts should not write a standalone AI policy but should first ask whether AI actually breaks existing policy on plagiarism, harassment, bullying, and data privacy. It carries no publication or revision date and its latest statutory reference is 2024.

  • The one thing districts must actually do: Public Act 26-15, from Senate Bill 5 and signed in late May 2026, added computer science to the required public school program of instruction, and provides that such instruction shall include computer programming, artificial intelligence, and emerging technologies. Effective July 1, 2026. There is no graduation requirement, no appropriation, no compliance deadline, and no reporting duty. Districts are obligated, unresourced, and unmeasured.

  • What the state funded, and what it produced: Public Act 24-151, a bonding act, carried the AI provisions as riders: an AI in education pilot for grades 7 through 12 with grants to a small number of boards spanning rural, suburban, and urban districts, educator professional development, and a model digital citizenship curriculum whose adoption is discretionary. The pilot ran from January to June 2025 and had published no findings as of August 17, 2026.

  • Where capacity building actually sits: Outside the agency. The RESC Alliance runs a K-12 AI micro-credential through Skills21 at EdAdvance with Southern Connecticut State University, and the Connecticut Online AI Academy at Charter Oak State College lists two K-12 courses as coming soon, one for district leaders developing AI policy. The Commission has an AI and Digital Learning Advisory Council. There is no district policy mandate and no deadline anywhere in this picture.

Source: Commission guidance on artificial intelligence | Public Act 26-15 text | Pullman and Comley, developments affecting schools | CSDE artificial intelligence pilot | RESC Alliance

SCREEN TIME | Every binding obligation is platform-side and creates no district compliance work. The measure that actually reaches schools is the August 2026 guidance, and it is advisory.

  • The layers already in force: Conn. Gen. Stat. 42-528, effective July 1, 2024, requires social media platforms to honor unpublish requests within 15 business days and deletion requests within 45. Conn. Gen. Stat. 42-529a, effective October 1, 2024, requires reasonable care to avoid heightened risk of harm to minors, bars targeted advertising, sale, and profiling that furthers decisions about education, and prohibits design features that significantly increase or extend minor usage. Both expressly carve out educational platforms and learning management systems. Enforcement is by the Attorney General; there is no private right of action.

  • What Public Act 26-15 adds, and when: The 2026 online safety act layers on verifiable parental consent before a covered platform serves a minor an algorithmically curated feed, a default cap of one hour a day on that feed, a notification blackout from 9 p.m. to 8 a.m. for accounts held by people under 18, and mental health warnings with annual company disclosures. The youth social media provisions are reported to take effect January 1, 2028, AI companion provisions from January 1, 2027, and synthetic media labeling from October 1, 2026. Confirm the dates against the enrolled act before relying on them.

  • Litigation status: Nothing has been filed against Connecticut. NetChoice's litigation docket does not include Connecticut as of August 17, 2026, and no injunction is in place. Note that the parental consent and age assurance architecture in Public Act 26-15 closely resembles laws enjoined in other states, and the long runway to 2028 leaves ample room for a pre-enforcement challenge.

  • Where screen time actually touches a district: Not in the consumer statutes but in the State Board's August 5, 2026 guidance, which defines screen time as time on school-provided devices during instruction, sets a developmental progression by grade band rather than numeric caps, points districts to the boards association model policy for daily limits, and asks for an audit of the ed-tech program. Separately, Public Act 26-13 creates real district work from 2027: districts must stand up organizational accounts on a secure messaging system and report addresses to CSDE so that safety plans for students returning from extended inpatient care can be shared.

Source: CGS 42-528 | CGS 42-529a | Governor on the online safety act | State Board technology position statement | Pullman and Comley, developments affecting schools

CELL PHONES / CONNECTED DEVICES | Not law. Bills died in the Senate in both 2025 and 2026. The instrument is an advisory State Board position statement from August 5, 2026, and it reaches student-owned devices only.

  • Two bills, two deaths, same reason: HB 7009 in 2025 would have required every board to adopt a smartphone policy using CSDE guidance by the start of 2026-27; it passed the House 128-20 and was never called in the Senate. HB 5035 in 2026 was a substantive bell-to-bell prohibition for all grades from opening bell to end of day including lunch and passing time, storage method left local and special education exceptions preserved; it passed the House 117-31 on April 28, 2026 and died at the May 6 adjournment. The objection was local control. The Senate Majority Leader said it did not have the votes; the Governor said it would pass next year.

  • What the guidance actually says: Adopted August 5, 2026 and replacing the August 2024 statement, it strongly recommends that policies restrict student use of personal technology from the beginning to the end of the school day, bell to bell, across elementary, middle, and high school with no differentiation in the restriction itself. Local policies must carry clearly defined exceptions for individualized education programs, Section 504 plans, health care plans, and other documented needs. It is advisory: no adoption deadline, no reporting, and the local board decides.

  • School-issued devices are expressly outside it: The document draws a hard definitional line. Personal technology means student-owned devices, primarily cell phones but also smartwatches, tablets, and emerging technology, and that is what the bell-to-bell recommendation covers. School-provided screens are treated separately as purposeful technology and governed as a screen time question rather than a restriction. The statement also preserves the principle that no student will be obligated to own a personal device to meet educational needs, which makes the district-managed fleet the sanctioned screen in the building.

  • Where districts already are, and what the state has not paid for: CSDE's own survey of 140 districts, run between October 2024 and February 2025, found bell-to-bell restrictions in roughly 70% of PreK-5 schools and 71% of middle schools but only 31% of high schools. Be careful with third-party trackers here: some count Connecticut among the states with a restriction, and it is not one. No state funding for pouches or storage has been identified; New Haven bought pouches with local money in January 2025.

Source: State Board technology position statement | CT Mirror on the 2026 guidance | HB 5035 bill history | CSDE release on personal and purposeful technology | CEA on the technology guidance

STUDENT DATA PRIVACY | The strictest thing in Connecticut education law, and the reason the contract conversation happens before the product conversation. Non-conforming contracts are void, not voidable.

  • The statute and what it requires: Conn. Gen. Stat. 10-234aa through 10-234dd, from Public Act 16-189 and amended in 2017 and 2018. A board must enter a written contract any time it shares or provides access to student information, and that contract must contain ten specific provisions: that student information, records, and student-generated content are not the contractor's property; a deletion procedure; use limited to authorized purposes; a review and correction procedure; confidentiality and security obligations; a breach notification procedure; unavailability of the data on expiration; a FERPA compliance statement; Connecticut governing law; and severability. Targeted advertising with student data is barred outright.

  • The two clocks that trip districts up: A contractor must notify the board within 30 days for student information and 60 days for directory information, records, and student-generated content. The board must then notify affected students and parents not later than two business days after receiving that notice, and post notice on its website. Separately, the board must maintain a public website listing every such contract and post the contract, its execution date, a description, and what data may be collected within five business days of execution. The two-business-day clock is the sharpest operational duty in the statute and it belongs to the district, not the vendor.

  • The instruments, and why Connecticut is not an SDPC state: The Commission for Educational Technology publishes the Model Terms of Service Addendum, in which only the red-highlighted fields are variable, and runs the Connecticut Educational Software Hub on LearnPlatform, where vendors sign the Connecticut Student Data Privacy Pledge and receive a compliance badge. Connecticut is not a Student Data Privacy Consortium alliance state and no RESC runs a shared agreement repository. Unlike Pennsylvania, where an intermediate unit runs the alliance, or Massachusetts, where a collaborative does, in Connecticut the state runs the privacy channel directly. One addendum and one Hub registration reach every district.

  • Enforcement is no longer theoretical: On November 6, 2025 the Attorney General brought the first enforcement action ever under the student data privacy law, settling with Illuminate Education over a 2021 breach that reached 28,610 Connecticut students across six districts. Connecticut's share of the $5.1 million multistate settlement was $150,000, and Illuminate must review and conform its contracts with Connecticut districts. Districts also carry the general breach statute at 36a-701b, which has no municipal exemption and requires notice within 60 days plus two years of identity theft protection.

5. Governance & Decision-Making

Last verified: August 17, 2026

Authority flows Governor to State Board of Education to CSDE, led by the Commissioner, to roughly 200 local and regional districts. The unusual feature is how much of that chain the Governor holds. Under Conn. Gen. Stat. 10-3a the Commissioner is appointed by the Governor on the State Board's recommendation, with a term coterminous with the Governor's, and confirmation under 4-7 runs through either house rather than both. Under 10-1 the Governor appoints every member of the State Board. Connecticut is therefore a Governor-dominant state chief model, closer to Virginia than to Maryland or New York, and the Blue Ribbon Commission confirms it: the Governor's Deputy Chief of Staff chairs the state's central education initiative and the Commissioner is vice chair.

  • Leadership, and the seat nobody is in: Commissioner Charlene M. Russell-Tucker is verified in seat as of August 2026 and is the operational center of gravity. The State Board chair position is unfilled: the members page lists a Vice Chairperson, Erin D. Benham of Wallingford, and a Secretary, who is the Commissioner, and names no chair. Benham called both the January 14 and May 13, 2026 meetings to order and no election of officers appeared on the August 5, 2026 agenda. Refer to Benham as the presiding officer rather than naming a chair.

  • Election-year context, provisional note: Every one of Connecticut's education leadership positions turns on one race. The governorship is on the November 3, 2026 ballot, Lamont won the August 11 Democratic primary and faces Sen. Ryan Fazio, and independent raters had the race Solid Democratic as of late 2025. All 187 General Assembly seats are also up. Because the Commissioner's term is coterminous with the Governor's and the Governor appoints the whole State Board, leadership risk here is unusually concentrated in a single contest that is currently rated safe. Treat the personnel picture as stable and the policy picture as genuinely open, because the commission reports January 15, 2027.

  • Fiscal dependence, with a discretion twist worth knowing: No Connecticut board of education has taxing authority; every dollar comes from the town. But under 10-222 the town appropriates a total and the money is expended by and in the discretion of the board of education, which may move funds between lines. The town sets the size of the envelope and the board decides what goes in it. That single distinction is why a purchasing conversation belongs with the superintendent and business official, and a budget conversation belongs with the town.

  • The regional layer, calibrated: Six Regional Educational Service Centers created by statute in 1972 under Conn. Gen. Stat. 10-66a through 10-66l: CREC in the Hartford region, ACES in south-central, C.E.S. in lower Fairfield, EASTCONN in the northeast, EdAdvance in the northwest, and LEARN in the southeast. They are voluntary, nonprofit, fee-for-service agencies governed by boards drawn from member districts. Their weight sits in magnet schools, special education programs, and professional learning rather than in purchasing, and 10-66j pays them by a fixed formula unrelated to what members buy. Section 7 carries the purchasing consequence.

  • Legislature, and a notable absence: The Joint Education Committee is co-chaired by Rep. Jennifer Leeper of Fairfield and Sen. Douglas McCrory of Hartford, with Sen. Eric Berthel of Watertown and Rep. Lezlye Zupkus of Prospect ranking. Appropriations is co-chaired by Sen. Cathy Osten and Rep. Toni Walker. Leeper's stated 2026 priorities were raising the foundation and indexing it, a statewide phone restriction, and student teacher pay; Berthel's was literacy implementation support; Zupkus's was mandate relief. The absence worth noting: Connecticut has no identifiable education technology legislator bloc, and the 2026 session produced no AI, data privacy, cybersecurity, or ed-tech procurement legislation. The technology agenda in this state does not run through the legislature. It runs through the Commission for Educational Technology inside Administrative Services.

  • Where early childhood sits: In a separate agency. The Office of Early Childhood is not a CSDE division, and the Early Childhood Education Endowment created in 2025 has received large surplus deposits with benefits beginning in FY2028. Early childhood questions route to a different commissioner.

6. Funding & Budget Rhythm

Last verified: August 17, 2026. FY2027 adjustments in effect since July 1, 2026

  • How the formula works, in four steps: First, start with a set amount per student, $11,525, which has not changed since 2013. Adjusted for inflation it would be above $16,000, and the state's own analysts note the figure was never based on any study of what educating a student actually costs. Second, add more for students who need more: 30% extra for each low-income student, another 15% in districts where 60% or more of students are economically disadvantaged, and 25% extra for each multilingual learner. Third, work out how much of that total the town can pay itself, using a wealth measure that counts property values for 70% and household income for 30%, compared against a statewide benchmark. Fourth, the state pays the rest. Poorer towns get a bigger state share. The statutes are 10-262f and 10-262h.

  • The local approval mechanism, stated explicitly: The town appropriates the money, and how it does that is set by the town's own charter rather than by state law. The sequence is fixed: the school board sends an itemized estimate to the town's finance body at least two months before the annual town meeting, that body must respond with recommendations within ten days, and the board may reject those recommendations but must explain why in writing. Final adoption happens in April or May, and depending on the town it runs through a board of finance and then either a town meeting, a referendum, a town or city council, or a representative town meeting. Referendums are common but not universal, and in referendum towns budgets are often voted more than once before they pass. Regional districts are the one clear case: their budgets go to simultaneous referendum in every member town and need a majority in each.

  • The spending floor, and why it is sinking: State law stops a town from budgeting less for schools than it did the year before. A town that breaks the rule forfeits state aid worth twice the shortfall, withheld two fiscal years later. But eight exceptions let a town go lower, including when its state aid falls, when it closes a school because of declining enrollment, when it documents savings from sharing services with other districts, and, the one that matters most right now, when enrollment declines, measured on a five-year lookback and worth up to half the reduction in per-student spending. Alliance and low-performing districts get no exceptions at all. With enrollment falling statewide, that exception is in active use and the floor is moving down across much of Connecticut. The statutes are 10-262i and 10-262j.

  • What the 2026 budget actually did: It left the formula alone. Two new grants sit on top: Supplemental Education Aid, paying each town the greater of what it would gain from a simulated $13,087 foundation or a 2% increase, and District Relief and Compensatory Use Learning Aid, guaranteeing every town at least 4% of its FY2027 ECS grant. Roughly $173 million reached districts. Published totals differ because they measure different scopes: about $162 million is the direct municipal aid line, roughly $173 million is the districts total including both grants, and about $180 million adds charter, magnet, and agriscience supplements. Say which one you mean. The aid does not count toward Minimum Budget Requirement calculations for 2027-28 and is expendable only on authorization of the board of education.

  • The phase-in that finished, and the cliff that follows: The eight-year ECS phase-in concluded in FY2026, the first year in state history the formula was fully funded as written, at a foundation frozen for thirteen years. Overfunded towns are held harmless rather than phased down, with reductions now postponed across 2028 to 2034. Sustaining the FY2027 supplements would require a $152.2 million increase in FY2028 and the budget carries no language directing it. See Section 3.

  • Special education money, and why districts never get the full share: When one student's special education costs run very high, the state shares the cost above a threshold. The threshold is 4.5 times what the district itself spent per student the previous year, or a much lower bar for students placed by a state agency. Above that line the law says the state pays 85% to 91%. In practice it pays less, because the reimbursement is capped by however much money is in the account: when districts request more than the account holds, everyone's percentage is cut proportionally. In 2025-26 districts requested roughly $317 million and collected between 66.8% and 71.5%. A separate $30 million grant arrives in October, January, and April installments. The superintendents association asks for full funding and earlier payment, because the timing is its own cash flow problem.

  • School construction, which moved agencies: Administered by the Department of Administrative Services, not CSDE. Reimbursement runs 20% to 80% for renovation and 10% to 70% for new construction, calculated annually by statutory formula on adjusted equalized net grand list per capita, with incentive add-ons of 5 to 15 points for regional facilities, early childhood, and special education expansion. The timing rule matters more than the rate: a project receives the rate in effect for the fiscal year in which local funding authorization is secured, not when the application is filed, which is why town referendum timing drives project economics. FY2027 carried $150.6 million; priority lists go out each December 15 and applications are due June 30.

  • Where technology money lives: Nowhere in particular, which is the finding. There is no dedicated state edtech fund, no device program, and no refresh line, and no technology line items appear in the FY2027 budget analysis. Technology rides the district operating budget inside the town appropriation, plus federal Title IV-A and E-rate. The state's substitute is aggregated pricing rather than appropriation, as Section 3 sets out. One recurring gap: the Commission has documented that Connecticut schools leave E-rate Category 2 matching funds unclaimed, but that analysis dates to 2019 and no updated figure has been published since, so treat the pattern as real and the number as unavailable pending federal Universal Service Administrative Company data.

7. Procurement & Vendor Path

Last verified: August 17, 2026

  • Framework, and the number that does not exist: There is no statewide competitive bid dollar threshold for Connecticut school districts. Conn. Gen. Stat. 7-148v lets a municipality set bidding requirements by ordinance and exempt purchases under $25,000 from sealed bidding, but it is permissive, municipal, and does not by its terms bind boards of education. Conn. Gen. Stat. 10-241d adds a consultation duty rather than an exemption: a board that has gone out to bid must consult the municipality's legislative body and consider a cooperative arrangement if the town can supply the same thing more cheaply, with computers named in the statute. In practice thresholds come from local board policy and town ordinance, typically tiered around quotes in the mid four figures, sealed bids around $10,000, and board approval above $25,000, citing no state statute for the numbers. Ask each district for its policy; there is nothing to memorize.

  • What carries the purchasing load, and how that follows from Section 2: Three things, and the regional layer is the weakest of them. First, the district's own board policy, which is where the real authority sits. Second, cooperative vehicles, which travel unusually well here. Third, and newly, the state itself, through the Department of Administrative Services. The RESC cooperative is real but commodity-weighted, and there is no aid incentive pulling a purchase through it, so it does not function as the channel a BOCES or an Intermediate Unit does. When Connecticut wanted technology aggregation in 2026 it did not route through the RESCs; DAS ran the computer reverse auction and issued the SIS solicitation directly. That is the clearest available evidence of where the load actually sits.

  • Connecticut is genuinely piggyback-friendly, which is a structural advantage: Conn. Gen. Stat. 7-148v(b) authorizes a municipality to purchase from a party holding a contract with other governmental entities and expressly names regional educational service centers, regional councils of governments, other states, political subdivisions, nonprofits, and public purchasing consortia; Public Act 16-14 reinforced this for consortia available through a RESC. The Capitol Region Council of Governments publishes a usable list that runs to Sourcewell, NASPO ValuePoint, NCPA, GSA schedules, and even the Pennsylvania intermediate unit vehicles PEPPM and Keystone Purchasing Network, though parts of it are stale where cooperatives have since merged. A national cooperative award travels in Connecticut, unlike in New Jersey.

  • The RESC cooperative, sized honestly: The six run one shared statewide cooperative rather than six competing ones, with a single manager and a $100 annual membership. Contracts result from competitive solicitations, which satisfies local bid requirements. The category mix is the tell: paper and supplies, custodial, food service, athletic surfaces, industrial arts equipment, roofing, and scoreboards, with interactive classroom technology and mobile learning solutions present as categories but thin in visible activity. It is a commodities and facilities rail, not a route to a district device fleet.

  • State vehicles and registration: CTsource is the state e-procurement portal for registration, solicitations, and the contract board, with BizNet handling business registration and compliance filings. Whether a given statewide contract is open to school districts is determined contract by contract through its additional purchasers language, so read the award document rather than assuming. Connecticut runs a small and minority business enterprise set-aside program through Administrative Services with compliance oversight at the Commission on Human Rights and Opportunities; confirm current goal percentages before bidding state work.

  • The compliance layer, which is the real gate: For anything touching student data the Model Terms of Service Addendum is effectively part of the procurement package and only its red-highlighted fields are negotiable, because a contract lacking the provisions required by 10-234bb is void. Register the product on the Connecticut Educational Software Hub and sign the state privacy pledge; registration is free and, given 563 compliant products out of 6,710 tracked across 75 districts, being on the compliant side is a genuine differentiator. The board is the statutory duty holder and owns the five-business-day public posting, so expect the contract to become public.

  • Two live state procurements worth tracking: Solicitation 25PSX0293 for a statewide student information system closed February 27, 2026 with no award published as of August 17, 2026, and the computer reverse auction planned for March 2026 had produced no posted vendor commitments by the same date. Both are price transparency vehicles districts may use rather than mandates, but whatever pricing eventually posts to CTsource becomes the reference point in every Connecticut budget conversation, which makes the outcome worth watching whether or not you are in it.

8. Technology & Digital Learning Posture

Last verified: August 17, 2026

  • Devices: No statewide 1:1 mandate, no device standard, no state device fund, and no refresh program. Districts built fleets independently, accelerated by pandemic relief that has expired, and the Everybody Learns initiative that delivered Chromebooks and hotspots in 2020 is defunct. The federal Digital Equity Program was terminated in May 2025, costing Connecticut roughly $29 million that would have supported device distribution, broadband access, and digital navigators. What the state offers instead is aggregated pricing through the DAS reverse auction, built on a common device specification developed with district leaders, whose results had not posted as of August 17, 2026.

  • Assessment infrastructure, which is stable and worth knowing anyway: Smarter Balanced in ELA and mathematics for grades 3 through 8, the Next Generation Science Standards assessment in grades 5, 8, and 11, the Connecticut SAT School Day in grade 11 serving as the federal high school accountability measure, alternate assessments, LAS Links for English language proficiency, the physical fitness assessment, and the kindergarten entrance inventory. Delivery runs on the Cambium Assessment platform. No vendor change, platform migration, or assessment redesign surfaced in 2026; a new 2026-27 calendar with adjusted testing windows is published. Unlike Virginia, where the assessment rebid is the dominant technology story, Connecticut's assessment stack is not the thing in motion.

  • The shared infrastructure layer is a network, not a service center: The Connecticut Education Network is the statewide fiber research and education network, a joint venture of the state and the University of Connecticut with UConn managing technical operations and the Commission for Educational Technology setting policy. Roughly 98% of public school districts use it as their internet provider, alongside colleges, libraries, municipalities, and state agencies. It is a not-for-profit cost recovery cooperative, so describe it as members buying below commercial rates rather than as free state broadband. Services run well past connectivity into managed firewalls, endpoint security, web filtering, and distributed denial of service mitigation. In most states the regional service agency is the shared technology layer; in Connecticut it is CEN.

  • Statewide platforms, and one clear absence: The Connecticut Educational Software Hub on LearnPlatform carries privacy compliance, GoOpenCT is the open educational resources portal, and researchIT CT, findIT CT, and eGO CT provide statewide databases, library catalog, and eBook access. There is no state-provided learning management system, which is a real contrast with Virginia's statewide Canvas instance and means the LMS decision is genuinely local here. Roughly 85% of districts use online courseware for credit recovery and course expansion. The Commission also runs a rapid-cycle evaluation partnership with a platform vendor and a small group of districts to measure whether ed-tech is moving outcomes, which fits the state's current posture of scrutiny.

  • Cybersecurity: high awareness, zero mandate: There is no district cybersecurity plan requirement, no incident reporting clock, no certification, no maturity assessment, and no insurance requirement. Senate Bill 403 in 2026 would have built a framework with incident notification and minimum safeguards and died in committee in March. The state chief information security officer has said publicly that the state lacks authority to compel local action. What exists is capacity rather than compliance: CEN handles hundreds of denial of service attacks annually for members and offers emergency crisis support, and the federal State and Local Cybersecurity Grant Program runs through emergency management, though that program faces a September 30, 2026 lapse with a House-passed reauthorization and a much shorter Senate proposal unreconciled. The scar tissue is real: the PowerSchool breach reached about 70% of districts, New Haven lost $6 million to a business email compromise, and Manchester lost $180,000 to a fraudulent vendor payment. The security conversation here is risk-led, not compliance-led, which is the opposite of Maryland.

  • AI capacity building: Distributed and mostly outside the education agency. The Commission for Educational Technology holds the guidance and has stood up an AI and Digital Learning Advisory Council. The RESC Alliance runs a K-12 AI micro-credential delivered through Skills21 at EdAdvance with Southern Connecticut State University. The Connecticut Online AI Academy at Charter Oak State College offers Google AI courses and lists two K-12 courses as coming soon, one aimed at district leaders writing AI policy. CSDE's 2025 AI pilot ran in a small group of districts and has published no findings. See Section 4.

  • Framing: Connecticut frames educational technology through a data privacy and cost lens, and since August 2026 increasingly through a student wellbeing lens. That is unusual. The state's most demanding instrument is a contract, its most recent major action asks districts to audit and justify the software they already own, and its largest technology moves in 2026 were procurements aimed at lowering unit price. There is no instructional expansion agenda coming from the state, no compliance mandate to sell against, and no state money. The live question here is value and stewardship, not adoption.

9. Instructional Priorities & Mandates

Last verified: August 17, 2026

  • Literacy is the dominant instructional mandate, and it has a date on it: The Right to Read law, enacted in the June 2021 special session as part of Public Act 21-2 and codified principally at Conn. Gen. Stat. 10-14hh, requires districts to use an approved evidence-based K-3 reading curriculum or compendium. Nine standalone programs are approved alongside nine compendium models, and CSDE states that all are approved for use until June 30, 2027. That is a firm expiry inside the coming school year, and every district on the list faces a re-adoption decision before it. Because the law is often described loosely as a 2023 measure, cite the act and the statute rather than a year.

  • The 2026 literacy bill was softened, and that is the story: Senate Bill 220 as introduced would have required individual reading plans for grades 4 through 9 students not meeting annual standards, reaching roughly half of Connecticut students. Commissioner Russell-Tucker testified that districts would likely require significant new resources not included in the Governor's budget, and the superintendents association called it considerable financial and operational strain. What became Public Act 26-149 instead requires CSDE to develop guidance on multitiered systems of support for struggling readers, phased through 2030, with no district compliance deadline. Separately, $2 million funded a K-3 literacy coaching network, below the $4.5 million proposed.

  • Computer science became required six weeks ago, quietly: Public Act 26-15 added computer science to the required program of instruction effective July 1, 2026, and provided that it shall include computer programming, artificial intelligence, and emerging technologies. There is no graduation requirement, no appropriation, no deadline, and no reporting duty. The act also renamed the computer science education account to add workforce development, with no funds appropriated to it. Districts carry a curricular obligation with no measurement and no money, which is the most defensible non-product opening on AI in this state.

  • Accountability is quiet, and its public page is not current: The Next Generation Accountability System remains the twelve-indicator framework and no 2026 legislation changed it. Be careful with the CSDE accountability page, whose most recent substantive content refers to 2020-21; current data lives on EdSight. Note the calendar: the prior year's assessment and chronic absenteeism results landed in late August 2025 and the 2025-26 results had not been released as of August 17, 2026, so expect them in the same window as early fall meetings. Chronic absenteeism had fallen for two consecutive years, and the Learner Engagement and Attendance Program home visiting model remains an official focus area, though no current appropriation for it appears in the FY2027 budget documents.

  • Graduation and mathematics: Graduation runs on a 25-credit mastery-based framework, and no 2026 change surfaced; the most cited mastery-based diploma work in the state dates to 2019. The live curricular deadline is mathematics: Public Act 26-139 requires a mathematics pathway redesign by January 1, 2027, which will pull on instructional materials without a state adoption list of the kind literacy has.

  • CTE and workforce, including a state-run district: The Connecticut Technical Education and Career System operates 17 technical high schools plus a technical education center with roughly 11,200 full-time students, funded through the state education budget and governed by an eleven-member board appointed by the Governor rather than by a local board. Treat it as a single statewide buyer that behaves like a mid-size district with state procurement rules, not as 17 schools. Note one wrinkle to confirm before relying on it: the system's own materials describe its board as advisory, so establish who signs. Elsewhere, Public Act 26-18 adds dual and concurrent enrollment coordination and tracking from January 2027, and Public Act 26-21 provides free transit passes for grades 9 through 12 from July 2027.

10. Landscape & Pressures

Last verified: August 17, 2026

  • Enrollment decline is the structural fact underneath everything else: Fall 2025 membership fell below 500,000 for the first time in roughly two decades, to approximately 498,000 on analysis of CSDE EdSight data. The School and State Finance Project describes 2025-26 as the largest decline since the start of the pandemic and attributes it to falling birth rates, growing interest in private and home schooling, and federal immigration policy. CSDE published no enrollment release for 2025-26, so pull the October 1 count from EdSight before quoting it. The shape matters more than the total: kindergarten has fallen by roughly a fifth over fifteen years while grade 12 is nearly flat, a pipeline problem rather than a blip, and multilingual learner enrollment reversed a long climb this year. New Haven closed four schools and still faced a gap.

  • Teacher workforce: CSDE designated eleven shortage areas for 2025-26, including bilingual education, special education, mathematics, science, world languages, technology education, school psychologists, and library media specialists. The 2026-27 list had not been posted as of August 17, 2026, so do not cite one. Public Act 26-12 revised teacher tenure, eliminated union service fees, added enhanced workers compensation for assaulted school employees, and created an externship pilot. The budget funded $2 million for roughly 75 teacher apprentices a year; student teacher stipends failed. WestEd named special education workforce shortages as a core finding.

  • Facilities: School construction runs through Administrative Services with wealth-adjusted reimbursement and a December priority list cycle, and the FY2027 budget carried $150.6 million. The consequence for technology is the timing rule described in Section 6: because a project locks its reimbursement rate in the fiscal year local funding authorization is secured, network and cabling work folded into an approved building project is both the most reliable way infrastructure gets funded and the reason town referendum timing is a technology question, not just a facilities one.

  • Choice pressure is entirely inside the public system, and it is in conflict with district finance: Connecticut has no voucher and no education savings account. The competitive pressure is interdistrict magnet schools, Open Choice, and roughly twenty charters, shaped by the Sheff v. O'Neill settlement that remains in force after its 2022 final agreement; FY2027 carried about $332 million for magnets and $147 million for charters. The live conflict is sharper than the numbers: in February 2026 Hartford hired a firm to recruit students back to the district, including students attending Sheff-related magnet schools, and CSDE has said such efforts are inconsistent with the state's obligation to ensure meaningful access to interdistrict choice. Magnet waitlists may make the savings illusory. A desegregation settlement and a city's fiscal survival are pulling against each other.

  • Federal climate: Less about formula funds and more about specific programs. The Digital Equity Program termination in May 2025 removed roughly $29 million, and the State and Local Cybersecurity Grant Program faces a September 30, 2026 lapse with reauthorization unresolved between the chambers. Immigration enforcement is a documented factor in both attendance and multilingual learner enrollment, and Public Act 26-14 responded by limiting peace officer detention in schools without a judicial warrant. No withholding of Connecticut's Title or IDEA formula funds is documented in current sources.

How to think about Connecticut right now:

  • Everything points at January 15, 2027, and saying so out loud is the opening: The funding formula is formally under review by a commission chaired from the Governor's office, this year's money is one-time with a $152.2 million cliff behind it and no distribution language written, and the report lands after the election and just before the session that writes the next biennial budget. Superintendents know this and are planning around it. Asking how a district is bridging FY2028 is a more useful question than anything about product, and it is the question they are already asking each other.

  • The privacy agreement is the door, and Connecticut's version has teeth other states' do not: A contract lacking the provisions required by 10-234bb is void. The state Model Terms of Service Addendum is the instrument, only its red fields are negotiable, the board is the statutory duty holder, and the board must post the contract publicly within five business days. Register on the Connecticut Educational Software Hub and sign the pledge before any district conversation; the Commission reports 563 compliant products out of 6,710 tracked, so this is both cheap and genuinely differentiating. Since the November 2025 Illuminate settlement, the first ever enforcement action under the law, districts ask sharper questions, and the two-business-day parent notification clock sits with them, not with you.

  • The state, not the regional layer, is the technology channel, and the big buyers are districts: Connecticut has no BOCES-style aid incentive, so a purchase through a RESC costs a district exactly what it costs direct. When the state wanted technology aggregation in 2026 it ran the computer reverse auction and the SIS solicitation out of Administrative Services, and it runs the privacy registry there too; neither procurement has published a result, so find out where they stand before quoting anything, because whatever posts to CTsource becomes the price every superintendent has seen. Doug Casey at the Commission is the most useful technology contact in the state. Two large buyers are themselves districts rather than channels: CREC, at roughly $437 million with 16 magnet schools and a new executive director since January 2026, and the state-run technical system, 17 schools and about 11,200 students with central procurement in Hartford, where the board is described in its own materials as advisory so confirm who signs.

  • Budget empathy here means knowing the charter, not the formula: Boards have no taxing authority. The town appropriates a bottom line and the board then spends it at its own discretion under 10-222, which is a real distinction worth respecting in both directions. How the town gets to that number varies by charter: board of finance, town meeting, referendum, council, or representative town meeting, in April or May, with re-votes routine where budgets fail. Regional districts need simultaneous majorities in every member town. And the Minimum Budget Requirement floor is sliding, because towns are using the enrollment-decline exception. Ask which mechanism a town uses and when it votes before assuming anything about timing.

  • The educator-first opening on AI is the gap the state left, not a product: There is no district AI policy mandate, no deadline, and no reporting. The state's own guidance advises districts against writing a standalone AI policy and is anchored to 2024. Meanwhile Public Act 26-15 made computer science including artificial intelligence part of the required program of instruction on July 1, 2026 with no money, no deadline, and no measurement. That combination, a curricular obligation with no support and guidance that has not been refreshed, is a real problem for a superintendent and an honest place to be useful. The capacity building that exists runs through the RESC Alliance micro-credential and Charter Oak, not through CSDE.

  • Read the room on screen time before talking about screens: The State Board's August 2026 guidance asks districts to audit their educational technology program and weigh the costs of digital tools against their benefits, and the teachers association endorsed it enthusiastically. Connecticut has no phone law because the Senate twice declined to override local control, so every district is writing its own policy this fall with state encouragement and no state requirement. Helping a district think through a defensible personal-device policy and a purposeful-use progression is timely. Arriving with an expansion argument is not.

  • Security is a risk conversation, because there is nothing to comply with: No plan mandate, no reporting clock, no certification, and the 2026 cybersecurity bill died in committee. But the PowerSchool breach touched roughly 70% of districts, New Haven lost $6 million, and the federal grant program lapses September 30, 2026. CEN carries more of the security load than most state networks do, so know what a district already gets from CEN before proposing anything adjacent. Capacity and incident readiness land here; compliance relief does not, because there is no compliance to relieve.

  • Small-state dynamics cut both ways: With roughly 200 districts, no functioning CTO association, and a single quarterly advisory council where state technology policy is actually shaped, a small number of relationships reach a large share of the state. The three fall events at two hotels cover the whole audience. But there is no single purchasing channel to unlock, so coverage still has to be planned district by district, with the Alliance districts and the two systems under state attention needing a different approach from the wealthier shoreline and Fairfield County towns.
