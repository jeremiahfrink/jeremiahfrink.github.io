1. Snapshot

Last verified: August 2026

Leadership & Governance
Governor Mike DeWine (R), term-limited. Open seat on November 3, 2026: Vivek Ramaswamy (R) vs. Dr. Amy Acton (D), polling as a dead heat in mid-2026
Director, Dept. of Education and Workforce Stephen D. Dackin, appointed November 2023, Senate-confirmed December 2023. Verified in seat August 2026. This is the operational chief of Ohio K-12
State chief selection Appointed by the Governor and Senate-confirmed. DEW is a cabinet agency, a 2023 change (see Section 3)
Superintendent of Public Instruction Dr. Philip H. Wagner, selected March 9, 2026, in office late July 2026. Sits under the State Board and handles educator licensure and conduct, not operations
State Board of Education 19 members: 11 elected from education districts plus 8 gubernatorial appointees. President: Paul LaRue. Post-2023 its remit is licensure, educator discipline, and territory transfers
Local control Strong. 606 districts with their own voter-approved taxing authority. No city or county appropriates district budgets, though county budget commissions gained new levy-trimming power in 2026
Scale
School districts 606 traditional districts, plus 339 community (charter) schools and 8 STEM schools
Students / schools 1,588,218 public students (2024-25); about 1.46 million in traditional districts. 2,971 traditional public buildings, roughly 100,600 teachers
Regional layer 51 Educational Service Centers and 16 Information Technology Centers under the Management Council of the Ohio Education Computer Network
Largest districts Columbus City (46,054), Cincinnati (34,390), Cleveland Metropolitan (33,399), Olentangy Local (23,232), South-Western City (21,952). Two Columbus-area suburbs now outrank Toledo and Akron
Money & Calendar
Funding formula Fair School Funding Plan (Cupp-Patterson, 2021): base cost built bottom-up from real district inputs, local share blending property wealth and income, money following the student. Base cost inputs are frozen (see Section 4)
Budget cycle Biennial, odd years. State fiscal year begins July 1. FY2026-27 biennium in effect, enacted as House Bill 96 on June 30, 2025
Current state budget $8.22 billion FY26 and $8.31 billion FY27 in foundation funding to traditional districts. Policy Matters Ohio puts the shortfall against full plan implementation at $2.86 billion
Legislative session Year-round. 136th General Assembly, Republican supermajorities in both chambers above the three-fifths override threshold
District budget rhythm Five-year forecast due November 30 and updated by May 31 (OAC 3301-92-04). Treasurer must certify funds before a contract is valid. Everything else is driven by levy elections
Local approval mechanism Voter-approved levies. Elections in May and November only; August specials are now limited to districts in fiscal emergency (ORC 3501.022)
Quick Yes/No
State AI guidance for schools? YES, and it is a mandate. ORC 3301.24 required a DEW model policy by December 31, 2025 and district adoption by July 1, 2026. Ohio was first in the nation (see Section 2)
Cell phone restriction? YES, a full ban. ORC 3313.753 as amended by HB 96 requires policies prohibiting all cell phone use during the instructional day, compliance deadline January 1, 2026 (see Section 2)
Screen time action? MIXED and unsettled. The 2023 Social Media Parental Notification Act was permanently enjoined in April 2025, then the Sixth Circuit vacated the injunction on June 18, 2026. A rehearing petition is pending (see Section 2)
Student data privacy law? PARTIAL. No SOPIPA-style vendor statute. ORC 3319.321 covers records; Senate Bill 29 (ORC 3319.325 to .327) adds vendor data rules and an unusually strict device monitoring ban (see Section 2)
SDPC / standard NDPA state? Not confirmed. No Ohio SDPC alliance or Ohio-specific NDPA could be verified. DPA practice runs through ITC and Management Council agreement libraries, which is more fragmented
1:1 / state device program? No state 1:1 mandate and no device program. The only recurring state technology money is the K12 Network Subsidy at roughly $2,000 per building per year, tied to E-rate filing
ESA / voucher program? YES, universal. EdChoice Expansion has no income cap, with roughly 143,500 students across the two main programs and about $1 billion a year. It was ruled unconstitutional in June 2025 and is on appeal (see Section 8)
Collective bargaining YES, full public-sector bargaining under ORC Chapter 4117, and teachers may lawfully strike after the impasse process with 10 days written notice

2. Hot Topics: The Four Questions of 2026

Last verified: August 2026

Ohio moved earlier and harder than most states on two of these four: it was the first state to require every district to adopt an AI policy, and it banned phones outright. Its social media law has been in court for two and a half years, and its privacy framework is light except in one very specific place.

ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE | First state in the nation to mandate district AI policies. Deadline already passed.

  • The mandate: ORC 3301.24, enacted by House Bill 96 and effective September 30, 2025, required DEW to develop a model AI policy by December 31, 2025 and required every district, community school, and STEM school to adopt an AI use policy by July 1, 2026. Districts could adopt the state model verbatim or write their own. Ohio was first in the country to impose this.

  • The model policy is published and it is commercially relevant: DEW released it in late December 2025, developed with the Ohio AI in Education Coalition. Its eleven sections include AI Literacy, Data Privacy and Security, Procurement and Evaluation of AI Tools, Ethical Use, and Academic Integrity. The vendor clause requires all third-party AI providers to comply with district standards and with state and federal law for data protection, ethical use, and accessibility.

  • Where compliance actually stands: Unknown. No source tracks district-level adoption and DEW has not published compliance rates or an audit posture, though the deadline passed six weeks before this profile was written. Treat adoption as uneven. The honest discovery question: did you take the state model or write your own, and who owns it now.

  • Also worth knowing: House Bill 594 would require one unit of computer science for graduation, argued explicitly on AI-literacy grounds, and is pending with no hearings held. Several other AI bills (a state AI commission, self-harm guardrails, watermarking) are also pending. District sentiment is skeptical rather than enthusiastic: Ohio Capital Journal ran a piece on August 13, 2026 on schools struggling to figure out what AI is worth buying.

Source: ORC 3301.24 | DEW AI in Ohio's Education | DEW model AI policy

SCREEN TIME | One law, enjoined, then revived on appeal in June 2026. Enforcement status genuinely unsettled.

  • The law: The Social Media Parental Notification Act, enacted in House Bill 33 in 2023, requires social media operators to obtain verifiable parental consent before a user under 16 creates an account. It is a platform obligation, not a school mandate.

  • The litigation, which is the whole story: NetChoice sued in January 2024 and won a preliminary injunction that February. On April 16, 2025 Judge Marbley granted summary judgment for NetChoice and entered a permanent injunction on First Amendment grounds. Ohio appealed, and on June 18, 2026 a divided Sixth Circuit panel upheld the law and vacated the injunction, treating the statute as a parental consent requirement rather than an access restriction.

  • Where it stands today: Contested. NetChoice petitioned for rehearing en banc on July 16, 2026 and that petition is undecided as of August 15, 2026, and the mandate must return to the district court before the order is formally lifted. Do not describe this law as either in force or blocked without that caveat.

  • What else is moving: Senate Bill 167 would shift the mechanism to app-store age verification, designed to survive the problems that sank the 2023 law. Senate Bill 379, Hailey's Law, would require age verification on online gaming sites and add grooming awareness education to required school curriculum, the direct K-12 hook. Both pending, neither enacted.

Source: NetChoice v. Yost | Sixth Circuit decision coverage | SB 167 coverage

CELL PHONES / CONNECTED DEVICES | Full ban during the instructional day. Compliance deadline was January 1, 2026, mid-year.

  • Two steps, not one: House Bill 250 (2024) required every district to adopt a cell phone policy by July 1, 2025, but not necessarily a ban. DeWine called for a full ban in April 2025 and it was folded into House Bill 96 and signed June 30, 2025. The operative citation is ORC 3313.753 as amended, effective September 30, 2025, with parallel provisions for community schools (3314.03) and STEM schools (3326.11).

  • Get the date right: Boards must adopt a policy prohibiting all cell phone use during the instructional day, and the compliance deadline was January 1, 2026, mid-school-year rather than the start of 2025-26. Many districts implemented early and voluntarily, but the legal deadline was January.

  • Scope is effectively bell to bell: The instructional day is defined broadly to include transitions between classes, recess, meal periods, and field trips. The device definition covers any battery or electrically powered device capable of transmitting or receiving communications, which sweeps in smartwatches. Exemptions cover board determination, IEPs, 504 plans, learning purposes, documented health concerns with a physician's statement, and emergency response. Policies must be posted on the district website and in buildings.

  • School-issued devices are not affected, and year one surfaced a real gap: The statute governs student-owned devices. District-issued Chromebooks, laptops, and tablets are untouched, and the learning-purposes exemption preserves them. A March 2026 University of Dayton review found more face-to-face interaction and less digital drama, but also a concrete problem: students could not receive multi-factor authentication codes for college applications, because the codes go to the banned phone. That is a managed-identity problem, not a hardware problem.

Source: ORC 3313.753 | DEW cell phones in Ohio schools | Year-one reporting

STUDENT DATA PRIVACY | Light overall, with one unusually strict rule on device monitoring.

  • What exists: Ohio has no SOPIPA-style statute binding edtech vendors, no parents bill of rights requirement, no per-contract public posting, no mandated security framework, and no Data Protection Officer requirement. ORC 3319.321 is essentially a state restatement of FERPA plus a bar on using directory information for profit-making activity. ORC 3301.0714 bars reporting student names, addresses, and Social Security numbers to the state, using a statewide SSID instead.

  • Senate Bill 29 is the real vendor statute: Enacted October 24, 2024 and codified at ORC 3319.325 to 3319.327. Education records a technology provider creates or maintains are solely district property. No sale, sharing, commercial use, or advertising. Employee access limited to official duties. Breaches disclosed to the district. All records destroyed or returned within 90 days of contract expiration. Districts must notify parents by August 1 annually of contracts with curriculum, testing, or assessment providers, and parents may inspect the full contract.

  • The provision that will surprise a vendor: ORC 3319.327 flatly prohibits districts and technology providers from electronically accessing or monitoring three things on school-issued devices: location tracking, audio or visual capabilities, and student interactions including keystrokes and browsing. Exceptions cover instructional purposes, technical support, exam proctoring, court order, stolen devices, and threats to life or safety, but written parent notice is required, within 72 hours for the warrant, stolen-device, and safety exceptions. This is more restrictive than most states and directly governs monitoring, filtering, and student-safety products.

  • The practical gate is now the AI policy: Ohio's Data Protection Act safe harbor (ORC Chapter 1354) covers businesses and state institutions of higher education but does not extend to K-12 districts. What districts actually ask for is FERPA language, an ITC-supplied or vendor DPA, SSID-only handling, and, since July 1, 2026, alignment with the district's adopted AI policy. Ohio's strongest vendor privacy lever arrived through an AI statute, not a privacy statute.

Source: ORC 3319.326 | ORC 3319.327 device monitoring | ORC 3319.321 student records

3. Governance & Decision-Making

Last verified: August 2026

Ohio restructured its education governance in 2023 and the change is now settled. Authority flows Governor to the Director of the Department of Education and Workforce, a cabinet agency, to 606 districts. House Bill 33 renamed the old Ohio Department of Education, made it a cabinet agency effective October 3, 2023, and moved nearly all academic and operational authority off the elected State Board.

  • Who does what now: DEW owns standards, assessment, report cards, funding administration, EMIS and data, federal programs, community schools and scholarships, career-technical education, and model policies including the AI and cell phone policies. The State Board and the Superintendent of Public Instruction retained educator licensure, conduct and discipline, and territory transfers. Send substantive program questions to DEW.

  • The governance fight is over: State Board members, parents, and the Toledo school board sued in September 2023, but the preliminary injunction was denied that October and on September 16, 2025 the Tenth District affirmed dismissal in Collins v. State on standing grounds, holding the loss of elected board representation was a generalized grievance. No further appeal has been verified. The structure stands.

  • November 2026 election, provisional note: DeWine is term-limited and this is an open seat: Vivek Ramaswamy (R) against Dr. Amy Acton (D), polling as a dead heat in June 2026. Because the DEW Director serves at the Governor's pleasure, a January 2027 change of administration means a probable new Director and a reset of executive priorities, and the incoming governor writes the FY2028-29 executive budget in roughly February 2027. Treat every DEW policy posture here as provisional until then. This is the most consequential leadership question in the batch.

  • HB 920 and the 20-mill floor are the whole game: Districts are fiscally independent with voter-approved taxing authority, but House Bill 920 (1976), implemented through ORC 319.301, reduces the effective rate of every existing voted levy as property values rise so the levy yields roughly the same dollars it did when approved. Operating revenue therefore does not grow with inflation or appreciation. The one exception is the 20-mill floor: reduction factors cannot push current-expense taxes below 20 mills, so districts at the floor do capture reappraisal growth. That is why floor status is the most fought-over question in Ohio school finance, and why passing a levy three years ago does not mean a district is fine.

  • Educational Service Centers: 51 ESCs under ORC Chapter 3311, with service agreements governed by ORC 3313.843. Districts with 16,000 or fewer students must align with an ESC and larger districts nearly all do, but districts may realign every two years and may purchase from any ESC at any time, so ESC agreements are not territorially exclusive. ESCs deliver special education, curriculum, professional development, technology, fiscal services, and cooperative purchasing.

  • Information Technology Centers are the technology channel: 16 ITCs coordinated by the Management Council of the Ohio Education Computer Network, a council of governments under ORC Chapter 167, serving more than 1.8 million students in over 1,000 districts. They host the student information system, run EMIS reporting, provide fiscal software, aggregate network and OARnet transit, deliver cybersecurity tooling, and operate purchasing cooperatives. META Solutions alone serves 475 districts. In Ohio the ITC is frequently the contracting party, the host, the integrator, and the purchasing vehicle at once.

  • Legislature: House Education is chaired by Rep. Sarah Fowler Arthur, Senate Education by Sen. Andrew Brenner. Money runs through House Finance (Rep. Brian Stewart) and Senate Finance (Sen. Jerry Cirino). Senate President Rob McColley led the levy-restriction override and Speaker Matt Huffman drove the property tax package. No legislator has emerged as an education technology champion; the AI work has been executive-branch driven.

Sources: DEW | State Board of Education | OESCA on ESCs | Management Council | Collins v. State

4. Funding & Budget Rhythm

Last verified: August 2026. FY2026-27 biennium in effect

  • How the formula works: The Fair School Funding Plan, the Cupp-Patterson plan enacted in 2021, has three parts. Base cost is built bottom-up from actual reported district inputs rather than a politically chosen per-pupil number, so each district gets its own. Local capacity blends property valuation with resident income. And money follows the student to the school actually attended. It carried a six-year phase-in and a commitment to refresh the input data each biennium.

  • What House Bill 96 did, and why superintendents are angry: Signed June 30, 2025, it continued phase-in years five and six but did not update the base cost inputs, which remain anchored to older cost data. The plan's own WorkGroup describes it as effectively freezing the base cost on 2021 figures, and Policy Matters Ohio calculates a $2.86 billion shortfall against full implementation. Foundation funding to traditional districts is $8.22 billion in FY26 and $8.31 billion in FY27. In place of the phase-in the budget added an Enrollment Growth Supplement and a Performance Supplement that ties dollars to report card star ratings for the first time.

  • The levy structure just got harder, twice: DeWine issued 67 line-item vetoes on HB 96, several protecting district finance. The legislature overrode veto item 66 (House 61-29 on July 21, 2025; Senate 21-11 on October 1, 2025), so from January 1, 2026 districts may no longer place emergency, substitute emergency, combined income and fixed-sum, replacement, or renewal-with-increase levies on the ballot, and existing ones cannot be renewed in those forms. Operating levies, income taxes, permanent improvement levies, bonds, and straight renewals remain. Then five property tax bills signed December 19, 2025 and effective March 18, 2026 tightened the rest: HB 186 caps revenue growth for districts at the 20-mill floor to the GDP deflator, HB 129 makes fixed-sum levies count toward the floor calculation from tax year 2026, HB 309 lets county budget commissions trim voted levy collections annually (not below 20 mills for current operating expense without district consent), and HB 335 caps unvoted inside millage growth. Estimated reductions from HB 129 and HB 335 alone run roughly $282 million in tax year 2026, rising past $500 million by 2028.

  • The May 2026 levy collapse: Only 24 of 66 school levies passed, a 36.4% rate, against 65.5% in November 2025. New levies passed at 24.0% while renewals passed at 75.0%. The structural driver is direct: 75.5% of operating levies on the ballot were new asks because the item 66 override eliminated the renewal forms districts would otherwise have used. Consequences were immediate. Parma authorized $9 million in cuts with layoffs, Streetsboro eliminated JV and freshman sports, Mentor authorized $3.4 million in cuts.

  • Calendar and the 2027 threat: Biennial budget introduced in early February of odd years, signed by June 30. The FY2028-29 process begins in early 2027 under a new governor. District five-year forecasts are due November 30 and updated by May 31, and the treasurer must certify funds before any contract is valid. Levy elections happen in May and November only, since August specials now require fiscal emergency status under ORC 3501.022. Separately, the Committee to Abolish Ohio Property Taxes failed to qualify its amendment for 2026 but is carrying signatures toward November 2027, and every CFO is planning against it.

  • Where technology money lives: The general fund carries recurring software and staffing and is the squeezed pot. Permanent improvement levies (ORC 5705.21, with permanent improvement defined in ORC 5705.01(E) as assets with a five-year-plus useful life) can fund hardware and network infrastructure but generally cannot fund salaries, consumables, or short-lived subscription software. That mismatch is the most common structural obstacle in Ohio edtech deals: a district may have permanent improvement money and no general fund money. Bond issues cover construction with infrastructure embedded, and the state K12 Network Subsidy pays roughly $2,000 per eligible building per year, conditioned on filing E-rate and taking at least 51% of bandwidth from an ITC or OARnet.

Sources: Policy Matters Ohio on HB 96 | Levy elimination override analysis | Property tax package analysis | May 2026 levy results | ORC 319.301

5. Procurement & Vendor Path

Last verified: August 2026

  • Framework, and a favorable exception: ORC 3313.46 governs competitive bidding for districts and ESCs, with the threshold floated through ORC 9.17 at $79,568 for calendar 2026, escalating 3% annually. The exceptions in 3313.46(B) matter: educational materials used in teaching, single-source acquisitions by two-thirds board resolution, energy conservation measures, and computer software and hardware for instructional purposes. That last one exempts instructional computer hardware and software from bidding outright, a meaningfully cleaner path than most states offer. Boards may still impose stricter local policies, so ask for the district's purchasing policy.

  • State vehicles: ORC 125.04(C) authorizes districts to buy from Department of Administrative Services state term schedules without their own bidding. Enrollment in the DAS Cooperative Purchasing Program is free and perpetual with no renewals, more than 2,900 organizations are enrolled, and contracts are searched in OhioBuys. Districts, ESCs, joint vocational districts, and chartered nonpublic schools are all eligible. Covered categories include IT hardware and software.

  • Cooperatives, and which ones actually matter here: ORC 9.48 lets political subdivisions purchase jointly, which is the authority the ESC and ITC cooperatives run on. The dominant vehicles in Ohio K-12 technology are the ITC-run cooperatives and OCEPC, the Ohio Council of Educational Purchasing Consortia, which is Ohio's member of the national AEPA bid and covers 45-plus commodity groups. OMNIA, Sourcewell, TIPS, NCPA, and E&I are legally available and used, but the ITC, ESC, and OCEPC layer is primary here in a way it is not in most states.

  • Registration: OhioBuys is the state supplier portal for state contracts and term schedules, with OAKS as the financial system of record. At district level, each treasurer maintains a vendor file, usually inside the ITC's fiscal system, and a W-9, certificate of insurance, and district data privacy agreement are standard. There is no statewide K-12 vendor registry.

  • Compliance layer: Lighter than New York but not trivial. Senate Bill 29 obligations (ORC 3319.326) attach to any technology provider supplying school-issued devices and creating or maintaining education records: district ownership of records, no commercial use, limited employee access, breach disclosure, and destruction or return within 90 days. ESCs and other public entities are excluded from the technology provider definition, so ESC and ITC relationships are not vendor relationships under the statute. No Ohio SDPC alliance or Ohio-specific NDPA could be verified, so ask the district or ITC rather than assuming a standard form exists.

Sources: ORC 3313.46 | ORC 9.17 threshold | DAS Cooperative Purchasing | OCEPC

6. Technology & Digital Learning Posture

Last verified: August 2026

  • Devices: No statewide 1:1 mandate and no state device program. The dominant device story is post-ESSER refresh: the 2020-2022 wave is now four to six years old and out of warranty, and it is landing at exactly the same moment as the general fund and property tax squeeze. The 2020-2021 emergency device and hotspot programs are concluded, and the Ohio Connectivity Champions service has been retired.

  • Assessment infrastructure: Ohio's State Tests are computer-delivered through a Cambium Assessment portal, covering grades 3-8 ELA and math, science in grades 5 and 8, and six high school end-of-course exams. Cambium's secure browser must be maintained on every testing device, districts pass an annual technology readiness trial, and testing windows are the peak concurrency event on a district network. That pushes districts toward their ITC for bandwidth headroom.

  • Statewide platforms run through the ITCs: There is no state-designated learning management system. What is statewide is the Ohio Education Computer Network: ITC-hosted student information systems (ProgressBook, PowerSchool, Infinite Campus), state-developed fiscal software, EMIS as the mandatory data pipeline, and INFOhio, the still-funded statewide PreK-12 digital library run as a division of the Management Council. Rostering, SSO, and integration conversations run through the ITC, not the district IT director.

  • Cybersecurity: There is no state K-12 cybersecurity mandate and no verified state cyber grant program. Capability lives in the ITC layer: the Management Council coordinates statewide security tooling and standards, offers TechGuard awareness and phishing simulation training at no cost to districts, and publishes alert levels through Ohio K12 Help. Note the countervailing constraint from Section 2: ORC 3319.327 restricts what a district or provider may monitor on a school-issued device, so security and safety tooling must map to a named exception and the parent notice regime.

  • AI capacity building: The DEW model policy is the operative state artifact, and the earlier InnovateOhio AI Toolkit appears superseded. Professional learning is delivered by ESCs and ITCs, with the Ohio ESC Association running an AI Education Project across the 51 ESCs, and DEW publishes AI integration guides for eight content areas. No dedicated state-funded AI professional development appropriation was verified.

  • Framing: Ohio frames technology primarily as compliance and infrastructure, secondarily as instructional, and increasingly as a privacy and wellbeing question. The only recurring K-12 technology money is a network subsidy tied to E-rate filing, the state technology apparatus exists to run reporting, fiscal, and SIS systems, and the named state technology staffer is an E-Rate Coordinator. The Straight A Fund, Ohio's one real innovation fund, has been dormant since roughly 2015. Conversations that lead with instructional transformation land softer here than refresh economics, cyber resilience, ITC integration, and total cost under levy constraints.

Sources: Ohio K12 Help | Management Council cybersecurity | INFOhio | DEW testing

7. Instructional Priorities & Mandates

Last verified: August 2026

  • Literacy is a statutory mandate with an approved-materials gate: ORC 3313.6028, enacted by House Bill 33 and amended effective March 20, 2026, requires science of reading instruction defined as explicit systematic work in phonemic awareness, phonics, vocabulary, fluency, comprehension, and writing. Schools may use only materials from DEW's approved high-quality instructional materials lists, three-cueing is banned by name, and implementation was required from 2024-25 for pre-K through grade 5. ReadOhio anchors the professional learning, and the state named 109 Science of Reading Champion schools in March 2026, up from 48. The Third Grade Reading Guarantee still exists but retention is no longer mandatory and parents may opt out.

  • Math is next: Senate Bill 19, signed June 24, 2026 and effective September 23, 2026, extends the literacy model to mathematics: academic intervention services requirements for reading and math, mathematics improvement and intervention plans, and an emerging high-quality instructional materials expectation in math. It amends ORC 3301.0714, 3302.03, and others. Products touching core reading or math instruction in K-5 need to be on or aligned to DEW's approved lists to be purchasable.

  • Computer science: ORC 3313.6033, effective September 30, 2025, lets any educator holding a valid K-12 license teach a computer science course after completing approved professional development, renewable every five years, with AP-endorsed programs automatically approved. That is Ohio's answer to CS teacher supply. Ohio has adopted CS standards and model curricula. House Bill 594 would make one unit of computer science a graduation requirement and is pending with no hearings held. Whether every district must currently offer a CS course could not be verified against a statute; do not assert it.

  • Accountability now carries dollars: Ohio School Report Cards use a one-to-five star rating in half-star increments across Achievement, Progress, Gap Closing, Graduation, Early Literacy, and College, Career and Workforce Readiness, with separate report card types for districts, career-technical planning districts, and dropout recovery schools. The material 2026 change is financial rather than methodological: HB 96's Performance Supplement pays districts based on star ratings, so report card results now translate directly into state dollars.

  • Graduation and CTE: Graduation requires course credits, demonstrated competency through Algebra I and ELA II end-of-course exams or an alternative pathway, and two diploma seals with at least one state-defined. Career-technical education is a co-equal DEW division, and workforce is literally in the agency's name, which is the clearest signal of state priority. Every district belongs to a career-technical planning district. ORC 3313.6031 now requires districts to grade and rank CTE courses on par with honors courses, a real status-parity change.

Sources: ORC 3313.6028 science of reading | Senate Bill 19 | ORC 3313.6033 computer science | Ohio School Report Cards | DEW career-tech

8. Landscape & Pressures

Last verified: August 2026

  • Enrollment: Statewide enrollment is declining, with roughly 1.46 million students in traditional public schools out of about 1.59 million public students overall. The divergence between urban and Appalachian decline and Columbus-suburb growth is now written into funding policy through the Enrollment Growth Supplement, which exists because districts in Delaware, Union, Licking, and Fairfield counties are growing fast while most of the state shrinks. Olentangy and South-Western now outrank Toledo and Akron.

  • Choice pressure is the dominant budget story: House Bill 33 removed the income cap on EdChoice Expansion in 2023, making every Ohio student eligible on a sliding scale. FY2026 participation runs 42,607 in traditional EdChoice and 100,941 in the Expansion, roughly 171,000 across all strands, at over $1 billion a year. HB 96 added $376 million across the five scholarship programs plus community and STEM facilities funding. Set against a formula whose base cost inputs are frozen, that asymmetry, not the property tax package alone, is the framing Ohio superintendents will use.

  • And the voucher program is in constitutional jeopardy: In Vouchers Hurt Ohio v. State of Ohio, brought by hundreds of public school districts, Judge Jaiza Page of the Franklin County Court of Common Pleas ruled on June 24, 2025 that EdChoice violates the Ohio Constitution's thorough and efficient common schools clause. She stayed the ruling, so the program continues to operate. The Tenth District Court of Appeals heard argument on May 12, 2026 and no decision had issued as of August 15, 2026. Both sides expect the case to reach the Ohio Supreme Court. Because so many districts are plaintiffs, most districts you meet are formally parties.

  • Workforce and facilities: Educator licensure and discipline now sit with the State Board rather than DEW. The clearest verified shortage workaround is the computer science professional development pathway in ORC 3313.6033; substitute teacher flexibility has been extended repeatedly but its current expiration could not be verified. On facilities, the Ohio Facilities Construction Commission administers the Classroom Facilities Assistance Program and publishes the Ohio School Design Manual, which makes it a de facto specifier for cabling, audiovisual, and network design in state-assisted projects.

  • Federal climate: In June 2026 the U.S. Department of Education announced transfers of major functions to other agencies, including IDEA administration to Health and Human Services and the Office for Civil Rights to Justice. A Senate education panel advanced bipartisan legislation to block most of the transfers on August 3, 2026, and the matter is unresolved. Ohio has neither a Returning Education to the States waiver nor Ed-Flex authority, and no state contingency or backfill appropriation was identified.

Sources: EdChoice appeal coverage | Trial court ruling | DEW enrollment data | Federal flexibility coverage

10. Key Notes for Engagement

Last verified: August 2026. WORKING DRAFT for Jeremiah's edit. Research-generated engagement judgment, not settled advice

How to think about Ohio right now:

  • Open with what they had to cut: Only 36.4% of school levies passed in May 2026, against 65.5% the previous November, and new levies passed at 24%. Districts lost the levy types they used to renew with, the state froze the formula's base cost inputs, and the property tax package capped what growth remained. Asking a superintendent about budget pressure reads as a prelude to a pitch. Asking what they had to cut, and what they protected, does not.

  • Learn the ITC before you learn the district: Sixteen Information Technology Centers host the student information system, run EMIS, provide the network, deliver cybersecurity, and operate purchasing cooperatives for more than a thousand districts. The ITC is often the contracting party, the integrator, and the purchasing vehicle at once, so a product that does not roster and integrate cleanly with ITC-hosted systems is structurally harder to sell here. OETC in February in Columbus is where that community gathers.

  • Know the difference between general fund and permanent improvement money: This is the most common structural obstacle in Ohio edtech. Permanent improvement levies fund assets with a five-year-plus useful life, so hardware and network infrastructure qualify while salaries, consumables, and short-lived subscription software generally do not. A district with no general fund room may still have permanent improvement capacity, and structuring around that distinction is often the difference between a deal and a polite no.

  • The phone ban is a tailwind, and it created a concrete unmet need: School-issued devices are untouched by ORC 3313.753 and the learning-purposes exemption protects them, so district-managed devices are now the only sanctioned screen in the building. Year one surfaced a specific problem: students cannot receive multi-factor authentication codes for college applications, because the codes go to the banned phone. That is a managed identity conversation, and the kind of practical problem an educator-first partner can help solve.

  • Read ORC 3319.327 before proposing anything that watches a student: Ohio's overall privacy burden is much lighter than New York's, which lulls vendors. But Senate Bill 29's flat prohibition on monitoring location, audio and video, keystrokes, and browsing on school-issued devices is more restrictive than most states, and exception-based access triggers parent notice within 72 hours. Anything in the monitoring, filtering, proctoring, or student-safety space must map to a named exception first.

  • Everything is provisional until the courts and the election resolve: The EdChoice constitutional case is pending at the Tenth District and headed for the Ohio Supreme Court with roughly a billion dollars a year at stake. The property tax abolition amendment moved to November 2027. Whoever wins on November 3, 2026 appoints a new DEW Director and writes the FY2028-29 budget in February 2027. Multi-year commitments will get scrutinized against all three, so structure proposals so a district is not exposed.

  • The AI policy mandate is a real opening, honestly used: Every district had to adopt an AI policy by July 1, 2026, nobody is tracking whether they did, and the state model's Procurement and Evaluation of AI Tools section is now the vendor gate Ohio's privacy law never provided. Asking to see the adopted policy is a genuine question and a natural place to help rather than sell. Note the mood: Ohio district leaders are skeptical about AI purchasing right now, not enthusiastic.
