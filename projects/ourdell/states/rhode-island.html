1. Snapshot

Last verified: August 17, 2026

Leadership & Governance
Governor Dan McKee (D), in office since 2021. On the ballot November 3, 2026 with a primary September 8 in which he faces a credible challenge from Helena Foulkes. Treat the outcome as genuinely open
Commissioner of Education Angelica Infante-Green, in the seat since 2019 and verified active in August 2026. She has just closed out the signature project of her tenure, the Providence intervention, so treat her tenure as a live question for 2027
State chief selection Unusual, and worth knowing. Under RIGL 16-60-6 the Council on Elementary and Secondary Education appoints the Commissioner with the consent of the Board of Education. The Governor does not appoint the Commissioner directly and the office is not elected
Governing bodies A Board of Education sits above two councils, one for elementary and secondary and one for postsecondary. The K-12 council is chaired by Patricia DiCenso and is the body that hires the Commissioner and approves charters
A political fact worth knowing Senate President Valarie Lawson is simultaneously president of the state's largest teachers union. That single fact explains a great deal about the direction of the charter moratorium and the funding formula debate
Local control School committees run schools but raise no money. Every dollar comes from the city or town, and in some communities voters approve it directly at a meeting or referendum (see Section 6)
Scale
Districts It depends what you count, so say which. RIDE counts 66 local education agencies: 32 regular districts, 4 regional districts, 4 state-operated schools, 1 regional collaborative, and 23 charters. A superintendent saying about 36 districts means the 32 plus the 4 regional
Students / schools About 138,000 students in preschool through grade 12, on a 2024-25 basis, down roughly 3.5% over five years and projected to level off between 135,000 and 137,000 through 2035. A statewide school count was not published in a reachable source
Regional layer Effectively none. The Northern Rhode Island Collaborative dissolved in 2023 after 36 years. One collaborative remains, in the East Bay, focused on special education and assistive technology. Nothing plays a purchasing or technology role (see Sections 2 and 7)
Largest districts Providence, Cranston, Warwick, Pawtucket, and Woonsocket. Providence alone is roughly a fifth of statewide enrollment
Scale, put plainly The whole state fits inside a 45-minute drive. State-level relationships reach nearly every district directly, which is the opposite of a long-tail market
Money & Calendar
Funding formula In place since 2010 under RIGL 16-7.2. A core instruction amount of $13,859 per student for FY2027, plus 43% more for each low-income student and 20% more for each multilingual learner in the lowest proficiency bands, split with the community by a wealth and poverty measure that ranges from about 15% to 96% state share
Budget cycle Annual. State fiscal year July 1 to June 30. The Governor files in late January, the Assembly runs to mid-June, and the FY2027 budget was signed June 12, 2026
Current state budget A $15.2 billion state budget carrying roughly $1.3 billion in formula aid, up about $38 million. High-cost special education rose to $20 million, transportation was fully funded, and school construction aid was cut to $102.9 million with $23.4 million pushed into later years
Legislative session January to mid-June. The 2027 session is where the funding formula rewrite lands, and it is the reason so much of 2026 was deferred
Local approval mechanism The school committee adopts a budget and sends it to the city or town for approval. Most communities decide at a council, but some hold a financial town meeting where voters decide on the floor and others hold a referendum. Which one applies is set by that town's charter (see Section 6)
District budget rhythm Budgets built late winter into spring, submitted to the council or the town meeting April to June. The state recalculates the formula on March enrollment data, so districts get their state number in April
Quick Yes/No
State AI guidance for schools? YES, and it says in writing that it is not a regulation. RIDE issued AI guidance on August 15, 2025 and its own page confirms districts are not required to adopt AI policies. Meanwhile the state just won $18 million in federal grants for AI tutoring (see Sections 3 and 4)
Cell phone restriction? YES, and it took effect 16 days ago. RIGL 16-21-43 required every district to adopt a policy barring student access to personal devices during the school day and to file it with RIDE by August 1, 2026 (see Section 4)
Screen time action? NO. A package of five online safety bills all failed in 2026, including one that would have regulated school-provided devices and apps. Expect them back in 2027 (see Section 4)
Student data privacy law? Partly, and the naming is confusing. There is a student data and cloud computing chapter, but the hard obligation is the general breach law, which gives municipal agencies 30 days and requires identity protection for minors until age 18 plus two more years (see Section 4)
SDPC / standard NDPA state? YES, with unusual ownership. The Rhode Island Student Privacy Alliance is run by the state ed-tech association in partnership with a Massachusetts nonprofit, not by RIDE. Membership in the association is the way in
1:1 / state device program? No statewide program and no state device funding. There is a 21st Century Technology and Equipment Fund, but it is aimed at STEM and career-technical spaces and its page had not been updated since April 2025
ESA / voucher program? None. Rhode Island had not opted into the new federal tax-credit scholarship as of early 2026 and is unlikely to. The choice pressure is charters, and the state just put a three-year stop on new ones
Collective bargaining Strong, and unusually close to the levers of power given that the Senate President leads the state's largest teachers union and its executive director sits on the council that hires the Commissioner

2. Where the Focus Is

Last verified: August 17, 2026

Two things happened in Rhode Island this summer that reset everything. On July 1 the state handed Providence schools back to the city after nearly seven years of state control, a year earlier than planned and with most of the original goals unmet. On August 1 a new law took effect requiring every district to have filed a personal device policy with the state. Underneath both sits the real fight, which is a funding formula rewrite that has been deferred to a commission reporting in 2027, and whose leading proposal already has published winners and losers by district name. The two lists below are ranked by attention consumed.

What superintendents are focused on

  • A formula rewrite whose winners and losers are already public: The full rewrite died in Senate Finance in May 2026 and was handed to a 16-member commission that meets by October 31, 2026, reports in draft on March 2, 2027, and finally on September 1, 2027. The leading proposal would lift the state share from 38% to 58% and have the state take over transportation, pensions, and special education, at about $590 million a year. It has been costed district by district: Providence gains roughly $186 million and Pawtucket $63 million, while Newport, Chariho, Westerly, and Middletown lose. Every superintendent in this state already knows which side of that line they are on. See Section 3.

  • Aid that went up while costs went up faster: Statewide formula aid rose about $38 million for FY2027. Providence still opened the year with a $16.5 million deficit, 109 positions at risk including 67 teachers, and service costs up 6.42%, driven by transportation, special education tuition, custodial, and medical benefits. That gap between a headline increase and a local shortfall is the conversation in most districts. See Section 6.

  • Enrollment decline quietly cutting both state aid and the local floor: Enrollment is down about 3.5% over five years to roughly 138,000. State aid follows students, so it falls automatically. And the law that stops a town from cutting its own contribution has an enrollment decline exception that lets the calculation be done per student rather than in total, so the local floor can fall too. Both sides of the budget shrink while fixed costs do not. See Section 6.

  • Being financially dependent on a municipality you do not control: School committees have no taxing power. In most places a council decides; in some, voters decide on the floor of a town meeting or at a referendum, which means a line item can be removed in May after the school committee approved it. When it breaks down it goes to court: Providence sued its city and won, producing a settlement in November 2024. Coventry lost its bond rating and its town council moved to take over school finances. See Section 6.

  • Two compliance deadlines that arrived while nobody was looking: The Right to Read Act's training deadline was September 1, 2025 and the enforcement tools are probationary status, a letter to every family explaining it, and non-compliance percentages published on the district report card. Separately, the readiness-based graduation requirements apply from the class of 2028, who are juniors right now, so this is the last full year to get course pathways in place. See Sections 3 and 9.

What technology leaders are focused on

  • The hardest legal clock in the state, and it lands on districts because their victims are children: Rhode Island's breach law gives a municipal agency, which includes a school district, 30 calendar days to notify, with the Attorney General and credit agencies added above 500 residents. The remediation obligation is the part people miss: at least five years of identity protection for adults, and for minors, coverage until they turn 18 plus two more years. After Providence's ransomware attack in September 2024, none of this is theoretical. See Section 4.

  • A phone law that just made district devices the only screen in the building: The law that took effect August 1, 2026 bars student access to personal devices during the school day, and it defines a personal device as one not owned or provided by the school. School-issued devices are excluded by definition. Overnight, every district that just removed phones is running on district-managed hardware only, with no state 1:1 program, no state device money, and no funding for storage. That is simultaneously a device management, filtering, and refresh moment. See Section 4.

  • Being asked to approve AI tools with nothing to point at: RIDE's AI guidance states in writing that it does not constitute formal recommendations or regulations, and RIDE's own page confirms districts are not required to adopt AI policies. At the same time the state's AI task force recommended an AI curriculum framework across public education by 2030, and on August 14, 2026 RIDE announced more than $18 million in federal grants for two projects using AI coaching and tutoring in middle-grades literacy. Technology directors are being asked to green-light AI with no statute, no required policy, and no procurement standard. See Sections 3 and 4.

  • A bill that would have regulated school technology directly, coming back in 2027: The Safe School Technology Act would have created a certification regime and barred geolocation, generative AI, targeted advertising, and addictive design features on school-provided devices and apps. It was held for further study on April 2, 2026 rather than defeated, and the software industry association filed formal opposition. It is the one measure in the 2026 online safety package that would have created real district work, and it is expected back. See Section 4.

  • No regional agency to lean on, and five assessment platforms every spring: There is no BOCES-style contract to ride and no technology center hosting anything. Districts run five separate assessment platforms from five vendors, on their own networks and devices. The one genuinely helpful state asset is a statewide protective DNS service, the first in the country, which is free and federally funded, opt-in, and of unclear continuity. See Sections 7 and 8.

The regional service agency layer

Rhode Island has no regional service agency with purchasing power, so this profile carries no third lens. The Northern Rhode Island Collaborative dissolved in 2023 after 36 years, displacing 77 students with special needs, and is still listed as active in directories, so do not cite it. One collaborative survives in the East Bay, doing special education programming and assistive technology, with no evidence of a purchasing or technology role. What fills the gap is a combination rather than an institution: EdTechRI, the state ed-tech association, holds master purchasing agreements, runs the state's student data privacy alliance with legal counsel attached, and gives technical feedback to legislators drafting technology bills. RIDE itself does the rest, running the school building authority, assessment procurement, and the statewide protective DNS deployment. In a state of about 36 districts inside a 45-minute drive, RIDE and one membership association together do what a regional agency does elsewhere. Sections 7 and 11 carry the detail.

3. What the State Is Driving

Last verified: August 17, 2026

Rhode Island's state agenda in 2026 was unusually decisive in the short term and unusually deferred in the long term. It ended its largest intervention, stopped charter growth outright, and then handed the money question to a commission. Two of the five items below are already in court or heading there.

THE PROVIDENCE HANDBACK | Done on July 1, 2026, a year early, on a 5 to 1 vote, with ten conditions attached and most of the original goals unmet.

  • What happened and when: The state took control of Providence schools in 2019 under the Crowley Act after a Johns Hopkins report. On May 26, 2026 the Council on Elementary and Secondary Education voted 5 to 1 to approve the Commissioner's transition order, and control returned to the city on July 1, 2026, one year ahead of the planned schedule. The district exits turnaround status. RIDE keeps full regulatory oversight.

  • What the record actually shows: Of 43 benchmarks, Providence met 10. Of 22 student learning goals, it met 2. Roughly one student in six meets grade level expectations, third grade reading proficiency declined against 2019, and 14.3% of juniors met math expectations, falling to 3.8% excluding magnet schools. The genuine success was attendance, where Providence cut chronic absence by more than 20 percentage points from its peak. Critics have called the early exit election-year timing. Say the record plainly; everyone in the room already knows it.

  • The conditions, which constrain the local board's agenda: The order requires a coordinator of board services, an independent governance policy review, ethics training, a city ordinance pledging future school funding, surplus district funds retained in the district, a single chief legal counsel, at least 40% of open-session board meeting time spent on student learning outcomes with published progress reports, and continued membership in the school committees association. The board is also barred from encroaching on the superintendent's operational authority.

  • What it means practically: Providence is now governed by a 10-member hybrid board, 5 elected and 5 appointed by the mayor, and it opened the year with a $16.5 million deficit, a budget of about $471 million using $3.2 million of reserves, and 109 positions at risk. The superintendent's contract expires June 30, 2027 and the board deferred renewal talks to fall 2026, so leadership there is unsettled. The city also owns a roughly $1 billion facilities plan that the state monitors but will not pay for.

Source: RIDE on the Providence transition order | Rhode Island Current on the Council vote | WPRI Target 12 on the takeover results | Coverage of the return to local control | Coverage of the Providence deficit

CHARTER CONTAINMENT | Enacted June 18, 2026 and in federal court five weeks later. A three-year stop, a lower cap, and a retroactive cutoff.

  • What the law does: H7415 imposes a three-year moratorium during which the Council may not approve new charter schools or expansions, cuts the statewide cap from 35 to 28, and applies retroactively so that no state approval or funding flows to any charter not fully approved before July 1, 2025. It passed 60 to 12 in the House and 31 to 6 in the Senate and was signed June 18, 2026.

  • Why the state pushed it: Charter aid runs through the same formula as district aid, so a student moving to a charter reduces the sending district's state money directly. With enrollment down roughly 10,000 students during the Governor's tenure and about 14,400 students in 23 charter organizations drawing roughly $202 million, the stated rationale was to study the funding formula before allowing further expansion. Note the political context: the Senate President also leads the state's largest teachers union.

  • The part that produced a lawsuit: One school's expansion, The Greene School, was carved out by amendment. Another, De La Comunidad Bilingual School, was not. On July 28, 2026 that school sued the Governor, the Speaker, the Senate President, and the K-12 Council in federal court in Providence, arguing the moratorium was politically motivated and unconstitutionally rescinded a preliminary approval, and pointing to the carve-out as evidence of targeting. It would have opened with 140 students. The case is pending.

  • What to watch: Enrollment projections published before the moratorium assumed charters would grow 10% to 15% in share through 2035. Those projections are now wrong, and the district-level enrollment planning built on them needs revisiting.

Source: Rhode Island Current on the charter moratorium | H7415 bill history | Boston Globe on the charter lawsuit | RIDE enrollment forecast report

REWRITING THE FUNDING FORMULA | The real fight, deferred to 2027. The full rewrite died in committee, a commission takes over, and the leading proposal is already costed by district.

  • What failed and what replaced it: The Education Funding and Accountability Act, a complete rewrite led by the Senate Education chair, was held for further study by Senate Finance on May 21, 2026. In its place the Assembly created a 16-member commission, the first comprehensive review in more than a decade, which must first meet by October 31, 2026, deliver an interim report by March 2, 2027, and report finally by September 1, 2027.

  • The proposal that will frame the debate: A Rhode Island Foundation blue ribbon panel reported January 5, 2026 with two scenarios. The preferred one raises the state share from 38% to 58%, has the state assume transportation, pensions, and special education, costs about $590 million a year more, saves municipalities roughly $280 million, and replaces the current wealth calculation with one based on property values. Providence would gain about $186 million and Pawtucket $63 million, while Newport, Chariho, Westerly, and Middletown would lose.

  • What actually changed for FY2027: Incremental things. Core instruction rose 4% to $13,859 per student. The low-income weight was set at 43%, worth about $5,959 per eligible student. Transportation was fully funded and high-cost special education rose $2.5 million. No structural change passed.

  • A credibility problem worth knowing about: RIDE has a recent public record of errors in formula estimates, and lawmakers held hearings on it in 2025. The FY2027 proposal was itself built on preliminary enrollment and poverty data and required substantial revision. District finance officers here are cautious about state projections, and that caution is earned.

Source: S3015 bill history | Rhode Island Current on the funding formula commission | Senate Fiscal Office education aid analysis | Senate Fiscal Office budget as passed | RIDE FY2027 formula calculations

LITERACY, BEHIND SCHEDULE AND SUDDENLY FUNDED THROUGH AI | A training mandate with real enforcement that ran past its deadline, and $18 million of new federal money that landed three days ago pointing straight at AI tutoring.

  • What the Right to Read Act requires: Enacted in 2019 at RIGL 16-11.4-6, it requires proficiency in literacy instruction for K-5 classroom teachers, reading specialists, special education teachers, and bilingual and English learner teachers, and awareness for secondary content teachers. Awareness means five free online courses totaling ten hours. Proficiency means a master's, a reading specialist certificate, 30 or more hours of coursework, or a state-adopted assessment.

  • Enforcement with actual teeth: Districts that fail to ensure completion are placed on probationary status, must send written notification to families explaining that status, and have their non-compliance percentages published on the district report card beginning in fall 2025. Teachers who refuse required training must be reassigned to roles without the requirement. Few states attach consequences this visible.

  • Where it actually stands: The deadline was September 1, 2025, already extended once by statute from the 2023-24 school year. RIDE's own page, updated July 10, 2026, still describes awareness courses as available now, which means educators were still being onboarded nearly a year past the deadline. Whether any district has actually been placed on probationary status is a good question to ask a superintendent directly.

  • The money that just arrived, and where it points: On August 14, 2026 RIDE announced more than $18 million in federal Education Innovation and Research grants for two projects, Project REACH at about $10 million and RISE-AI, both explicitly using AI coaching and tutoring to lift middle-grades literacy. That is the state's newest money and it sits exactly at the intersection of AI, literacy, and the middle grades, in a state with no AI mandate at all.

Source: RIDE Right to Read Act | RIGL 16-11.4-6 | RIDE on the federal literacy grants | Providence Business News on the AI literacy grants | RIDE artificial intelligence page

PHONES OUT OF SCHOOLS, EFFECTIVE SIXTEEN DAYS AGO | A statutory duty on every district to adopt a policy and file it with the state by August 1, 2026. Section 4 carries the mechanics.

  • Why the state pushed it: Rhode Island took the middle path between doing nothing and writing a single statewide rule. Rather than legislating what the policy must say, the state required every district to have one, prohibiting student access to personal devices during the school day and covering school grounds and school-sponsored activities, and left storage and enforcement to local judgment.

  • What districts actually did: Practice varies widely, which is what happens when the state sets a duty rather than a rule. North Providence uses locked pouches at its middle school and an out-of-sight rule at the high school and reports fewer discipline issues. Central Falls uses pouches in grades 6 through 12. Lincoln went with off and away in backpacks and lockers. Cumberland allows teacher-approved educational use on school wireless, a notably permissive reading. Some districts were still waiting on the Commissioner's guidance as the deadline approached.

  • The cost nobody funded: No state appropriation for pouches or storage was identified. Districts using them are paying out of their own budgets, in the same year most of them are cutting.

  • The consequence that matters for technology: Because the statute defines a personal device as one not owned or provided by the school, district-issued devices are outside the ban entirely. In practice the state has just made the district-managed device the only sanctioned screen in the building, without funding a single one of them. See Sections 4 and 8.

4. The Four National Questions

Last verified: August 17, 2026

These are the four questions every state is answering, and the point of this section is comparability. Rhode Island's pattern is distinctive: it acted on phones, it has done nothing binding on AI or on social media, and its sharpest obligation is not a student privacy law at all but a general breach statute that happens to be unusually expensive for schools. Where a topic is also a state initiative, Section 3 explains why the state pushed it and this section carries the rules and dates.

ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE | Guidance that states in writing that it is not a regulation, no required policy, and $18 million of new federal money pointed at AI tutoring. Section 3 carries the money.

  • What exists: RIDE issued Artificial Intelligence Guidance for Local Education Agencies on August 15, 2025. Its own text says distribution of the guidance does not constitute formal recommendations or regulations, and RIDE's AI page confirms that districts are not mandated to adopt AI policies. The closest thing to a duty is an expectation that districts communicate acceptable AI use to staff, students, and families, which is not enforceable.

  • What it recommends: Everything is framed as should rather than must: AI policies and acceptable use policies, AI task forces with diverse stakeholders, age-appropriate AI literacy, data governance plans, vendor vetting processes, and academic integrity policies. It covers instruction and academic integrity, equity and bias, security and privacy, college and career readiness, district operations, and family engagement. RIDE backed it with a six-session webinar series in October and November 2025 and even published a custom chatbot to help educators interpret it.

  • What the legislature did and did not do: No 2025 or 2026 law requires a district AI policy. A bill that would have barred generative AI on school-provided devices and apps without certification died in committee on April 2, 2026. A separate act requiring healthcare providers to disclose AI use in patient documentation did pass, which signals appetite for disclosure mandates rather than prohibitions.

  • The state-level layer above K-12: A state AI task force created by executive order released a 75-page report on January 28, 2026 recommending that Rhode Island implement an AI-focused curriculum framework across public education by 2030, incentivize vetted AI education tools, and standardize AI-use frameworks across public schools. Read alongside the $18 million in AI literacy grants, the direction of travel is clear even though nothing is binding.

Source: RIDE AI guidance for local education agencies | RIDE artificial intelligence page | Rhode Island AI Task Force | RIDE on the federal literacy grants | H7895, the Safe School Technology Act

SCREEN TIME | Nothing enacted. Five bills died in 2026, including the only one that would have created real district work. Expect them back in 2027.

  • The package that failed: An age-appropriate design code, a Children's Online Safety Act, a Social Media Regulation Act that would have barred accounts for under-18s beginning in 2027, an AI companion safety bill, and a study commission on third-party digital platforms in schools. All were platform-facing except one, and none passed. Rhode Island therefore has no current screen time or social media obligations on anyone.

  • The one that matters to districts: The Safe School Technology Act of 2026 would have applied to school-provided devices and apps, creating a certification system and barring geolocation tracking, generative AI, targeted advertising, and addictive design features on school technology. It was held for further study in House Education on April 2, 2026 rather than voted down, and the software industry association filed formal opposition, which tells you it was taken seriously.

  • Why this is worth tracking rather than dismissing: Held for further study in Rhode Island usually means returning. The bill had ten sponsors, and the state ed-tech association now explicitly offers technical feasibility reviews during policy drafting, which exists because bills like this get written without district input. If it returns in a workable form it would be the most consequential technology regulation in the state.

  • Litigation: Providence sued major social media platforms over youth mental health harms, but the available coverage of that filing appears to date to roughly 2023 and no current source states where it now stands. No current Rhode Island statute is enjoined, because there is no current statute.

Source: H7895, the Safe School Technology Act | Coverage of the 2026 online safety bill package | EdTechRI areas of work

CELL PHONES / CONNECTED DEVICES | In force since August 1, 2026. A duty to have a policy rather than a single state rule, and school-issued devices are excluded by definition.

  • The law: RIGL 16-21-43, enacted by two 2025 public acts signed June 26, 2025, effective August 1, 2026. Every local education agency must adopt a policy prohibiting student physical access to a personal electronic device during the school day, and the policy must also cover school grounds and school-sponsored activities. There is no grade limitation; it applies across all public schools. Policies had to be filed with RIDE by August 1, both by email and through a checklist form.

  • The carve-out, which is explicit and statutory: A personal electronic device is defined as a smartphone, mobile phone, tablet, computer, smartwatch, or similar device not owned or provided to a student by a public school. School-provided devices are therefore excluded from the prohibition by definition. This is the single most consequential sentence in the statute for anyone thinking about school technology.

  • Exemptions and the ambiguity to watch: Four mandatory exemptions: assistive technology required by a 504 plan or individualized education program, medical needs including glucose monitoring, language access services for multilingual learners, and emergencies. The open question is scope: the statute says during the school day but leaves the definition of school day to RIDE rather than fixing it in law, so whether a district reads it as bell to bell or as instructional time only depends on guidance.

  • What is not funded: No state money for pouches or storage was identified. Districts buying them, including Central Falls and North Providence, are paying for them locally. The enforcement burden is entirely local too, since the statute imposes a filing duty and specifies no penalty.

Source: RIGL 16-21-43, personal electronic devices | RIDE personal electronic device policy page | Valley Breeze on district phone policies

STUDENT DATA PRIVACY | The student data chapter is thin. The obligation that actually bites is the breach law, and for schools it is unusually expensive because the victims are minors.

  • Get the naming right, because three things get confused: Rhode Island's student data chapter is titled Student Data and Cloud Computing. There is separately a general consumer privacy act, the Data Transparency and Privacy Protection Act, which is not a student data law. A statute named the Student Data Transparency and Security Act does not appear in current law under that name. Cite the chapter, not a remembered title, and confirm its current text before relying on specific contract terms.

  • The clock that matters: Under the Identity Theft Protection Act at RIGL 11-49.3-4, a municipal agency, which includes a school district, must notify within 30 calendar days of confirming a breach, against 45 for private entities. Above 500 Rhode Island residents affected, the Attorney General and the major credit bureaus must also be notified. The notice must describe how the breach happened, how many people were affected, what data was involved, the dates, and how to obtain security freezes.

  • The part that makes it expensive for schools: State and municipal agencies must provide at least five years of identity theft protection for adults, and for minors, coverage until the child turns 18 plus a minimum of two additional years. A district whose breach victims are seven-year-olds is looking at more than a decade of coverage per student. This is the most concrete and costly compliance obligation in Rhode Island K-12 technology, and it became real after Providence's September 2024 ransomware attack, which included a reported $1 million demand and credit monitoring offered that October.

  • The instrument districts use, and who owns it: Rhode Island is a Student Data Privacy Consortium state through the Rhode Island Student Privacy Alliance, which is run by EdTechRI in partnership with a Massachusetts nonprofit rather than by RIDE. Joining costs association membership plus a dollar per student per year capped at $5,000. It provides standard negotiated agreements, a portal listing which vendors have signed, training, and legal counsel. Note that several of the national consortium's Rhode Island pages were returning errors in August 2026.

5. Governance & Decision-Making

Last verified: August 17, 2026

Rhode Island puts the Commissioner one step further from the Governor than most states. A Board of Education sits over two councils, one for K-12 and one for higher education, and under RIGL 16-60-6 it is the K-12 council that appoints the Commissioner, with the Board's consent. The Governor does not appoint the Commissioner directly. Below that sit roughly 36 traditional districts and 23 charter organizations, all fiscally dependent on cities and towns. There is no regional layer in between.

  • Leadership, and a genuinely open question: Commissioner Angelica Infante-Green has served since 2019 and is verified in seat in August 2026. She has just closed out the signature project of her tenure. The K-12 council is chaired by Patricia DiCenso and includes the executive director of the state's largest teachers union among its members. Treat who holds the commissionership in mid-2027 as genuinely unsettled, both because of that and because of the election.

  • Election-year context, provisional note: The governorship is on the ballot November 3, 2026, with a primary on September 8 in which the incumbent faces a credible challenge. Because council seats turn over through gubernatorial appointment over time, a change of administration would eventually reach the commissionership, though not immediately. Separately, the House changed Speakers mid-session in May 2026, so the House education agenda under new leadership is not yet settled. Treat leadership risk here as higher than in Connecticut or Vermont.

  • The political fact that explains the most: Senate President Valarie Lawson is simultaneously president of NEA Rhode Island, and that union's executive director sits on the council that hires the Commissioner. This is not a scandal; it is simply how a very small state works. But it explains the charter moratorium, the shape of the funding formula debate, and why proposals that shift costs onto districts tend not to advance. Anyone reading Rhode Island education politics without knowing this will misread it.

  • Fiscal dependence, and the court as backstop: School committees adopt budgets and have no taxing authority; the city or town appropriates. When that breaks down, RIGL 16-2-21.4 lets a school committee take its municipality to court over an inadequate appropriation. Providence did exactly that and won, producing a November 2024 settlement in which the city agreed to escalating payments and the state permitted a city audit of school finances. That pathway is live and known.

  • The regional layer, stated plainly: There is none with purchasing power. The Northern Rhode Island Collaborative dissolved in 2023. One collaborative remains in the East Bay doing special education and assistive technology. No state aid incentive rewards buying regionally. Section 7 carries what fills the gap.

  • Legislature: Senate Education is chaired by Sen. Hanna Gallo, who led the failed funding formula rewrite, with Sen. Melissa Murray as vice chair and the floor manager on the charter moratorium. House Education is chaired by Rep. Joseph McNamara. The charter moratorium was led by Reps. Mary Messier and Rebecca Kislak. On technology specifically, Rep. Justine Caldwell led the Safe School Technology Act and Rep. Teresa Tanzi has carried AI disclosure legislation. Unlike Connecticut, Rhode Island does have legislators who write technology bills, which is why the 2027 session is worth watching.

6. Funding & Budget Rhythm

Last verified: August 17, 2026. FY2027 budget in effect since July 1, 2026

  • How the formula works: In place since 2010 under RIGL 16-7.2 and built in three steps. Start with a core instruction amount per student, $13,859 for FY2027, up 4%. Add 43% more for each student from a family at or below 185% of the federal poverty level, worth about $5,959. Add 20% more for each multilingual learner in the three lowest English proficiency bands, worth about $2,772, a weight moved from a separate grant into the formula in FY2025. Then split the total between the state and the community using a wealth and child poverty measure, which produces state shares ranging from about 15% to about 96% by district. A stabilization fund partially cushions districts facing large drops.

  • The local approval mechanism, stated explicitly: The school committee adopts a budget and submits it to the local appropriating authority. Which body that is depends on the municipal charter, and the range is real. In mayor-council cities such as Providence, Cranston, Warwick, and Pawtucket, the mayor compiles and the council gives final approval. In the council-manager and council-administrator communities that make up most of the state, the manager drafts and the council adopts after a public hearing. Some towns hold a financial town meeting where electors vote on the floor. Barrington and Glocester hold town-wide budget referendums. The practical consequence for anyone selling into a district: in a town meeting or referendum community, a line item approved by the school committee can still be removed by voters in May.

  • The floor under local contributions, and the exception that is eroding it: Under RIGL 16-7-23 a municipality must appropriate no less for schools than it did the prior year, monitored by RIDE. But the exceptions matter, and one is doing enormous work right now: enrollment decline permits the calculation on a per-student rather than a total basis, so a district losing students can see its local floor fall in absolute terms. Other exceptions cover deficit reduction, non-recurring expenditures with the Commissioner's approval, and high-contribution communities in special circumstances, and districts facing unachievable budgets can request a waiver.

  • What FY2027 actually carried: The enacted state budget totals $15.2 billion. Education aid figures circulate in several forms because they measure different things, so say which you mean: roughly $1.31 billion in formula aid, up about $38.4 million, against roughly $1.35 billion counting all categorical grants. The Assembly added about $47 million on top of the Governor's recommendation, including $20.9 million from a March enrollment update, the increase in the low-income weight to 43%, $2.5 million to fully fund transportation, $2.5 million more for high-cost special education, and $14.2 million restoring construction aid.

  • School construction, where the state is stretching payments: The School Building Authority sits inside RIDE and runs two mechanisms, a housing aid program and a capital fund, entered through a necessity of school construction application. The state minimum share is 35% and the maximum reaches 95.3% for Central Falls. Voters approved $250 million bonds in 2018 and again in 2022, with no statewide school construction bond since. FY2027 construction aid fell $17 million to $102.9 million, with $23.4 million pushed into FY2028 and FY2029 through modified reimbursement schedules. Read that as the state managing its own cash flow, which changes the timing of district projects.

  • Where technology money lives: In the district operating budget, and essentially nowhere else. There is no state technology fund for general use and no device program. The one dedicated vehicle is the 21st Century Technology and Equipment Fund inside the building authority, which ran a $15 million first round in 2022 and a $3 million second round in 2025 focused on STEM and career-technical spaces rather than general computing. That page had not been updated since April 2025 and no third round was announced, so confirm before treating it as available.

  • The calendar detail that shapes district planning: The state recalculates the formula on March enrollment and student data, and the FY2027 calculation was issued April 23, 2026. That means districts learn their state number in April, after most have built their budgets, and before their council or town meeting votes in May or June. It also means RIDE's estimate accuracy matters enormously, which is why the 2025 hearings on faulty funding estimates left a mark.

7. Procurement & Vendor Path

Last verified: August 17, 2026

  • There is no statewide school bidding threshold: Rhode Island's procurement code at Title 37 governs state purchases, not school district purchases. District bidding thresholds live in local school committee policy and in municipal charters and ordinances, and they vary. Ask each district for its purchasing policy; there is no state number to memorize. Note this is an inference from how Title 37 is structured plus the existence of district-level policies rather than from an affirmative statutory statement, so verify per district.

  • What carries the purchasing load, since no regional agency does: This is the through-line from Section 2. With no regional service agency, purchasing runs through two doors. The first is the state master price agreement route under RIGL 37-2-56, which lets municipalities and regional school districts buy from state agreements. The second is EdTechRI, the state ed-tech association, which holds its own master purchasing agreements and vendor relationships for member districts. Everything else is district by district.

  • The catch in the state route, which people miss: RIGL 37-2-56 does not compel anyone. The contractor must be willing to extend its terms to a municipality when asked, and the state purchasing agent cannot force a successful bidder to do so unless the contract arose from a cooperative purchasing arrangement the local entity was already part of. Participating districts pay vendors directly rather than through the state. So a state agreement is an opportunity for a district, not an entitlement, and vendor willingness is the gate.

  • The association route, which is where technology actually happens: EdTechRI describes itself as managing master purchasing agreements and vendor relationships to streamline procurement for member districts and facilitating collaborative purchasing. It also runs the state's student data privacy alliance and convenes the cybersecurity summit. Membership is a precondition for joining the privacy alliance, which means essentially every district serious about data privacy is in the room. Note that the organization does not publish its executive director's name or its event calendar, so reaching it takes a phone call rather than a web form.

  • The privacy layer: The Rhode Island Student Privacy Alliance provides standard negotiated agreements, a portal showing which vendors have signed, training, and legal counsel, at association membership plus a dollar per student per year capped at $5,000. It is run by EdTechRI in partnership with a Massachusetts nonprofit rather than by RIDE, which is unusual and worth knowing, because the state education agency is not the counterparty. Signing the standard agreement is the practical entry requirement. See Section 4.

  • What is genuinely absent: There is no BOCES-style contract to ride, no technology center hosting student information systems, and no aid incentive that makes a regional purchase cheaper. The Northern Rhode Island Collaborative dissolved in 2023 and is still listed as active in third-party directories, so do not build a plan around it. Coverage planning here is district by district, which in a state of about 36 districts is more feasible than it sounds.

8. Technology & Digital Learning Posture

Last verified: August 17, 2026

  • Devices, and a policy that just changed the demand picture: No statewide 1:1 program, no device standard, and no state device funding. The dedicated state vehicle, the 21st Century Technology and Equipment Fund, is aimed at STEM and career-technical spaces and its page had not been updated since April 2025. What changed on August 1, 2026 is that the new phone law removed personal devices from the school day while explicitly excluding school-provided ones. Districts that just banned phones are now entirely dependent on the fleet they own, in a state that has never funded one.

  • Assessment, which means five platforms every spring: Rhode Island runs more assessment vendors than any state in this batch. RICAS in English language arts and mathematics is delivered by Cognia and is the same instrument family as the Massachusetts test, which is why cross-state comparisons keep appearing in Rhode Island political speech. The SAT and PSAT run on College Board's digital platform, with a spring administration window. The Next Generation Science Assessment runs on Cambium. English language proficiency runs on WIDA through a separate portal. The alternate assessment runs on Kite. Five vendors, five platforms, all on district networks and district devices in the same few weeks.

  • A genuine state asset: first-in-the-nation protective DNS: In December 2024 Rhode Island became the first state to deploy a statewide protective domain name service for K-12, announced by the White House cyber office, covering all 64 public school districts and about 136,000 students, free to districts and federally funded. Districts opt in by signed pledge. Two honest caveats: the vendor was not named in the announcement, and continuity of the federal funding has not been publicly addressed. Ask a technology director whether they are enrolled and whether anyone has told them what happens when the money ends.

  • Cybersecurity, where the obligation is a clock rather than a standard: There is no state cybersecurity standard for districts, no required incident report to RIDE, and no certification. The only hard duty is the 30-day breach notice, with identity protection for minors running until age 18 plus two years. The reference incident is the Providence ransomware attack of September 2024, with a reported $1 million demand, student data exposed, an initial denial, credit monitoring offered in October, and sustained criticism that families were left in the dark. Every technology leader in the state watched that unfold. See Section 4.

  • Broadband: OSHEAN is the statewide research and education network serving community anchor institutions, built out substantially under federal broadband grants. Its current K-12 district membership and whether it leads the E-rate consortium is not stated publicly, so ask directly rather than assuming it serves a given district.

  • Statewide platforms: No state student information system and no state learning platform. What the state does run is data and survey infrastructure: SurveyWorks, the annual statewide education survey, plus the RIDE data center, InfoWorks, and the public report card. Those are the tools a superintendent will cite when talking about their own performance, so they are worth knowing by name.

  • Framing: Rhode Island frames technology through a safety and compliance lens rather than an instructional one, and the pattern is consistent: a first-in-nation security deployment, an expensive breach obligation, a phone law, and a bill to regulate school-provided technology that will return. The one place the state is leaning forward on instruction is AI, and it is doing it with federal grant money rather than policy. That combination, aggressive on protection and permissive on adoption, is unusual and worth naming.

9. Instructional Priorities & Mandates

Last verified: August 17, 2026

  • Literacy is the dominant mandate and it has real consequences attached: The Right to Read Act at RIGL 16-11.4-6 requires literacy proficiency for elementary, reading specialist, special education, and bilingual and English learner teachers, and literacy awareness for secondary content teachers. Districts that fail to ensure completion go on probationary status, must write to families explaining it, and have their non-compliance published on the district report card. The deadline was September 1, 2025, already extended once, and RIDE was still onboarding educators in July 2026. See Section 3.

  • Graduation requirements change with the class of 2028, who are juniors now: Through the class of 2027 the requirement is 20 courses, demonstrated proficiency in six core areas, and one performance-based diploma assessment such as a portfolio, exhibition, senior project, or capstone. From the class of 2028 the state moves to readiness-based graduation requirements adopted in November 2022, which make college and career-ready coursework the default expectation for every student. Those students entered their junior year this fall, so 2026-27 is the last full year to get course pathways and systems in place. Rhode Island also awards a Seal of Biliteracy that public colleges must recognize for credit by statute.

  • Accountability, and where the state is honest about weakness: Schools receive one to five stars based on achievement and growth in mathematics and English language arts, science, four-year graduation rate, and chronic absenteeism. In 2025 about 60% of schools earned three stars or better, 37 improved against 24 that declined, and for the first time more than half of graduates earned a postsecondary success credential. The weakness the state names itself is achievement: 77 schools were flagged for improvement in both English and mathematics, and secondary schools lag elementary.

  • Career and technical education is the Governor's favorite story: Roughly 100 new programs and about 3,000 more students, with a proposed $50 million dedicated bond raised in the 2026 budget address. Voter authorization for that bond is not confirmed in a public source, so treat the bond as proposed rather than approved. Structurally this is smaller than the funding and governance fights, but politically it is the item the administration leads with.

  • Artificial intelligence enters instruction through grant money, not policy: There is no AI curriculum requirement. But the state AI task force recommended an AI-focused curriculum framework across public education by 2030, and RIDE landed more than $18 million in federal grants on August 14, 2026 for two projects using AI coaching and tutoring in middle-grades literacy. If AI reaches Rhode Island classrooms systematically, this is the route it takes. See Sections 3 and 4.

10. Landscape & Pressures

Last verified: August 17, 2026

  • Enrollment is falling but is expected to level off, which is unusual in this region: About 138,000 students on a 2024-25 basis, down from roughly 143,000 in 2019-20, a 3.5% decline over five years and slower than earlier forecasts predicted. The state projects stabilization between 135,000 and 137,000 through 2035, under 2% further decline. The pattern is the reverse of what many expect: urban cores including Providence, Pawtucket, and Central Falls are stabilizing or growing in the early grades on in-migration and multilingual learner growth, while rural districts decline. Note that these projections were built before the charter moratorium and assumed charter growth, so the district-level detail needs revisiting.

  • Attendance is the state's clearest success: Chronic absence peaked at 34.1% in 2021-22 and fell to 22.1% in 2024-25, the fifth largest decline in the country, with about 71% of schools improving. Central Falls and Providence each cut more than 20 points from their peaks. Two honest caveats: the rate is still above the 19.1% pre-pandemic baseline, and the district range runs from under 5% to over 40%. The state's vehicle is a campaign and partnership rather than a mandate, and the bill that would have required districts to implement attendance best practices did not advance.

  • Teacher workforce, where the data is genuinely stale: The best available specifics date to August 2024: Providence with more than 100 openings, hardest vacancies in special education, multilingual learner instruction, world languages, and secondary mathematics and science, and an average teacher salary around $79,000 against roughly $92,000 in Massachusetts. Per-pupil spending shows the same gap. RIDE began running statewide hiring fairs, which the Commissioner noted the state had never done before. No 2026 statewide vacancy count was published, so do not cite one.

  • Districts under financial strain: Providence carries a $16.5 million FY2027 deficit with 109 positions at risk. Coventry faced roughly a $5 million school deficit, lost its Moody's rating, and its town council moved in March 2025 to take control of school department finances, with a deficit reduction plan still under development. Woonsocket confirmed layoffs through its budget. In a state this small, three districts in distress is a meaningful share of the market.

  • Federal climate: Mixed rather than uniformly negative. The state just won more than $18 million in federal innovation grants for literacy and AI. Against that, the statewide protective DNS service is federally funded with no publicly stated continuity plan, and a separate state social services platform breach affecting more than 709,000 people shaped statewide political urgency about cybersecurity in ways that reach schools. Rhode Island had not opted into the new federal tax-credit scholarship as of early 2026, and given a charter moratorium and the current legislative leadership, opting in looks unlikely.

12. Key Notes for Engagement

Last verified: August 17, 2026. WORKING DRAFT for Jeremiah's edit. Research-generated engagement judgment, not settled advice

How to think about Rhode Island right now:

  • This is a state-level market, not a district-level one, and that changes the whole approach: About 36 traditional districts inside a 45-minute drive, one association that technology directors actually belong to, one state agency that does what a regional service agency does elsewhere, and a council meeting monthly that appoints the Commissioner and approves charters. A handful of relationships reaches nearly everyone. The flip side is that there is no purchasing channel to unlock and reputation travels instantly.

  • EdTechRI is the door, and it takes a phone call: It holds the master purchasing agreements, it runs the student data privacy alliance in partnership with a Massachusetts nonprofit, and membership in it is a precondition for joining that alliance, so essentially every district that takes privacy seriously is a member. It does not publish its executive director's name or its calendar. That is one call, and it is the highest-value call on this list.

  • Lead with the breach obligation, because it is the sharpest thing any technology director carries: Thirty calendar days to notify, the Attorney General above 500 residents, and identity protection for minors until they turn 18 plus two more years. A district whose breach victims are second graders is looking at more than a decade of coverage per child. Providence lived this in September 2024. Being fluent in that obligation, and in what a vendor does to reduce the district's exposure to it, is worth more here than any product conversation.

  • The phone law just made the district fleet the only screen, and nobody funded it: The law took effect August 1, 2026 and excludes school-provided devices by definition. Every district that removed personal phones is now fully dependent on hardware it owns, in a state with no 1:1 program, no device money, and a technology fund whose page has not been touched since April 2025. That is a real and immediate management, filtering, and refresh conversation, and it is happening right now rather than next year.

  • On AI, the money is moving before the policy is: There is no mandate and the guidance says so in writing. But the state AI task force wants an AI curriculum framework by 2030 and RIDE landed more than $18 million on August 14, 2026 for AI coaching and tutoring in middle-grades literacy. Technology directors are being asked to approve AI tools with no statute, no required policy, and no procurement standard to point at. Helping a district build a defensible vetting process is genuinely useful and does not require selling anything.

  • Know which side of the funding formula line a district sits on before you walk in: The leading proposal is already costed by district name. Providence gains about $186 million and Pawtucket $63 million; Newport, Chariho, Westerly, and Middletown lose. The commission reports in draft March 2, 2027 and finally September 1, 2027. A superintendent in a losing district is planning defensively and a superintendent in a gaining district is planning for a windfall that may not arrive. Those are two completely different conversations.

  • Budget empathy here means knowing the town's charter, not just the formula: School committees raise no money. In most communities a council decides, but in a financial town meeting or referendum town, voters can remove a line item in May after the school committee approved it. And when the town underfunds, the committee can and does go to court, as Providence did successfully. Ask which mechanism a community uses and when it votes before assuming anything about timing.

  • Providence is a special case and should be treated as one: It returned to local control on July 1 with ten conditions constraining its board's agenda, a $16.5 million deficit, 109 positions at risk, a superintendent whose contract expires June 30, 2027 with renewal talks deferred to this fall, and a roughly $1 billion facilities plan the state monitors but will not pay for. It is a fifth of the state's students and the least stable buying environment in it. Approach with patience and without assuming decisions can be made quickly.

The vendor was not named in the announcement and nobody has publicly said what happens if the federal money stops.
