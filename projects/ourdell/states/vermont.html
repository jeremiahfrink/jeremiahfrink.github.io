1. Snapshot

Last verified: August 17, 2026

Leadership & Governance
Governor Phil Scott (R), in office since 2017. Vermont governors serve two-year terms, so he is on the ballot again on November 3, 2026, seeking a sixth term. Raters had the race Solid or Likely Republican
Secretary of Education Zoie Saunders. The Senate rejected her in April 2024, she served on an acting basis, and the Senate confirmed her on March 13, 2025. Several Agency pages still call her interim; she is not. Do not use that word
State chief selection The Governor appoints and the Senate confirms. Vermont is not a state where the board picks the chief. The Secretary sits on the State Board without a vote
State Board of Education 11 members, 9 voting plus 2 students, all appointed by the Governor with Senate consent. Confirm the current chair by name before a meeting; the roster was not reachable in August 2026 (see Section 5)
Local control Two layers. A local school board votes a budget and takes it to voters. A supervisory union sits above it and runs most operations, including in many cases technology (see Sections 2 and 5)
Scale
Districts About 119 school districts inside 52 supervisory unions and supervisory districts. As of 2026, also 7 new regional service agencies called CESAs, and 20 merger study groupings
Students / schools Roughly 83,000 students and about 305 schools on the most recent federal figures, which date to 2022 and are almost certainly high now given closures. The Agency's live enrollment dashboard is the place to get a current count
Regional layer Unlike any other state in this batch. Every district belongs to a supervisory union by law, and the union employs the superintendent, runs the business office, and usually runs technology. New in 2026: seven CESAs on top (see Sections 2 and 7)
Largest districts Burlington, Essex Westford, South Burlington, Champlain Valley, Rutland City. By national standards every Vermont district is small
Money & Calendar
Funding formula Today, none in the usual sense. Each district votes its own budget and the state converts that into a tax rate. A per-student foundation formula of $15,033 was written into law but does not start until July 2029 at the earliest, and needs another act of the legislature first (see Section 3)
Budget cycle Annual state budget, two-year legislative term. State fiscal year July 1 to June 30. The 2026 session ran January to late May
Current state budget A $9.4 billion state budget with a record $105 million for property tax relief. The FY2027 yield bill used about $105 million of one-time money to hold the average homestead tax rate change to roughly a 3.5% decrease, though total homestead taxes still rose because property values did
Legislative session Convenes in early January. The next two-year term begins January 2027 with an entirely newly elected legislature
Local approval mechanism Voters, on Town Meeting Day, the first Tuesday of March. Some districts vote from the floor at a meeting where the budget can be amended; others use a secret paper ballot with no amendment. If a budget fails the board revises and puts it back to voters, as often as needed (see Section 6)
District budget rhythm Budgets built October to January, warned about a month ahead, voted the first Tuesday of March, re-voted in spring if they fail. Tax rates are not final until the legislature passes the yield bill in May or June, so people vote before they know their rate
Quick Yes/No
State AI guidance for schools? YES, and advisory only. The Agency of Education released AI Guidance for Education on January 27, 2026. No district policy is required, no deadline, no reporting, and no state money or pilots attached (see Section 4)
Cell phone restriction? YES, in law. Act 72 of 2025 at 16 V.S.A. 583 bans personal devices from arrival to dismissal, all grades. District policies must be in place for this school year, 2026-27 (see Section 4)
Screen time action? YES, on two tracks. An Age-Appropriate Design Code takes effect January 1, 2027 and binds platforms, not schools. Separately, Vermont was reportedly the first state to bar schools from using social media to communicate directly with students, and that one does create district work (see Section 4)
Student data privacy law? YES, and it points at vendors rather than districts. 9 V.S.A. 2443 and following regulates operators of school services. The 45-day breach notice law at 9 V.S.A. 2435 does bind districts (see Section 4)
SDPC / standard NDPA state? YES. The Vermont Student Privacy Alliance runs the Student Data Privacy Consortium model with a Vermont standard agreement. The Agency of Education is the contact point rather than a regional body
1:1 / state device program? No statewide device program, no state student information system, no state network, and no state learning platform. E-rate is the only real subsidy. Everything runs supervisory union by supervisory union
ESA / voucher program? No voucher in the usual sense, but something older and more contested. Towns without their own schools pay tuition to schools families choose, including independent schools. Act 73 narrowed which schools qualify and that narrowing is in litigation
Collective bargaining Yes, and with a Vermont twist: health benefits are bargained once, statewide, under a 2018 law. Districts pay the bill and have no say in the design

2. Where the Focus Is

Last verified: August 17, 2026

Vermont is in the middle of rebuilding how it runs and pays for schools, and the single most useful thing to understand is what has actually become law and what has not. Act 170 of 2026, signed June 24, 2026, is the operative law. It requires every district to take part in a merger study. It does not merge anyone. Voters decide that in March 2028. Meanwhile the thing that will actually squeeze budgets in the next two years is not the new formula, which is still years away and not yet certain, but a spending penalty that tightens starting in 2028. The two lists below are ranked by attention consumed.

What superintendents are focused on

  • A spending penalty that gets tighter every year, measured in teaching positions: Vermont taxes districts twice on every dollar they spend above a threshold. That threshold is 118% of the statewide average per weighted student today, and the 2026 laws tighten it toward 12.5% above average by 2032, starting in 2028. One supervisory union modeled a $2.5 million annual hit, which it described as 12 to 15 teaching positions. This is the near-term pain, and it arrives before any of the structural reform does. See Section 3.

  • Health costs they pay for and cannot control: School employee health benefits in Vermont are bargained once for the whole state under a 2018 law, by a commission of five employee and five employer representatives, with the current agreement running through the end of 2027. Costs rose roughly 35% over three years. Districts absorb that with no ability to change plan design, and the 2025 reform law pointedly did not touch it. See Section 6.

  • Special education costs rising faster than enrollment is falling: Special education spending reached $473 million in FY2024, up $76 million since 2018, which works out to more than $5,000 per student statewide counting every student. Reimbursement for extraordinary costs rose 60% over the same period. Vermont sends students to out-of-district placements at more than twice the national rate even though it includes students in general classrooms at above average rates. See Section 10.

  • Merger committee work on top of the day job, and school closure politics underneath it: Every district must appoint a representative by September 15, 2026, committees first meet by October 15, 2026, reports are due September 1, 2027, and voters decide March 7, 2028. At the same time enrollment is falling and Act 73 put minimum class sizes into law with an enforcement path that ends in a closure recommendation after three years of non-compliance. Superintendents will own those conversations in public. See Section 3.

  • Town Meeting Day, which got harder again: On March 3, 2026 voters approved 85 school budgets and rejected 19, with 12 more pushed to later votes. That is better than 2024, when roughly a third failed and triggered the whole reform, but worse than 2025, when 9 failed. The re-vote treadmill is back. See Section 6.

What technology leaders are focused on

  • A new regional agency that is now required to offer IT services, and nobody is sure whether they have to buy: Act 170 created seven CESAs, regional service agencies whose boards were being seated in July and August 2026. From launch they must offer special education, business, information technology, and administrative services, with professional development, curriculum, transportation, and facilities planning added July 1, 2027. Membership is mandatory. Whether a district must actually purchase those services is described differently by the Agency of Education and by at least one supervisory union. For a technology director this reads as either relief or the consolidation of their own job. It is the open question in Vermont technology right now. See Sections 3 and 7.

  • Real security scars and no requirement to point a budget request at: The PowerSchool breach in December 2024 and January 2025 reached 39 Vermont districts. Vermont imposes no cybersecurity plan requirement, no incident reporting clock for schools, and no certification. The only binding duty is the general 45-day breach notice that applies to anyone holding personal data. The Agency offers planning rubrics rather than rules or money. The state ed-tech association's fall 2026 calendar is stacked with cybersecurity sessions, which tells you where the demand is. See Section 8.

  • Phone compliance is due now, unfunded, and comes with an unusual second job: District policies under the 2025 phone law must be adopted and enforced for this school year. No state money for pouches or storage has been identified, so districts are self-funding. The same act also bars schools from using social media to communicate directly with students unless an administrator has approved the platform, which means every team, club, and classroom account needs an approval process someone has to run. School-issued devices are outside the restriction, which protects 1:1 programs. See Section 4.

  • AI guidance with nothing underneath it: The Agency published AI guidance on January 27, 2026. It is advisory, sets no deadline, requires no policy, and came with no money, no pilots, and no state-funded training. Every district writes its own policy from scratch. The guidance leans toward caution, arguing students need core knowledge before they can judge what AI produces, which will not always match vendor enthusiasm. See Section 4.

  • No state rails, and a merger that will eventually force a systems reconciliation: There is no statewide device program, no state student information system, no state network, and no state learning platform. PowerSchool is the de facto common system and is usually hosted at the supervisory union. If mergers pass in March 2028 and new districts start July 2029, someone has to reconcile student information systems, identity and rostering, email domains, and device fleets, and that cost is on nobody's line. See Section 8.

What the regional service agency layer is focused on

Vermont's regional layer needs its own explanation because it is genuinely unlike the others in this series. In New York, Ohio, Pennsylvania, or Massachusetts, the regional agency is an optional vendor that districts join. In Vermont the supervisory union is the layer above the districts and every district belongs to one by law. The union employs the superintendent and the central office, runs the business office and payroll, administers special education, usually runs transportation and technology, and typically hosts the student information system. It is often the contracting party. In a single district supervisory union the distinction with the district disappears entirely. So it is not a channel you can go around. For operational purposes it is closer to being the district.

On top of that, as of July 2026, sit seven brand new CESAs with mandatory supervisory union membership and a statutory duty to provide special education, business, information technology, and administrative services. They have the ambition of a New York BOCES and none of the financial machinery: nothing in the law pays a district back for buying through a CESA, so volume has to be earned rather than subsidized. The only money incentive in Act 170 rewards merging, not purchasing. One published analysis also notes that CESA boundaries and merger study groupings do not line up, so a district can be in one CESA while studying merger with districts in another. These organizations were holding their first board meetings weeks ago. Sections 5 and 7 carry the consequences.

3. What the State Is Driving

Last verified: August 17, 2026

Vermont is the state in this batch where the difference between enacted, scheduled, and merely proposed matters most, because a great deal has been announced and rather less is actually binding. Each box below says plainly which it is.

SCHOOL DISTRICT CONSOLIDATION | Mandatory process, voluntary outcome. The study is required, the merger is not, and voters decide on March 7, 2028. No map has ever been enacted.

  • What Act 170 actually did: Act 170 of 2026, passed 125 to 10 in the House and 27 to 2 in the Senate and signed June 24, 2026, sorts all 119 districts into 20 merger study groupings. Every district must appoint a board member and take part in good faith. Forced mergers came off the table in the end-of-session compromise, so voters, not the state, decide. The target is roughly 2,000 students per new district, with special handling for districts under 750.

  • What did not happen, and gets misreported: Act 73 of 2025 directed a task force to draw a consolidation map. The task force declined, recommending voluntary mergers and regional service agencies instead, and a competing map drawn by a senator was rejected. The Secretary of Education said publicly that the recommendation did not match the law's intent. No Vermont district map has been enacted. Any briefing showing the new Vermont map is wrong.

  • The dates districts are working to: Facilitators hired by September 1, 2026. Districts appoint representatives by September 15, 2026. Committees first meet by October 15, 2026. Final reports due September 1, 2027. Voters decide on Town Meeting Day, March 7, 2028. New districts operate from July 1, 2029. A moratorium on districts withdrawing from unions runs to 2035.

  • The carrot: School construction aid was reestablished and merged or aligned districts can qualify for 30% to 75% of an entire project cost. That is the financial argument for merging. Note the timing problem: the aid is not flowing yet, because three conditions must be met first, and the rulemaking deadline is March 1, 2028, weeks before the merger vote.

Source: Act 170 of 2026, as enacted | VTDigger on the final reform bill | VTDigger on the redistricting task force | Agency of Education, education transformation | Agency of Education, school construction aid

THE SPENDING PENALTY THAT TIGHTENS EVERY YEAR | Enacted, and the thing that will actually change district behavior before any of the structural reform arrives.

  • How it works, in plain terms: Vermont sets a ceiling on district spending per weighted student, expressed as a percentage above the statewide average. Spend a dollar above that ceiling and the district is taxed on it twice, with the proceeds effectively subsidizing districts below the line. The current ceiling is 118% of the statewide average.

  • What changes and when: The 2026 laws phase the ceiling down from 18 points above average to 12.5 points above by FY2032, restarting the ratchet in FY2028. The Senate had pushed a tighter version at 112%, which analysts said would have taken the number of districts over the line from about 10 to roughly 32 and penalized about $21 million of spending.

  • What it looks like inside a district: Mountain Views Supervisory Union in Woodstock modeled a $2.5 million annual hit, which it translated to 12 to 15 teaching positions. That is the arithmetic every Vermont business manager is now running, and it is why the near-term conversation is about cost reduction rather than investment.

  • Why it matters more than the formula: The foundation formula is contingent and years out. This is already law, already scheduled, and hits budgets districts are building this winter. If you only understand one number in Vermont school finance, make it this one.

Source: Agency of Education, excess spending threshold | Vermont Public on the property tax debate | Act 170 of 2026, as enacted | Public Assets Institute on education funding reform

THE FOUNDATION FORMULA THAT IS NOT YET SETTLED | Written into law, delayed to July 2029, and still requiring a future legislature to pass more legislation before it can start.

  • What it would do: Replace the current system, in which each district votes its own budget and the state converts that into a tax rate, with a per-student amount set by the state. The base as written is $15,033 per student, adjusted by weights for disadvantage and English learners. It would be the largest change to Vermont school governance in generations.

  • Where the date actually sits: Act 73 set July 1, 2028. Act 170 moved it to July 1, 2029, the 2029-30 school year. Both framings appear in press coverage and both are technically true: relative to Act 73 it is a one-year delay, and relative to the House version of the 2026 bill it is a one-year acceleration, which is what the Governor got in the compromise.

  • The condition that matters most: It is not self-executing. Implementation depends on receiving specified reports and on the legislature enacting further legislation covering career and technical education, special education weighting, sparsity, secondary school weighting, geographic cost differences, and pre-kindergarten funding. A future legislature must act again. Do not describe this as settled.

  • Why November matters here: That future legislature is being elected on November 3, 2026. Every one of Vermont's 150 House and 30 Senate seats is up, as they are every two years. A single election can reverse direction here, as it did between the Senate rejecting the Secretary of Education in 2024 and confirming her in 2025. The largest risk to the whole transformation is the election, not the policy.

Source: Act 73 of 2025, as enacted | Act 170 of 2026, as enacted | VTDigger on the Senate compromise | Public Assets Institute on education funding reform | 2026 gubernatorial election

CESAs, THE NEW REGIONAL LAYER | Fully enacted and already standing up. Seven agencies, mandatory membership, boards seated weeks ago, and no purchasing subsidy behind them.

  • What they are: Seven regional agencies created by Act 170 to provide shared services. The law replaces the older BOCES terminology throughout Vermont's education title. Every supervisory union is assigned to one by name in the statute, and each appoints a director annually who must be a board member or the superintendent. Boards meet at least four times a year and must have an executive director.

  • What they must provide, and when: From launch: special education including low-incidence and high-cost services, plus business, information technology, and administrative services, plus help creating union districts. From July 1, 2027: professional development, curriculum coordination, transportation, and facilities master planning.

  • The ambiguity worth resolving: The Agency of Education describes participation as mandatory with required services. At least one supervisory union has told its community that shared services remain optional collaborations. The reconciliation is probably that membership is mandatory and the CESA must offer the services, while whether a district must buy them is the contested part. That is precisely the question a technology director cares about, and it is not yet publicly settled.

  • The design gap already being noticed: Nothing in the law pays a district back for buying through a CESA, unlike New York's BOCES aid. The financial incentive in Act 170 rewards merging, not purchasing. Published analysis has also flagged that CESA boundaries and merger study groupings do not overlap, creating conflicting obligations, while estimating meaningful savings are available if the shared services actually get used.

Source: Act 170 of 2026, as enacted | Agency of Education, education transformation | A supervisory union explainer on Act 170 | VTDigger opinion on merger incentives | VSBA education transformation resources

STANDARDIZING WHAT SCHOOL LOOKS LIKE INSIDE | A cluster of state-set minimums replacing local variation. Class sizes are enacted, graduation requirements and a statewide calendar are scheduled, and literacy is required but not funded.

  • Minimum class sizes, enacted: Act 73 set minimums in statute: 10 in grade 1, 12 in grades 2 to 5, 15 in grades 6 to 8, and 18 in grades 9 to 12, applying to public and approved independent schools. Kindergarten, career and technical education, Advanced Placement, driver education, and specialized equipment courses are exempt, and geographically isolated schools can seek waivers. Three consecutive years out of compliance leads to technical assistance, state administrative control, or a closure recommendation. Two Agency pages disagree on the compliance start date, so confirm it before quoting one.

  • One statewide graduation standard replacing 119: Vermont is moving from district-set proficiency-based graduation requirements to a single statewide requirement. Agency recommendations went to the legislature in December 2025, floating a 22.5-credit minimum. The State Board must adopt by July 1, 2027, with implementation in 2027-28. The first affected cohort is this year's eighth graders, the class of 2031. More than 1,500 students were consulted between February and July 2026.

  • A statewide school calendar: The Secretary must publish one by January 15, 2027, effective in 2028-29. For anyone planning professional learning, assessment windows, or deployment schedules across districts, this eventually removes a real source of variation.

  • Literacy, required and underfunded, and the Agency says so: Act 139 of 2024 requires evidence-based explicit K-3 foundational skills instruction, universal reading screening, supplemental instruction for students below benchmark, parent notification, and educator training, and requires all K-3 educators to hold certification in evidence-based foundational literacy by 2030. The Agency's own May 2026 report says the act set ambitious expectations without dedicated state funding, and asked for $700,000, against $15 million a year in Mississippi and $55 million in Maryland. 42% of K-3 students screened below proficiency, and 41% of principals report trouble hiring teachers prepared in reading science.

4. The Four National Questions

Last verified: August 17, 2026

These are the four questions every state is answering, and the point of this section is comparability. Vermont's pattern is easy to summarize: it moved early and hard on phones, it regulates ed-tech vendors rather than districts on privacy, its social media law binds platforms rather than schools, and it has done the least of any state in this batch on AI. Where a topic is also a state initiative, Section 3 explains why the state pushed it and this section carries the rules and dates.

ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE | Guidance only, published January 27, 2026. No mandate, no deadline, no reporting, and no money.

  • What exists: The Agency of Education published AI Guidance for Education on January 27, 2026. It is advisory. It is not a rule, it does not require a district to adopt a policy, it sets no date, and it imposes no reporting. No state funding, pilot program, or state-run training was announced with it.

  • What it actually argues: The guidance leans cautious. Its themes are human judgment and educator agency, using AI in service of Vermont's proficiency-based and project-based approach, accessibility and reducing administrative load, and, notably, building core content knowledge first so students can evaluate what AI produces. The Secretary framed it as helping educators use tools already present in classrooms with intention and confidence.

  • What is not there: No Vermont legislation requiring district AI policies was found. There is no state review process for AI tools, no procurement rubric of the kind Maryland uses, and no adoption deadline. Every district is writing its own policy from a blank page.

  • Where the help is coming from: The Agency has run follow-up workshops on moving from guidance to practice. The state ed-tech association is the other venue. In practice, capacity building here is thinner than in any other state in this batch, which is worth naming honestly rather than treating as a gap in the profile.

Source: Agency of Education AI guidance release | AI Guidance for Education | Agency of Education, education technology | Vita-Learn

SCREEN TIME | Two separate things. A design code that binds platforms from January 1, 2027, and a first-in-nation restriction on schools using social media that binds districts now.

  • The platform-side law: Vermont's Age-Appropriate Design Code, enacted in 2025 and codified at 9 V.S.A. 2449a and following, takes effect January 1, 2027. It reaches businesses and online services reasonably likely to be accessed by minors, a deliberately low threshold with no minimum business size, and requires high-privacy defaults, a duty of care against specified harms, limits on profiling, algorithmic transparency, and restrictions on manipulative design. Obligations fall on platforms, not on schools. The Attorney General has rulemaking under way.

  • Litigation status: No Vermont-specific challenge had been filed as of mid-2026, though the trade group that has successfully enjoined similar laws elsewhere testified against the bill. The long runway to January 2027 leaves room for a pre-enforcement challenge, so check before treating it as certain.

  • The part that is district work, and it is unusual: Act 72 of 2025 reportedly made Vermont the first state to bar public schools and approved independent schools from using social media to communicate directly with students, or from requiring a social media account for academic or extracurricular participation, except on platforms an administrator has approved. This took effect on signing, June 27, 2025. In practice it means every coach's team account, classroom account, and club account needs an approval workflow and someone to run it.

  • Where screen time meets school devices: Vermont has not set numeric screen time limits for school-provided devices, unlike the direction Connecticut took in August 2026. The state's attention here is on personal devices and on platforms, not on auditing the district's own software.

Source: Analysis of the Vermont design code | Attorney General design code rulemaking | Coverage of the school social media provision | Act 72 of 2025 summary | VTDigger on the phone law

CELL PHONES / CONNECTED DEVICES | In law since 2025, arrival to dismissal, all grades. School-issued devices are expressly outside it, and district policies are due this school year.

  • The law: Act 72 of 2025, from H.480, signed June 27, 2025, codified at 16 V.S.A. 583. It applies to all public schools and approved independent schools and all grades, and it covers the whole school day, from arrival to dismissal, not merely instructional time.

  • What is covered, and the carve-out that matters: The statute reaches cell phones and other non-school-issued personal electronic devices with cellular, internet, or wireless capability, explicitly including smartwatches. Because it reaches only non-school-issued devices, district-issued laptops, Chromebooks, and tablets sit outside the restriction. That protects 1:1 programs and, as in most states with these laws, makes the district-managed device the sanctioned screen in the building.

  • Exemptions and deadlines: Three statutory exemptions: devices required by an individualized health care plan, an individualized education program, or a Section 504 plan, including disability accommodations; use approved by an administrator for an academic, school-sponsored athletic, or cocurricular purpose; and use required under the federal McKinney-Vento Act for students experiencing homelessness. District policies must be adopted and enforced by the 2026-27 school year, meaning now. The Secretary must publish and annually review a model policy, which is a floor districts may exceed. No reporting duty appears in the statute.

  • The unfunded part: No state appropriation for pouches, lockers, or storage was identified. Districts are self-funding and solving it differently, from purchased lockable pouches at Harwood Union to a zero-cost off-and-out-of-sight rule at Maple Run. That is a real cost landing on districts this fall with no state help behind it.

Source: 16 V.S.A. 583, personal electronic devices | Act 72 of 2025 summary | VTDigger on the phone law | Agency of Education, education policy changes

STUDENT DATA PRIVACY | The duties sit on the vendor, not the district, which is the reverse of Connecticut. Vermont does run a standard agreement, and a separate breach law does bind districts.

  • The statute: 9 V.S.A. 2443 and following, effective July 1, 2020, regulates operators of websites, online services, and applications used primarily for preschool through grade 12 school purposes. Operators may not use school-collected data for targeted advertising, may not build student profiles for non-educational purposes, and may not sell, barter, or rent student information. They must maintain reasonable security, delete student data on request, and disclose their data practices publicly. The Attorney General enforces. Some sources cite an act number rather than the statute; cite the statute.

  • Districts are not the regulated party here: The obligations attach to the vendor rather than to the school board, and the subchapter does not displace the federal student records law. That is a meaningful difference from Connecticut, where the board is the statutory duty holder and a non-conforming contract is void. In Vermont the district's leverage is contractual rather than statutory.

  • The instrument districts actually use: Vermont is a Student Data Privacy Consortium alliance state. The Vermont Student Privacy Alliance runs a Vermont standard K-12 student data privacy agreement, with the Agency of Education as the contact point rather than a regional service agency. Signing that agreement is the practical entry requirement.

  • The law that does bind districts, plus the recent scar: Vermont's security breach notice law at 9 V.S.A. 2435 requires notice within 45 days of discovery and reaches anyone holding personal data, districts included; 2020 amendments extended covered data to biometric, genetic, and health information and to login credentials. The Attorney General posts breach notices publicly, which is a useful way to check a district's history before a meeting. The reference incident is PowerSchool in December 2024 and January 2025, which reached 39 Vermont districts, with Champlain Valley confirming it was affected. No statewide system change followed.

Source: 9 V.S.A. chapter 62, personal information | Vermont Student Privacy Alliance | Vermont K-12 student data privacy agreement | Attorney General security breach notices | VTDigger on the PowerSchool breach

Sources: 9 V.S.A. chapter 62, personal information | Agency of Education, education technology | Analysis of the Vermont design code | Act 72 of 2025 summary | Attorney General security breach notices

5. Governance & Decision-Making

Last verified: August 17, 2026

Authority runs Governor to Secretary of Education and the Agency of Education, with a State Board that sets rules, down to 52 supervisory unions and about 119 school districts. The Governor appoints the Secretary and the Senate confirms, and the Governor appoints every State Board member with Senate consent. The Secretary sits on the State Board but does not vote. The practical thing to understand is that the district is often not the operating unit. The supervisory union above it usually is.

  • Leadership: Secretary Zoie Saunders was appointed in 2024, rejected by the Senate that April, served on an acting basis, and was confirmed on March 13, 2025. Several Agency pages still describe her as interim and are simply out of date; do not repeat that in a meeting. The State Board chair is not named on any current source as of August 2026: the Agency roster was unreachable and the best available listing carries its own out-of-date warning. Get the name from a recent board agenda before using it.

  • Election-year context, provisional note: Vermont elects a governor every two years and all 180 legislative seats every two years, with no staggering, so the entire legislature is on the ballot November 3, 2026. Governor Scott announced on May 28, 2026 that he is seeking a sixth term against Esther Charlestin, and raters had the race Solid or Likely Republican. The reason this matters more here than anywhere else in the batch: the foundation formula cannot start until a future legislature passes further legislation, and that legislature is the one being elected in November. Vermont has already shown a single cycle can reverse a position, given the same Senate that rejected the Secretary in 2024 confirmed her in 2025.

  • The supervisory union, and why it is the answer to most questions: Every district belongs to a supervisory union by law. The union employs the superintendent and the central office, runs the business office and payroll, administers special education, and typically runs transportation and technology and hosts the student information system for its member districts. It is frequently the contracting party and the employer of record. In a supervisory district, which is a single-district union created under the 2015 consolidation law, the union and the district are the same thing. This is not an optional service organization. It is the operating layer.

  • The CESAs on top of it, brand new: Seven regional agencies created by Act 170, with mandatory supervisory union membership, boards seated in July and August 2026, and a statutory duty from launch to provide special education, business, information technology, and administrative services. Each member union appoints one director annually, who must be a board member or the superintendent. They are the newest institutions in New England education and almost nobody outside Vermont knows they exist. See Sections 3 and 7.

6. Funding & Budget Rhythm

Last verified: August 17, 2026. FY2027 yield bill in effect since July 1, 2026

  • There is no funding formula yet, and that is the point: Unlike every other state in this series, Vermont does not currently distribute a per-student grant. Each district votes its own budget and the state converts that spending into a property tax rate. A foundation formula at $15,033 per student is written into law but does not begin before July 2029 and requires further legislation first. See Section 3.

  • The local approval mechanism, stated explicitly: Voters decide, on Town Meeting Day, the first Tuesday of March. Some districts vote from the floor at a meeting, where the budget can be amended in the room. Others use a secret paper ballot, where voters answer yes or no to the number as printed and cannot change it. Which method a district uses is a local choice, and it largely determines how stressful February is for a superintendent. If a budget fails, the board revises it and warns it again for a later vote, as many times as needed. There is no state takeover and no shutdown. In 2026, 85 budgets passed, 19 failed, and 12 were pushed to later votes.

  • How a district budget becomes a homeowner's tax bill, in plain steps: First, divide the district's budget by its weighted student count, where students are weighted for poverty, English learner status, and small or rural schools. Second, divide that by the statewide property yield, which the legislature sets each year and which is the amount of per-student spending a $1.00 tax rate is designed to buy. The FY2027 yield is $9,401, so a district spending $11,281 per weighted student gets a rate of $1.20. Spend more per student and your rate rises proportionally and automatically. There is no local rate-setting discretion; the yield is the state's throttle. Third, adjust for the town, because towns assess property differently. That adjustment used to be the Common Level of Appraisal alone and since July 2025 is the Common Level of Appraisal divided by a statewide adjustment figure. The state describes this as a restatement rather than a change in what anyone pays, but it is why homeowners sometimes blame the school board for what is really an outdated town assessment.

  • Most homeowners do not actually pay the property rate: Vermont adjusts most homestead bills against household income rather than property value, using an income yield set alongside the property yield; the FY2027 income yield is $12,960. Act 73 would replace that credit with a capped homestead exemption, but that change is contingent and not in effect. Businesses, second homes, and rentals pay a flat statewide rate instead, $1.643 per $100 of equalized value for FY2027, with no yield mechanic at all.

  • The gap that produces Town Meeting Day anger: Districts vote budgets in early March. The legislature does not pass the yield bill that sets the rate until May or June. So Vermonters vote on a school budget without knowing what it will do to their tax bill. Every February and March conversation in this state happens inside that uncertainty.

  • What the state did for FY2027, and what it did not: The 2026 yield bill used roughly $105 million in one-time general fund money and prior-year surplus to buy the increase down, bringing the average homestead rate down about 3.5%. Total homestead taxes still rose about 4.6%, because property values rose. Analysts have been blunt that this is a one-time buy-down that does not change the trajectory. Education property taxes have risen roughly 40% over five years and health costs about 35% over three.

  • Special education and the cost curve: Special education spending reached $473 million in FY2024, up $76 million since 2018. Reimbursement for extraordinary costs rose from $53 million in 2018 to $86 million in 2024. Act 73 mandated a three-year special education strategic plan with a $2.865 million appropriation, and Act 170 handed low-incidence and high-cost services to the new CESAs, which is an untested transfer of responsibility.

  • School construction, reestablished but not yet paying: A construction aid program was created July 1, 2025 as a shell, and FY2027 funded four staff positions and roughly $800,000 to $900,000 for master planning grants, with authority for up to $50 million a year in additional state bonding. Three conditions must be met before aid actually flows: bonding recommendations, an operational construction division with completed rules, and a committed funding source. Rules are not due until March 1, 2028. Merged or aligned districts can qualify for 30% to 75% of project cost, which is the financial argument behind voluntary merger.

  • Where technology money lives: In the supervisory union's operating budget, inside the district budget that goes to voters in March, and nowhere else. There is no state technology fund, no device program, and no dedicated line. E-rate is the only meaningful subsidy. Every technology purchase therefore competes directly against staff inside a budget that a spending penalty is squeezing from above.

7. Procurement & Vendor Path

Last verified: August 17, 2026

  • The threshold, which unlike most states is actually in statute: 16 V.S.A. 559 requires a school board or supervisory union board to publicly advertise or invite three or more bids when the cost exceeds $40,000. It covers construction, purchase or lease of supplies and equipment, maintenance and repair, transportation items, and service contracts for transportation, maintenance, and repair. Below $40,000 it is local policy, and expect wide variation. Textbooks and instructional materials, emergency repairs, school nutrition contracts, and qualifying renewals are exempt.

  • The cleanest path in the batch: The same statute says a board need not bid at all if it buys through the Commissioner of Buildings and General Services under the state purchasing law. Buying off a state contract satisfies the bidding requirement outright. That is unusually clean, and it is statutory rather than a matter of reading each contract's fine print as it is in Connecticut. Joint bidding among districts and supervisory unions is also expressly permitted.

  • What carries the purchasing load, and how that follows from Section 2: The supervisory union, in most cases. Because the union employs the central office, runs the business office, and usually runs technology and hosts the student information system, it is frequently the buyer and the signer. Selling around it to a member district is not a strategy in Vermont, it is a misunderstanding of the structure. The new CESAs are the layer to watch, not yet the layer to sell through, because they were seated in July and August 2026 and have no purchasing subsidy behind them to pull volume in.

  • The CESA question a buyer will ask you: Because Act 170 requires CESAs to offer information technology services from launch, a district may reasonably ask whether it will still be buying its own technology in two years. The honest answer today is that nobody knows: the Agency describes participation as mandatory with required services, and at least one supervisory union has told its community that shared services remain optional. Treat that ambiguity as a live question rather than resolving it for a district.

  • Cooperative purchasing: Vermont has no strong statewide K-12 purchasing consortium of the kind Pennsylvania or Maryland have. State contracts through Buildings and General Services are the statutorily blessed route. National cooperatives are not prohibited, but how much Vermont districts actually use them is not documented publicly, so ask rather than assume.

  • The privacy layer: Vermont is a Student Data Privacy Consortium alliance state with a Vermont standard student data privacy agreement, coordinated through the Agency of Education. Signing it is the practical entry requirement. Note the difference from Connecticut: in Vermont the statutory duties sit on the vendor rather than making a non-conforming contract void, so the agreement is the district's leverage rather than a statutory gate. See Section 4.

  • Two other statewide rails, neither of them technology: The Vermont School Boards Insurance Trust handles insurance and risk pooling and hosts the business officials association's monthly meetings. The Vermont Education Health Initiative administers the statewide school employee health plan. Both are genuine statewide service organizations, and neither buys technology, but the insurance trust's building in Berlin is where the state's business managers physically gather every month.

8. Technology & Digital Learning Posture

Last verified: August 17, 2026

  • Devices: No statewide 1:1 program, no device standard, no state refresh fund, and no state purchasing aggregation of the kind Connecticut is attempting. Districts and supervisory unions buy independently. Because refresh sits inside a budget that goes to voters in March and is squeezed by a spending penalty from above, device replacement is a genuinely contested line rather than a routine one.

  • Assessment: Cognia is the statewide assessment vendor, delivering the Vermont Comprehensive Assessment Program in English language arts, mathematics, and science online. The vendor was selected in 2022. No 2026 change of vendor or platform surfaced, and the contract renewal date was not established from public sources. Vermont's assessment stack is not the thing in motion here, unlike Virginia's.

  • Statewide platforms, and their absence: There is no state student information system, no state learning platform, and no state K-12 network of the kind New York, Ohio, or Pennsylvania run. PowerSchool is the de facto common student information system, used by at least 39 districts and typically hosted at the supervisory union. Broadband build-out in Vermont runs through municipal Communications Union Districts rather than through a school network, so E-rate is the mechanism that actually matters for schools. The Agency publishes a digital learning plan development guide, which is recommended rather than required.

  • Cybersecurity: no mandate, real exposure: Vermont imposes no cybersecurity plan requirement on districts, no school-specific incident reporting clock, no certification, and no insurance requirement. The only binding duty is the general 45-day breach notice at 9 V.S.A. 2435. The Agency offers breach planning rubrics, data security guidance, and pointers to federal resources, all recommended. The reference incident is PowerSchool in December 2024 and January 2025, which reached 39 Vermont districts, with Champlain Valley confirming exposure of student names, addresses, emails, and birthdates. Notably, no statewide system change followed. The Attorney General publishes breach notices, which is the fastest way to check a district's history.

  • AI capacity building: Thin, and honestly so. Advisory guidance since January 2026, follow-up workshops from the Agency, and the state ed-tech association's programming. No state funding, no pilot program, no state-run training, and no micro-credential of the kind Connecticut's regional agencies run. This is the least developed AI support picture in the batch.

  • Framing: Vermont frames technology through a cost and governance lens rather than an instructional one. The state's energy is going into who runs schools and what they cost, not into what happens on a screen. Its AI guidance is cautious about substituting tools for knowledge, its phone law is among the strictest in the region, and it has built no state technology infrastructure to lean on. For a technology leader that means very little arrives from the state in either direction: no mandates to comply with and no rails to ride.

9. Instructional Priorities & Mandates

Last verified: August 17, 2026

  • Literacy is the dominant instructional mandate, and the Agency says it is underfunded: Act 139 of 2024 requires evidence-based explicit and systematic foundational skills instruction in kindergarten through grade 3, universal reading screening, tier one plus supplemental reading instruction for students below benchmark across all grades, parent notification in K-3, and educator professional learning. By 2030 every K-3 educator must hold certification in evidence-based foundational literacy. The statute requires only one screening a year; the Agency strongly recommends three, and 92% of responding schools screen. The results explain the urgency: 42% of K-3 students screened below proficiency and third grade reading proficiency sits at 48%.

  • The funding complaint is the state's own: The Agency's May 2026 annual report states plainly that Act 139 set ambitious literacy expectations without dedicated state funding, and it requested $700,000 framed as a reappropriation. For scale, the same report compares that with $15 million a year in Mississippi, $35 million in Massachusetts, $55 million in Maryland, and $168 million in Ohio. Separately, 41% of principals report difficulty hiring teachers prepared in reading science, which is the practical constraint behind the 2030 certification deadline.

  • Graduation requirements are being standardized for the first time: Vermont has run on proficiency-based graduation requirements set district by district, which means 119 different systems. That is being replaced with a single statewide requirement. Agency recommendations went to the legislature in December 2025 and floated a 22.5-credit minimum, the State Board must adopt by July 1, 2027, implementation follows in 2027-28, and the first cohort affected is this year's eighth graders, the class of 2031. More than 1,500 students were consulted between February and July 2026.

  • Class size minimums are now law, with teeth: Minimums of 10 in grade 1, 12 in grades 2 to 5, 15 in grades 6 to 8, and 18 in grades 9 to 12, applying to public and approved independent schools, with exemptions for kindergarten, career and technical education, Advanced Placement, driver education, and specialized equipment courses, and waivers for geographically isolated schools. Three consecutive years of non-compliance leads to technical assistance, state administrative control, or a closure recommendation. Two Agency pages give different compliance start dates, so confirm before citing one. See Section 3.

  • Career and technical education, which the reform is trying to universalize: A 2026 act expanded career and technical education access and protects credits, proficiencies, and grades earned there. Merger study committees are directed to consider universal access to technical education as they design new districts, and technical centers appear as members of the new regional agencies. In a state rebuilding its district map, technical education is one of the few areas where the direction is expansion rather than contraction.

  • A statewide school calendar is coming: The Secretary must publish one by January 15, 2027, effective in the 2028-29 school year. It has had almost no coverage, but for anyone planning training, assessment windows, or deployments across districts it eventually removes a real source of variation.

10. Landscape & Pressures

Last verified: August 17, 2026

  • Enrollment is falling and special education need is rising, which is the squeeze: Enrollment is declining, while the share of students with individualized education programs has been rising faster than the national average since 2019-20, driven by autism and emotional disturbance classifications. Current enrollment and school counts are not available from public pages in August 2026 because the Agency's dashboard is interactive; the most recent federal figures, roughly 83,000 students and about 305 schools, date to 2022 and are almost certainly high now. Get the current number from the Agency dashboard before quoting one.

  • Teacher workforce, with an unusual constraint: Hiring difficulty is concentrated where the mandates are: 41% of principals report trouble hiring teachers prepared in reading science against a 2030 certification requirement. The more distinctive Vermont fact is on the compensation side. Health benefits are bargained once for the entire state by a ten-member commission under a 2018 law, with disputes going to arbitration and the current agreement extended through December 31, 2027. Districts have no design authority over their fastest-growing cost.

  • Facilities: Vermont went years without a state construction aid program and has just reestablished one that is not yet paying out. Legacy debt aid is capped around $46 million a year, new bonding authority runs to $50 million a year, and rules are not due until March 1, 2028. A separate 2026 act extended the deadline for PCB testing in school buildings to 2035 and created a fund for it, which is a real operational relief for facilities directors and got almost no coverage.

  • School choice, which in Vermont is older and more contested than a voucher debate: Towns without their own schools have paid tuition for students to attend schools their families choose, including independent schools, for roughly two centuries. Act 73 narrowed which independent schools qualify, using two tests: the school must be in a district or supervisory union without public schools for the relevant grades, and at least 25% of its 2023-24 enrollment must have been publicly funded. Reportedly only about 18 schools qualify. Students already enrolled are grandfathered through graduation. Act 170 added a bar on charging tuition above what the sending district pays. Both changes are in active litigation, including a state case filed March 3, 2026 and a separate federal suit. Handle this topic carefully in Vermont; it is genuinely raw.

  • The political weather: The governor has been explicit that the current system cannot continue, telling the legislature in January 2026 that education transformation is not optional and warning of another $200 million in spending need and double-digit property tax increases. Legislative leadership has been equally explicit that it wants long-term solutions rather than what the Speaker called band-aids. Both are on the ballot in November. Expect district leaders to be weary of the argument and focused on the spending penalty in front of them.

12. Key Notes for Engagement

Last verified: August 17, 2026. WORKING DRAFT for Jeremiah's edit. Research-generated engagement judgment, not settled advice

How to think about Vermont right now:

  • Know the difference between what passed and what was proposed, and you will be the most useful person in the room: A great deal has been announced in Vermont and much less is binding. Consolidation: the study is mandatory, the merger is not, and voters decide March 7, 2028. No district map was ever enacted, and the task force asked to draw one refused. The foundation formula is real but does not begin before July 2029 and needs another act of the legislature first. The class size minimums and the spending penalty, by contrast, are law now. Getting that distinction right is a credibility marker, because most outside briefings get it wrong.

  • Sell to the supervisory union, or you are not selling: This is the structural fact that most outsiders miss. The supervisory union employs the superintendent, runs the business office, administers special education, and usually runs technology and hosts the student information system. The district votes a budget; the union operates. Going around it to a member district is not a strategy, it is a misreading of how Vermont works.

  • The seven CESAs are the single most actionable thing in this profile: They were created in June 2026, their boards were seated in July and August, they are statutorily required to provide information technology services from launch, and they add professional development, curriculum, transportation, and facilities planning in July 2027. They have no purchasing subsidy behind them, so they have to earn volume. Anyone who gets in front of a CESA executive director this year is talking to an organization still deciding what it is. That window will not stay open.

  • Lead with the spending penalty, not with the formula: Vermont business managers are not planning around the 2029 formula. They are planning around a penalty that taxes every dollar above a ceiling twice, and a ceiling that starts tightening in 2028. One supervisory union put it at $2.5 million a year and 12 to 15 positions. Any conversation that does not acknowledge that arithmetic will sound like it came from outside the state.

  • Budget empathy in Vermont means understanding March: Voters decide school budgets on the first Tuesday of March, either from the floor of a meeting where the number can be amended or by secret ballot where it cannot. If it fails the board revises and comes back, sometimes more than once. In 2026, 19 budgets failed and 12 more were pushed to later votes. And they vote before the legislature sets the tax rate in May or June, so they are voting without knowing the consequence. That moves the whole influence calendar earlier than in any state profiled so far: by April the decisions are made.

  • On phones, the useful help is operational and unfunded: The law is settled and strict, arrival to dismissal, all grades, since 2025, with district policies due this school year. School-issued devices are outside it, which protects 1:1. What is not settled is how districts pay for storage, since no state money was appropriated, and how they run the approval process the same law requires for any school use of social media to reach students. Those are practical problems a district would welcome help thinking through.

  • On AI, Vermont has done the least in this batch, and that is the opening: Advisory guidance with no mandate, no deadline, no money, no pilots, and no state training. Every district writes its own policy from a blank page, and the state's own guidance argues for building knowledge before tools. Helping districts think that through is genuinely useful. Arriving with a product argument into a state whose guidance counsels restraint will not land.

  • Small state, real consequence, and one week in the fall: With about 119 districts, 52 supervisory unions, and seven CESAs, a small number of relationships reaches most of the state, which is the opposite of a long-tail market. The joint superintendents and school boards conference on October 29-30 in Burlington and Vermont Fest on November 4-6 at Killington are within a week of each other and together cover the board, superintendent, business, and technology audiences. If you attend one Vermont event, make it Vermont Fest; if you attend two, make it both.
