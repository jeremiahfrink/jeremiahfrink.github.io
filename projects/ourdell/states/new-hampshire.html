1. Snapshot

Last verified: August 17, 2026

Leadership & Governance
Governor Kelly Ayotte (R), in office since January 2025. New Hampshire governors serve two-year terms, so the seat is on the November 3, 2026 ballot with a primary September 8. Forecasters rate the race Likely or Solid Republican
Commissioner of Education Caitlin Davis, confirmed unanimously by the Executive Council on July 30, 2025 for a four-year term. She is not Frank Edelblut, who stepped down; the department's own page still showed him as commissioner in August 2026, so do not trust that page
State chief selection Unusual. Under RSA 21-N:3 the Governor appoints after consulting the State Board, but the appointment needs the consent of the Executive Council, a separately elected five-member body that holds a real veto. All five Council seats are also on the November ballot
State Board of Education 7 members, 5 from the Executive Council districts and 2 at large, all appointed by the Governor with Executive Council consent, serving five-year terms
Local control Stronger than anywhere else in this batch. Voters decide school budgets directly, and a new law puts the central office budget on its own ballot line (see Section 6)
Scale
Districts and SAUs Two different things, and the distinction matters. Roughly 160 school districts, each with its own board, budget, and vote, grouped into School Administrative Units numbered 1 to 106 that employ the superintendent and central office. One unit can serve several districts. Current counts are hard to verify; the best public figures date to 2022
Students / schools 160,323 students in 2025-26, down from 162,660 the year before and from more than 205,000 twenty years ago. Roughly 456 schools on a 2022 count. Legislators project about 1% annual decline for at least another decade
Regional layer None. School Administrative Units are administrative, not service agencies, and hold no purchasing power. There is no equivalent of a BOCES, Intermediate Unit, or education service center (see Sections 2 and 7)
Largest districts Manchester, Nashua, Concord, Rochester, and Derry. Even the largest are modest by national standards, and most of the state is very small districts
Money & Calendar
Funding formula Adequate education aid: a base of $4,351 per student for FY2027, plus $2,441 for each student eligible for free or reduced-price meals, $2,229 for each student in special education, and $849 for each English learner. New Hampshire relies on local property tax more than any other state
Budget cycle Biennial, passed in odd years. State fiscal year July 1 to June 30. The FY2026-2027 budget passed in 2025, so 2026 was a policy year. The next budget is written in 2027
A number worth knowing Total adequate education aid runs about $1.06 billion for FY2027, but roughly $364 million of that is a statewide education property tax raised and kept locally. About a third of what is called state aid never leaves the town
Legislative session Annual, January to roughly June. Note that major education policy often rides the budget trailer bill in odd years rather than appearing as an education bill
Local approval mechanism Voters, in March, one of two ways. In traditional districts they assemble at a school district meeting and can amend the budget on the floor. In official ballot towns they debate and amend at a February deliberative session and then vote yes or no on a March ballot. If the budget fails, a default budget takes over (see Section 6)
A second vote, new this year A 2026 law puts a school district tax cap question on the November 2026 and November 2028 general election ballots statewide, so districts face a vote in November as well as in March
Quick Yes/No
State AI guidance for schools? NO. New Hampshire is the only state in this batch with no state AI guidance at all. What exists is a voluntary 77-page document published in June 2025 by a coalition of associations, not by the state (see Section 4)
Cell phone restriction? YES, and it is a year old. RSA 189:1-a requires every board to adopt a policy banning personal devices from the first bell to the dismissal bell, all grades, effective July 1, 2025, with an annual review duty (see Section 4)
Screen time action? NO. Both 2026 bills, an app store age verification act and a broader age verification bill, were killed. No New Hampshire law creates any district obligation here (see Section 4)
Student data privacy law? YES, and it contains a rule most people miss: installing monitoring software on student devices requires board approval after a public hearing plus written parental consent (see Section 4)
SDPC / standard NDPA state? YES, with out-of-state ownership. The alliance was founded by the state CTO Council and is operated under contract by a Massachusetts cooperative at $1.10 per student per year, capped at $5,000
1:1 / state device program? No state device program and no state network. The one genuine statewide platform is Khan Academy's AI tutor, made available free to all New Hampshire educators and students through the Department
ESA / voucher program? YES, and universal since June 2025. Education Freedom Accounts served 10,510 students in 2025-26 at roughly $4,911 each, with a cap of 12,500 for 2027 and costs roughly doubling toward $50 million (see Section 3)
Collective bargaining Permitted. Two unions, with NEA-New Hampshire the larger at about 17,000 members. Neither is close to the levers of power the way Rhode Island's is

2. Where the Focus Is

Last verified: August 17, 2026

New Hampshire is the only state in this batch where the courts have twice ruled the funding system unconstitutional and the legislature's answer was to redefine the obligation rather than fund it. The state is now asking its Supreme Court to overturn the thirty-year-old rulings that created the obligation in the first place. Underneath that, districts face something no other state in this batch does: their budgets go to voters twice this year, in March as always and again in November under a new tax cap law. The two lists below are ranked by attention consumed.

What superintendents are focused on

  • Their operating model is on the ballot twice in one year: The March budget vote is normal. What is new is that a 2026 law puts a school district tax cap question on the November 2026 general election ballot statewide, and a cap once adopted stands two years and takes a supermajority to remove. A separate 2026 law now requires the School Administrative Unit budget, which funds the central office, to appear as its own warrant article so voters can strike it directly. A third bill would remove the default budget cushion entirely. See Sections 3 and 6.

  • A scheduled, multi-year revenue decline with no state offset: Stabilization grants are phasing out through 2034 and FY2027 pays 80% of the FY2025 amount. In FY2026, 145 municipalities received less aid than the year before, and 109 of them are hold-harmless towns where supplemental aid does not cover the cut. Derry, Laconia, Concord, and Weare were hit hardest. This is not a forecast; it is a published schedule. See Section 6.

  • Courts said the funding is unconstitutional and the answer was a definition change: The Supreme Court held in July 2025 that base adequacy of about $4,266 per student did not meet constitutional muster and affirmed a trial court finding that $7,356 was the supported minimum, while declining to order payment. A trial judge found the model unconstitutional again in August 2025. The legislature's response, signed in March 2026, redefined adequacy so that targeted aid counts toward it and removed the link to the state's own minimum standards, adding no money. The state is now asking the Supreme Court to overturn the Claremont rulings outright. See Section 3.

  • Money leaving the system, and a new duty to serve the students who left: Education Freedom Accounts became universal in June 2025 and served 10,510 students in 2025-26, with spending expected to roughly double toward $50 million. A 2026 law now requires districts to provide curricular classes to those students without compensation. Both unions opposed it. See Section 3.

  • Enrollment falling, buildings aging, and a new path to state takeover: Enrollment is down to 160,323 and projected to fall about 1% a year for a decade. Roughly 42% of school buildings predate 1950 and about 24% contain asbestos or lead, against a building aid program where more than half the appropriation services old debt. Meanwhile 2026 legislation created state receivership for financially distressed districts, with a state administrator able to override the board, terminate staff, and cancel contracts. Claremont's $5 million shortfall is the exhibit. See Sections 3 and 10.

What technology leaders are focused on

  • Every AI decision is local, with local liability, and the state has said nothing: New Hampshire has published no state AI guidance. That was still true as of July 2026. The only artifact is a voluntary 77-page document produced in June 2025 by the superintendents, principals, school boards, special education administrators, and technology associations together. Survey data cited in mid-2026 put more than 60% of teachers reporting no guidance at all on applying AI to instruction. Meanwhile the state made Khan Academy's AI tutor free statewide. Districts like Manchester are writing their own policies, with district counsel conceding publicly that AI detection tools are unreliable. See Section 4.

  • A consent rule that quietly governs the whole student safety monitoring category: Under RSA 189:68, installing monitoring software on student devices requires school board approval after a public hearing, plus written parental consent. The same structure applies to tracking devices and to classroom recording, which additionally needs the teacher's consent. This reaches every device-side monitoring or remote-view product on the market, and most people outside New Hampshire do not know it exists. It was amended in 2025 through the budget trailer bill and the change has not been characterized publicly. See Section 4.

  • The technology budget now sits on a ballot line voters can strike: District technology usually lives in the School Administrative Unit budget, and a 2026 law makes that budget a separate warrant article rather than part of the district budget. A second 2026 law caps central office administrative expense. Together they turn central office spending, which includes technology, into a standalone target in a state where voters decide directly. See Sections 3 and 6.

  • Contract structure is risk management, not paperwork: In official ballot districts, if voters reject the budget the district runs on a default budget calculated as last year's budget plus obligations already incurred or mandated by law, minus one-time costs. A signed multi-year contract survives that, because it is an obligation already incurred. A year-to-year subscription does not, and neither does general cost inflation. A pending bill would remove the default budget entirely and simply revert to the prior year. How a technology contract is structured determines whether it survives a failed vote. See Section 6.

  • One shared rail in the whole state, and it is run from Massachusetts: There is no regional service agency, no state network, no state device program, and no statewide bidding threshold. The single shared contracting rail is the state student data privacy agreement, run through an alliance founded by the state CTO Council but operated under contract by a Massachusetts cooperative at $1.10 per student per year. The template on file dates to 2019. Separately, RSA 189:66 requires every district to maintain an annually updated data governance plan covering software inventory, privacy, breach response, and service provider standards, a duty many districts treat as dormant. See Sections 4 and 7.

The regional service agency layer

New Hampshire has no regional service agency with purchasing power, so this profile carries no third lens. School Administrative Units are administrative bodies under RSA 194-C that provide central administration to their own member districts; they hold no master contracts other districts can ride and are not intermediaries between the state and districts. One organization, the Southeastern Regional Education Service Center in Bedford, resembles a Massachusetts collaborative but is services only, weighted toward special education and professional development, with nothing on purchasing or technology. What fills the gap is a split: the state CTO Council is the decision channel where technology leaders gather and which founded the state privacy alliance, a Massachusetts cooperative is the contracting rail for that alliance, national cooperatives substitute for a purchasing agency, and the school boards association's policy service is how model policy actually reaches districts. Sections 7 and 11 carry the detail.

3. What the State Is Driving

Last verified: August 17, 2026

More than one in five bills filed in the 2026 session were education bills, so this was the dominant subject of the year. The through-line across all five items below is the same: narrow what the state owes, expand what families can take elsewhere, and push cost decisions down to local voters. One live note: the legislature convenes on August 19, 2026, two days from now, to take up 31 of the Governor's record 33 vetoes, several of them education bills, so check outcomes before any meeting after that date.

NARROWING THE CONSTITUTIONAL OBLIGATION | The state lost twice in court, responded by redefining what an adequate education means, and is now asking the Supreme Court to overturn thirty years of precedent. No new money.

  • What the courts found: In the ConVal case the Supreme Court held on July 1, 2025, three to two, that a base adequacy figure of about $4,266 per student did not support constitutional muster, and affirmed the trial court's finding that $7,356 was a supported minimum. It reversed the order compelling immediate payment on separation of powers grounds, so there is no court deadline. Compliance would cost more than $500 million a year, about a 50% increase in state education spending. In a separate case the Court upheld the statewide education property tax in June 2025, including the rule letting property-wealthy towns keep the excess. In August 2025 the same trial judge again found the model unconstitutional, including on special education, and again declined to set a legislative deadline.

  • What the legislature did instead: In January 2026 the House voted 190 to 155 to kill a bill that would have raised per-pupil adequacy to the court's figure. In March 2026 the Governor signed a law that redefines the state's adequacy payment as one component of the cost of education rather than the whole, counts targeted aid for special education, low income, and English learners toward the adequacy obligation, and removes the requirement that an adequate education meet the state's own minimum standards. It adds no money. Public testimony ran 1,033 opposed to 8 in support.

  • The escalation that most people have not registered: In the appeal filed February 25, 2026, the state's Solicitor General asked the Supreme Court to overturn the Claremont rulings, arguing the state constitution imposes neither a quality standard for education nor a state funding obligation. That is a request to remove the foundation of New Hampshire school finance entirely. It is pending and no argument date has been published.

  • Why this reaches technology: The same 2026 law severed adequacy from the state's minimum standards for school approval. Those standards are where class size, course requirements, and program expectations live. Once adequacy no longer has to fund them, the standards become aspirational. Districts read that as the state stepping back from what a school must provide.

Source: NH Bulletin on the ConVal ruling | ConVal v. State, Supreme Court opinion | NH Bulletin on the renewed unconstitutionality finding | Concord Monitor on the adequacy redefinition law | InDepthNH on the request to overturn Claremont

UNIVERSAL SCHOOL CHOICE, NOW UNDER BIPARTISAN OVERSIGHT PRESSURE | Enacted and growing fast, with a 44-finding audit, a rejected rules petition, and a new duty on districts to serve the students who left.

  • How it works and how big it is: Education Freedom Accounts became universal in June 2025 when income limits were removed, making New Hampshire the eighteenth state with universal private school choice. Enrollment reached 10,510 students in 2025-26 at roughly $4,911 per student, against a statutory cap of 12,500 for 2027. Spending was $20.2 million in 2024-25 across more than 65,000 transactions and is projected to roughly double toward $50 million.

  • Who actually runs it, which surprises people: Not the Department of Education. A nonprofit contractor, the Children's Scholarship Fund New Hampshire, approves vendors and expenses and holds the data. The Department has told records requesters that much of the data being sought is not in its possession. Of 2024-25 spending, 57% went to tuition, with the top twenty recipients almost entirely Christian academies. The remaining 43% went to curriculum, online programs, technology, and homeschool supplies, with a single online retailer capturing about 15% of curriculum spending.

  • The oversight fight, which is not partisan: Lawmakers from both parties petitioned the State Board in April 2026 for clearer rules on qualifying expenses and vendors. The Board rejected the petition on procedural grounds on June 11, 2026. Commissioner Davis has said the Department will revisit the rules within a year after receiving an audit report containing 44 findings, and the audit was expanded in May 2026 to residency and educational attainment data. The contractor removed vendor order detail from recent reports citing harassment concerns. A Republican-leaning town even passed a warrant article asking for financial need restrictions.

  • The part that lands directly on districts: A 2026 law requires districts to provide curricular classes to Education Freedom Account students without compensation. Both teachers unions opposed it, arguing it means fewer opportunities for enrolled students. For a superintendent this is money out and workload in, at the same time.

Source: Coverage of universal eligibility | NH Bulletin on Education Freedom Account oversight | NH Bulletin on Education Freedom Account spending | Reaching Higher NH on Education Freedom Accounts | NH School Scoop session wrap

PUTTING LOCAL COST DISCIPLINE ON THE BALLOT | Three enacted laws and one pending bill that together move spending decisions to voters and make the central office the target.

  • A tax cap question on the general election ballot: A law signed in mid-July 2026 forces a school district local tax cap question onto the November 2026 and November 2028 general election ballots statewide, and limits central office administrative expense. A cap, once adopted, stands two years and takes a supermajority to overturn. This is the first time most districts will face a budget-related vote in November as well as March.

  • The central office budget on its own ballot line: A separate 2026 law requires the School Administrative Unit budget to appear as a distinct warrant article, so voters vote on central administration directly rather than inside the district budget. If rejected, the unit runs on a level-funded prior-year budget. Because district technology, business services, and curriculum staff usually sit in that budget, this makes them individually vulnerable.

  • State takeover of districts that fail financially: 2026 legislation created a receivership framework: the State Board may place a district on probation for up to six months, a state-appointed administrator may override the school board, terminate staff, and cancel contracts, the window to initiate an audit drops from twelve months to four, and daily non-compliance fines double to $500. Claremont's $5 million shortfall, which left the district on a bank loan into April 2026, is the case that produced it. Confirm final enactment details before relying on specifics.

  • The cushion that may disappear: A pending bill would remove the default budget option entirely, so a rejected budget would simply revert to the prior year with staffing and program cuts, rather than to a default budget that includes contractual obligations. It crossed over and had a Senate hearing in April 2026, and no source records how it finished. If it passes, multi-year contracts lose the protection described in Section 6.

Source: InDepthNH on the local school budget cap law | Reaching Higher NH on local control legislation | NH School Scoop session wrap | NH Municipal Association on the default budget | NHFPI 2026 legislative wrap-up

PHONE-FREE SCHOOLS, NOW A YEAR OLD AND BEING CAMPAIGNED ON | Enacted through the 2025 budget trailer bill, implemented statewide, surveyed, and politically owned. Section 4 carries the mechanics.

  • Why the state pushed it, and how it actually passed: Three separate phone bills were in play in 2025. One died in March, one collapsed in a conference committee in June, and one was vetoed in July. The ban that took effect did so through the budget trailer bill, which is why people looking for a phone law by name cannot find one. The Governor's veto message said plainly that she was grateful the language had made it into the budget she signed. This is a useful lesson about New Hampshire: in odd years, watch the trailer bill, not the education bills.

  • What year one actually showed: The Department surveyed teachers and administrators in spring 2026. Between 75% and 77% of teachers rarely or never see non-instructional phone use, up from 68% a year earlier. Around 80% of teachers and 88% of administrators call it effective at reducing distraction, and 75% report improved student focus. But only 38% report improved academic outcomes and only 30% report fewer disciplinary incidents. Be careful with the claims here; the evidence supports attention, not achievement.

  • The gap that is the actual management problem: About 50% of teachers say consequences are applied consistently, against about 80% of administrators. That 30-point perception gap between the people enforcing the rule and the people setting it is the real implementation issue in year two, and it is a practical conversation rather than a policy one.

  • The unfunded part: No state money for storage was appropriated; the fiscal note called costs indeterminable. Districts bought lockers, pouches, and containers themselves. About 60% simply require phones out of sight, 25% use lockers or containers, and the rest use pouches or confiscation.

Source: RSA 189:1-a, personal communication devices | NH Bulletin on the year one survey | InDepthNH on the phone survey | NH Bulletin on phone ban implementation

DEREGULATING HOME EDUCATION AND WHAT A SCHOOL MUST PROVIDE | Two quieter changes that together loosen what the state requires, both already in effect.

  • Home education, now largely on the honor system: A 2026 law effective in July makes notification to the state or district optional, eliminates the annual evaluation requirement, and makes record keeping optional, while preserving homeschooled students' free access to public school classes and extracurricular activities. Districts therefore keep the obligation to serve without the ability to know who is out there.

  • The minimum standards rewrite, landing this school year: The State Board adopted a rewrite of the minimum standards for public school approval in December 2024 after a party-line committee vote that acknowledged overwhelming public opposition. It eliminated maximum class size caps in favor of undefined student-educator ratios and replaced courses with loosely defined learning opportunities. Graduation credit changes take effect in 2026-27, meaning now.

  • Why the two changes compound: The 2026 adequacy law removed the requirement that an adequate education meet these minimum standards, which is precisely what critics warned about when the standards were rewritten. Taken together the state has loosened what schools must provide and severed the funding obligation from it.

  • A stale page to avoid: The General Court's published rule text for the minimum standards still shows effective dates of 2014, 2019, and 2022 and does not reflect the December 2024 adoption. Anyone reading that page will get the current rule wrong.

Source: Reaching Higher NH on the minimum standards rewrite | NH School Scoop session wrap | NH Bulletin on the adequacy bill | Reaching Higher NH on local control legislation

4. The Four National Questions

Last verified: August 17, 2026

These are the four questions every state is answering, and the point of this section is comparability. New Hampshire's pattern is the sparest in this batch: it has acted decisively on phones and on almost nothing else, it has published no AI guidance at all, and its student privacy chapter contains one rule that constrains ed-tech more than most states' entire privacy regimes. Where a topic is also a state initiative, Section 3 explains why the state pushed it and this section carries the rules and dates.

ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE | Nothing from the state. No guidance, no mandate, no money, no reporting. The only document is voluntary and was written by the associations themselves.

  • The finding, stated plainly: The Department of Education has not released official AI guidance, a point restated in New Hampshire commentary as recently as July 2026. The state does not appear on national trackers of departments that have issued K-12 AI guidance, and the 2026 legislative tracker covering 77 AI education bills across 27 states does not list New Hampshire at all. There is no district AI policy requirement and no deadline.

  • What fills the vacuum: A 77-page advisory document published in June 2025 by a coalition of the superintendents association, the principals association, the school boards association, the special education administrators association, the state technology in education society, and a learning initiative. It is advisory and it is not a state document, but it is the de facto standard because it is the only thing that exists.

  • What the legislature did: A comprehensive AI act covering an AI council, consumer disclosure, anti-discrimination provisions, biometric limits, and a regulatory sandbox was killed on the House floor on February 19, 2026 as inexpedient to legislate. Nothing replaced it.

  • What the state did do, and what districts are doing: The Department made Khan Academy's AI tutor available free to all New Hampshire educators and students, which is the one statewide AI asset. The Commissioner has described AI as comparable to a pocket calculator fifty years ago, favoring instructor-led use with student privacy at the center, which is permissive rather than directive. Districts are filling the gap themselves: Manchester adopted a revised AI policy in April 2026 naming three approved platforms, requiring disclosure of AI assistance, and linking to academic integrity, with district counsel acknowledging publicly that detection tools are unreliable.

Source: NH Bulletin on a path forward for AI in schools | NH Bulletin on the association AI guidance | HB 1725 bill text and history | FutureEd state AI legislation tracker | Coverage of the Manchester AI policy

SCREEN TIME | Nothing enacted, and both 2026 bills were killed outright. Zero obligations on districts, and the cleanest nothing-to-do-here in this batch.

  • What was proposed and what happened: An App Store Accountability Act would have required age verification at account creation and parental consent for minors' downloads and in-app purchases, taking effect January 1, 2027. It was killed in the House on March 11, 2026 as inexpedient to legislate. A separate age verification bill for commercial sites distributing material harmful to minors, narrowed by the Senate to pornographic sites, passed the Senate in March and was killed in the House on May 14, 2026.

  • Who was lobbying: The technology trade association that has litigated against similar laws in other states testified against a New Hampshire identification mandate bill, so industry was actively engaged in Concord. No New Hampshire age verification or addictive feed litigation exists, because no such law exists.

  • The law that sounds relevant and is not: RSA 189:70 addresses educational institution policies on social media, but it is the older statute limiting a school's ability to demand a student's social media credentials. It is not a platform regulation or an addictive design law, and it should not be cited as one.

  • Where the actual change came from: What altered student screen behavior in New Hampshire was the phone ban, not any social media law. A wraparound children's mental health services bill also failed in 2026 with the Governor opposed, so the youth mental health track has not produced district obligations either.

Source: HB 1658, app store accountability | SB 648, age verification | RSA chapter 189, table of contents

CELL PHONES / CONNECTED DEVICES | In law since July 1, 2025, first bell to dismissal bell, all grades, with a mandatory local policy and an annual review duty most boards forget.

  • The law and how to cite it: RSA 189:1-a, paragraph V, enacted through the 2025 budget trailer bill effective July 1, 2025, with a further amendment effective July 15, 2025. There is no standalone phone act to point to, which is why searching for one fails. School boards and chartered public school trustees must develop and adopt a policy prohibiting all personal communication device use by students from when the first bell rings to start instructional time until the dismissal bell ends the academic school day.

  • Scope, and two duties that are easy to miss: Bell to bell, not instructional time only, and all grades with no distinction, including chartered public schools. The policy must be developed in collaboration with parents and teachers, and it must be reviewed annually. That annual review is a recurring board agenda item and it is the duty districts most often let lapse. No adoption deadline appears in the statute, and the school boards association distributed a sample policy to get boards compliant quickly.

  • Exemptions, which are mandatory rather than discretionary: The statute protects students with medical needs such as insulin pumps and glucose sensors, students with disabilities using a device identified in an individualized education program or Section 504 plan, and emergent multilingual students needing language access under federal civil rights law. The superintendent or a designee approves case-by-case exceptions for medical, disability, or language proficiency need.

  • School-issued devices, with an honest caveat: The statute regulates personal communication devices, so a district's own fleet sits outside it, and Department guidance clarified that laptops need not be banned where used academically. But the words school-issued do not appear in the statutory text, so this is inference plus guidance rather than an express carve-out, and several districts wrote personal laptops and tablets into their own bans. A district's 1:1 fleet is safe; a student's own laptop generally is not. No state funding for storage was appropriated.

Source: RSA 189:1-a, personal communication devices | NH Bulletin on phone ban implementation | NH Bulletin on the year one survey | NHSBA policy services

STUDENT DATA PRIVACY | A vendor conduct law, an annual district planning duty, and one consent rule that constrains the entire student monitoring category. No district breach clock in this chapter.

  • The consent rule that matters most, and that outsiders miss: Under RSA 189:68, installing remote monitoring software on student devices requires school board approval after a public hearing plus written parental consent, with narrow exceptions for recovering stolen or damaged devices. The same structure governs tracking devices, where parental consent may be withheld without consequence, and classroom recording for teacher evaluation, which additionally requires the teacher's written consent and every affected student's parent's consent. This reaches essentially every device-side monitoring, filtering with remote view, and student safety alerting product. It was amended in 2025 through the budget trailer bill and that change has not been publicly characterized, so confirm the current text.

  • The annual duty districts forget: RSA 189:66 requires every local education agency to develop and present a data governance plan, updated annually, covering software inventories, privacy protections, breach response, and service provider standards. It is the closest New Hampshire comes to a district vendor management mandate, and in many districts it is dormant. The same section requires districts to publicize federal student records rights including inspection of records within 14 days, which is a real and checkable duty.

  • The vendor law, and what it does not do: RSA 189:68-a governs operators of sites, applications, and services directed at K-12 school purposes. It bars targeted advertising using student data, building student profiles, selling student information, and disclosure except by judicial process, and requires reasonable security and deletion of student data on school request. Note the limits: it does not prescribe required contract clauses, a public posting duty, or a breach notification clock, which is where it differs sharply from Connecticut.

  • Where the breach clock actually lives, and the shared instrument: There is no district-level breach clock in the student privacy chapter; the statute imposes notification duties on the Department rather than on districts, and district obligations run through the general personal information breach law. The shared contracting instrument is the New Hampshire Student Data Privacy Agreement, through an alliance founded by the state CTO Council and operated under contract by a Massachusetts cooperative at $1.10 per student per year capped at $5,000. The published template dates to 2019, so check for a newer version.

Source: RSA chapter 189, table of contents | New Hampshire student data privacy alliance | The Education Cooperative data privacy program | New Hampshire student data privacy agreement | SDPC New Hampshire alliance

5. Governance & Decision-Making

Last verified: August 17, 2026

Authority runs Governor to Commissioner and the Department of Education, with a seven-member State Board setting rules, down to roughly 160 school districts grouped into School Administrative Units. The New Hampshire quirk is the Executive Council: a separately elected five-member body that must consent to the Commissioner's appointment and to every State Board appointment. Under RSA 21-N:3 the Governor appoints the Commissioner after consulting the State Board, for a four-year term, with Council consent. All five Council seats are on the November ballot alongside the governorship.

  • Leadership, and a stale official page to avoid: Commissioner Caitlin Davis was confirmed unanimously by the Executive Council on July 30, 2025 for a term running to 2029. She spent fifteen years inside the department, most recently as its budget and finance analyst, and drew support from both the teachers unions and school choice advocates. Her predecessor stepped down after being asked not to seek another term and endorsed her. Note that the department's own page about the commissioner still showed the previous commissioner in August 2026, so anyone relying on the official site will get this wrong. The State Board chair is not named on any dated source; verify before naming anyone.

  • Election-year context, provisional note: Everything turns over at once. The governorship, all 400 House seats, all 24 Senate seats, and all five Executive Council seats are on the November 3, 2026 ballot, with a primary September 8. The Senate currently holds a veto-proof Republican majority. Because the Executive Council confirms the Commissioner and every State Board member, and because education committee chairs can change entirely, a superintendent's 2027 legislative environment is genuinely unknown. Treat leadership risk here as the highest in this batch.

  • Districts and administrative units, which outsiders regularly confuse: A school district is the legal and taxing body: it has an elected board, holds an annual meeting, raises money, owns buildings, and employs staff. A School Administrative Unit is a separate legal corporation formed by one or more districts under RSA 194-C to provide shared central administration, including the superintendent, business office, curriculum, and special education compliance. One unit can serve several districts, each of which keeps its own board, budget, and vote. The unit is governed by a joint board of members drawn from the constituent districts, with voting weighted by enrollment. Creating, merging, or withdrawing from a unit requires a three-fifths majority in each affected district.

  • Why that distinction decides who you are actually selling to: The buying entity is usually the administrative unit, because that is where the technology director and the business administrator sit. But the money and the vote belong to each district. A three-district unit may need three separate warrant articles to fund one purchase. The first structural question to ask in New Hampshire is how many districts are in this unit and whether they all fund this.

  • The regional layer, stated plainly: There is none with purchasing power. Administrative units serve their own members and hold no master contracts others can ride. One organization in Bedford resembles a Massachusetts collaborative but does special education services and professional development rather than purchasing or technology. Section 7 carries what fills the gap.

  • Legislature, and an unusual committee structure: New Hampshire splits education policy from education funding in both chambers, which is rare and worth knowing when tracking a bill. The House has Education Policy and Administration, chaired by Rep. Kristin Noble, and Education Funding, chaired by Rep. Rick Ladd. The Senate has Education, chaired by Sen. Ruth Ward, and Education Finance, chaired by Sen. Keith Murphy. Rep. Bob Lynn, a former Chief Justice of the state Supreme Court, authored the 2026 adequacy redefinition and is the most consequential education legislator of the year. Note that several briefing documents still name a different House Education chair; the roster changed.

6. Funding & Budget Rhythm

Last verified: August 17, 2026. FY2026-2027 biennial budget in effect

  • How the formula works, and the number that is not what it seems: Adequate education aid is a per-student base plus targeted add-ons. For FY2027 the base is $4,351 per student, with $2,441 more for each student eligible for free or reduced-price meals, about 26% of students, $2,229 more for each student in special education, about 21%, and $849 more for each English learner, about 4%. Total adequate education aid runs about $1.06 billion. But roughly $364 million of that is the statewide education property tax, which is raised locally and, since a 2011 law upheld in June 2025, kept locally by property-wealthy towns. About a third of what is called state aid never leaves the town, and one coastal town generates almost $48,000 per pupil against an obligation of about $4,600 and keeps the difference.

  • The local approval mechanism, stated explicitly: Voters decide, in March, and there are two systems. In traditional districts, voters assemble at a school district meeting, debate warrant articles, amend them on the floor, and vote the same day. In official ballot districts, operating under RSA 40:13, there are two sessions: a deliberative session in late January or early February where voters debate and amend the wording, and then a ballot vote usually the second Tuesday in March where voters simply mark yes or no with no debate and no amendment. Which system a district uses is the first thing to establish, and no current count of which districts use which is published anywhere.

  • The default budget, which is the single most important mechanism in New Hampshire: In an official ballot district, if voters reject the budget the district does not shut down and does not automatically get a re-vote. It runs on a default budget, calculated as last year's approved operating budget, plus amounts previously incurred or mandated by law such as debt service, signed multi-year contracts, and legally required insurance, minus one-time expenditures that are not expected to recur. Critically, it does not include general cost inflation: if electricity or a software renewal costs more this year, the default budget does not cover the increase. The school board alone sets the default number and the deliberative session cannot change it, though it must be disclosed at the first public budget hearing on a prescribed form. After a failed vote the board may let the default stand or call a special meeting to propose a revised budget.

  • Why that makes contract structure a strategic decision: A signed multi-year contract survives a failed budget, because it is an obligation previously incurred and rolls into the default budget. A year-to-year subscription with no binding term does not, and is exposed the moment a budget fails. In a state where budgets fail regularly, how a technology agreement is structured is risk management rather than paperwork. A pending 2026 bill would remove the default budget entirely and revert to the prior year's budget, which would strip even that protection; no source records how it finished.

  • Two new pressures on top of the March vote: A 2026 law puts a school district tax cap question on the November 2026 and November 2028 general election ballots statewide and limits central office administrative expense. A second 2026 law requires the administrative unit budget to appear as its own warrant article, so central office spending is voted separately; if rejected, the unit runs level-funded. See Section 3.

  • The aid decline that is already scheduled: Stabilization grants are phasing out through 2034, with FY2027 paying 80% of the FY2025 amount. In FY2026, 145 municipalities received less than the prior year, and 109 of those were hold-harmless towns where supplemental aid does not offset the cut. Derry, Laconia, Concord, and Weare were hit hardest; Manchester and Nashua gained. A new fiscal capacity disparity aid program reaches 34 municipalities at a median of about $320 per pupil, which is small against the phase-out. Property taxes fund about 61% of district revenue, the highest local reliance in the country.

  • School building aid, where demand far exceeds supply: New Hampshire ran a building aid moratorium from roughly 2008 to 2022, funding only three projects in about thirteen years and accumulating a backlog. The program resumed with a $50 million annual cap, later raised, but more than half the appropriation services debt on pre-moratorium projects, leaving a fraction for new work against demand several times larger. About 42% of school buildings predate 1950 and about 24% contain asbestos or lead. The last statewide facilities survey was in 2019 and no new one is planned. Current-year appropriation figures are not published in a reachable source, so ask rather than quote.

  • Where technology money lives: In the administrative unit budget, which is now its own ballot line, or in the district operating budget, which goes to voters in March. There is no state technology fund, no device program, and no state network. Federal funds carry their own procurement rules regardless of state law. The practical consequence is that a technology proposal has to be in a board's hands by roughly October or November to make the March ballot, which sets the entire selling calendar in this state.

7. Procurement & Vendor Path

Last verified: August 17, 2026

  • There is no state bidding threshold for school districts: The state purchasing chapter at RSA 21-I applies to entities operated by or for the state, and its $10,000 bid waiver threshold is a state agency threshold, not a district one. Nothing in it extends state contracts or purchasing authority to districts. The only school-related cooperative provision is narrow and covers food supplies. The school district chapter, RSA 194, contains no competitive bidding provisions at all; it addresses district powers, joint maintenance agreements, long-term contracts capped at twenty years, and contracts with schools. Thresholds are therefore set by local board policy. Ask each district for its purchasing policy; there is no statewide answer.

  • What carries the purchasing load, since no regional agency does: Nothing, at scale, and that is the finding. This is the through-line from Section 2. There is no BOCES to be listed with, no technology center that acts as the contracting party, and no intermediate unit rail. Districts buy individually, through their administrative unit, with national cooperatives as the practical substitute for a purchasing agency, though New Hampshire district usage of those cooperatives is not published in a public source. Joint arrangements among public agencies are possible under RSA 53-A but are not a standing purchasing vehicle.

  • The one genuine shared rail, and it is run from out of state: The New Hampshire Student Data Privacy Agreement, through an alliance founded by the state CTO Council and operated under contract by The Education Cooperative, a Massachusetts nonprofit that has served Massachusetts districts since 1968 and runs privacy alliances in several states. Districts pay $1.10 per student per year, capped at $5,000. For any vendor touching student data this is the gate, and it is the only multi-district contracting channel in New Hampshire ed-tech. The published template dates to 2019, so check whether a newer version exists before assuming terms.

  • The decision channel, which is separate from the contracting rail: The New Hampshire CTO Council is where district technology leaders actually gather. It is a CoSN affiliate, it founded the privacy alliance, and it runs an annual clinic. It has no contracting power, but it is the room where the shared judgments get made. That split, one body for influence and an out-of-state cooperative for contracts, is unusual and worth understanding before planning any statewide approach.

  • How model policy actually reaches districts: Through the school boards association's policy service, which districts subscribe to for a modest annual fee and which maintains a sample policy database with legal references. That service wrote and distributed the sample cell phone policy that got boards compliant quickly in 2025. If you want to understand how a statewide policy change becomes 160 local policies in New Hampshire, that is the mechanism.

  • Two compliance items that belong in a procurement conversation: First, any product that installs monitoring or remote-view software on a student device requires board approval after a public hearing and written parental consent under RSA 189:68. That is a procurement timeline issue, not just a legal one, because it adds a public meeting to the path. Second, RSA 189:66 requires an annually updated district data governance plan covering software inventory and service provider standards, which means a district should already have a list of what it runs. See Section 4.

8. Technology & Digital Learning Posture

Last verified: August 17, 2026

  • Devices: No state 1:1 program, no device standard, and no state device funding. What changed the demand picture is the phone ban: because the statute reaches personal devices and department guidance clarified that academic use of laptops need not be banned, the practical effect has been pressure to make sure school-provided devices are available in class. Several districts wrote personal laptops and tablets into their bans, which pushes more instructional load onto the district fleet.

  • Assessment: The state's assessment delivery vendor and platform is not published in a public source, because the department's assessment pages were unreachable and the vendor portal blocks automated access. What is confirmed is the department's assessment electronic device policy, dated February 1, 2024, which requires devices collected before testing, powered off and stored away, staff monitoring during breaks, and test machines pre-configured to restrict access to everything except the secure test browser, with exemptions for documented medical need and for devices in an individualized education program or Section 504 plan. Confirm the current vendor directly rather than assuming.

  • Statewide platforms, of which there is essentially one: Khan Academy's AI tutor, made available free to all New Hampshire educators and students through the Department. That is the one genuine statewide platform. There is no state student information system, no state learning platform, and no statewide K-12 network or broadband consortium of the kind New York, Ohio, or Rhode Island operate. Everything else is district by district.

  • The constraint that shapes the whole student safety category: RSA 189:68 requires school board approval after a public hearing plus written parental consent before monitoring software is installed on student devices, with narrow exceptions for recovering stolen or damaged equipment. The same structure governs tracking devices and classroom recording. This reaches device-side monitoring, remote viewing, and student safety alerting products, and it means the purchase path includes a public meeting and a consent process. It was amended in 2025 through the budget trailer bill and the change has not been publicly characterized. See Section 4.

  • Cybersecurity, where there is no mandate and no verified program: No state cybersecurity requirement on districts, no incident reporting clock in the student privacy chapter, and no state K-12 cyber program could be verified. The nearest thing is the annual data governance plan duty, which includes breach response. Notably, no recent New Hampshire district cyber incident appears in any public source either, so treat the absence as unknown rather than as a clean record. The one funded state infrastructure item is emergency response mapping of schools, authorized in April 2026 from the state public school infrastructure fund, which is physical security rather than cyber.

  • AI capacity building: Effectively none from the state. The associations produced the guidance, Khan Academy provides the tool, and districts write their own policies. There is no state AI training program, no pilot, no funding line, and no procurement rubric. In a state that has published no guidance at all, the capacity gap is not a nuance; it is the situation.

  • Framing: New Hampshire frames technology through a local control and parental consent lens rather than an instructional or compliance one. The state has almost no technology policy, and what it does have is about who must agree before something is installed on a child's device. There are no mandates to sell against and no state rails to ride, which means every conversation starts from the district's own judgment and the board's own vote. That is unusual, and it rewards patience over programs.

9. Instructional Priorities & Mandates

Last verified: August 17, 2026

  • What a school must provide has been loosened, and this school year is when it lands: The State Board adopted a rewrite of the minimum standards for public school approval in December 2024. It eliminated maximum class size caps in favor of undefined student-educator ratios and replaced the language of courses with loosely defined learning opportunities. Graduation credit changes, rebalancing toward more mathematics and social studies and less English and electives, take effect in 2026-27. Be careful with the published rule text: the General Court's page still showed 2014, 2019, and 2022 effective dates in August 2026.

  • And the funding obligation has been cut loose from it: The 2026 adequacy law removed the requirement that an adequate education meet those minimum standards. That was the specific mechanism critics warned about when the standards were rewritten. Taken together, the state has both loosened what schools must do and severed its funding obligation from what it asks. See Section 3.

  • Special education, where a court finding met a formula rewrite that arrives late: The trial court's August 2025 ruling found targeted aid, and special education aid in particular, constitutionally insufficient. A 2026 law rewrites the special education formula to add state aid for students costing between 250% and 350% of average per-pupil expenditure, while reducing state coverage above 1,000%. It does not take effect until July 2028. Districts therefore face two more budget years under the formula a judge called inadequate.

  • A new duty on every teacher, with discipline attached: A 2026 law effective in July requires educators to answer written parent inquiries completely and honestly within ten business days, with exceptions where disclosure is legally prohibited or where a good-faith determination of imminent risk applies. The State Board must update the educator code of ethics by July 2027, after which non-compliance becomes a discipline matter. Expect this to appear in bargaining and in professional learning, and note that it creates a records and response workload that lands partly on district systems.

  • Home education is now largely voluntary to report: A 2026 law makes notification to the state or district optional, eliminates the annual evaluation requirement, and makes record keeping optional, while preserving free access to public school classes and extracurricular activities. Districts keep the obligation to serve without a reliable way to know who is out there.

  • Career and technical education, quietly expanded: A 2025 law created an alternate certification path for career and technical education instructors, which addresses one of the more persistent staffing constraints in that area. This is not a headline initiative in New Hampshire the way it is in Rhode Island or Connecticut, but it is the area where the state has loosened a requirement in order to expand access rather than to reduce obligation.

10. Landscape & Pressures

Last verified: August 17, 2026

  • Enrollment decline is long, steady, and now a political argument: 160,323 students in 2025-26, down from 162,660 the year before and from more than 205,000 twenty years ago, with births falling from roughly 14,000 a year in the early 2000s to between 11,000 and 12,000. Legislators project about 1% annual decline for at least another decade. Regional examples are stark: one district fell from 2,497 students in 2011 to 1,862 in 2025-26. The politically consequential part is that class sizes are shrinking faster than staffing, and that observation is the intellectual argument behind the 2026 administrative cost caps.

  • Consolidation politics runs the opposite direction from Vermont: Where Vermont is pushing districts together, New Hampshire's 2026 legislation made it easier to come apart. New laws ease withdrawal from cooperative districts on a three-fifths vote, let twenty voters force a school closure vote, and break the administrative unit budget out for a separate ballot question. The combined effect is that regional arrangements are easier to dismantle and central administration is harder to fund. Anyone reading New England as a single consolidation story will get New Hampshire backwards.

  • Teacher workforce, where the public data is thin: No 2026 statewide shortage dataset was published, so treat any number with caution. The qualitative signals: vacancies concentrated in school psychology, occupational therapy, and speech pathology, with special education vacancies reported in Claremont and Newport. One district approved retirement buyouts of up to $15,000 for teachers retiring by June 2027, which is cost shedding rather than a shortage response. The larger union, at about 17,000 members, campaigns on pay and on the state's last-place ranking for state share of education funding.

  • Facilities, where the backlog is structural: About 42% of school buildings predate 1950 and about 24% contain asbestos or lead, with more than 33,000 students in buildings essentially unchanged for 35 years or more. After a roughly fourteen-year building aid moratorium, the restored program spends more than half its appropriation servicing old debt, and demand has run several times the funds available for new projects. The last statewide facilities survey was 2019 and no replacement is planned, which means nobody has a current picture of the problem.

  • The choice pressure is unusually large for a state this size: Education Freedom Accounts served 10,510 students in 2025-26 with a cap of 12,500 for 2027 and spending heading toward roughly $50 million, in a system of 160,000 students. That is a materially different budget conversation from Rhode Island, which has no voucher at all, or Connecticut and Vermont. Add the 2026 requirement that districts serve those students in curricular classes without compensation and the effect on a district budget is both revenue and workload. See Section 3.

12. Key Notes for Engagement

Last verified: August 17, 2026. WORKING DRAFT for Jeremiah's edit. Research-generated engagement judgment, not settled advice

How to think about New Hampshire right now:

  • Start by asking two structural questions, before anything else: First, how many districts are in this administrative unit and do they all fund this, because the unit employs the technology director but each district votes its own money. Second, is this an official ballot district or a traditional meeting district, because that determines whether a failed budget produces a default budget or a floor fight, and whether a proposal can be amended in the room. Nobody publishes the answer to either question statewide. Asking them early signals you understand the state.

  • The selling calendar is set by March, which means October: Voters decide in March. Official ballot districts finalize wording at a deliberative session in late January or early February. Boards build budgets from October to December and must disclose the default budget at the first public hearing in January. So a proposal has to be in a board's hands by roughly October or November to make the March ballot. Arriving in February is arriving for the following year.

  • Contract structure is the most valuable thing you can get right: In an official ballot district, if the budget fails the district runs on a default budget that includes obligations already incurred, including signed multi-year contracts, but excludes general inflation. A multi-year agreement survives a failed vote. A year-to-year subscription does not. In a state where budgets fail regularly and where a pending bill would remove the default budget altogether, structuring an agreement so it survives is a genuine service to the district, not a sales tactic.

  • Know the monitoring consent rule before any student safety conversation: Installing monitoring software on student devices requires school board approval after a public hearing plus written parental consent. Tracking devices and classroom recording have the same structure, and recording also needs the teacher's consent. Most vendors do not know this exists. Knowing it, and knowing it adds a public meeting to the timeline, will separate you immediately from people who treat New Hampshire as a light-regulation state. It is light on mandates and specific about consent.

  • On AI, New Hampshire has published nothing, and that is the whole opportunity: No state guidance, no mandate, no money, no template, confirmed as recently as July 2026. The only document is a voluntary one the associations wrote themselves in June 2025, and more than 60% of teachers report no guidance at all. Districts like Manchester are writing policies from scratch with counsel conceding detection tools do not work. Helping a district think through policy, professional learning, and a defensible vetting process is straightforwardly useful here in a way it is not in states with a mandate to comply with.

  • Budget empathy means understanding that the technology line is now its own ballot question: The administrative unit budget, where technology usually lives, is now a separate warrant article voters can reject on its own, and a second law caps administrative expense, and a tax cap question goes on the November ballot statewide. A superintendent is defending central office spending in public, by name, twice this year. Any proposal that increases that line needs an argument that works in a school gymnasium, not just in a cabinet meeting.

  • Do not confuse this with the rest of New England: Vermont is consolidating districts; New Hampshire made it easier to break them apart. Connecticut and Rhode Island have no voucher; New Hampshire has a universal one serving more than 10,000 students and now requires districts to teach those students for free. Rhode Island runs a first-in-nation state security service; New Hampshire has no state network at all. The regional framing that works elsewhere in this batch will actively mislead you here.

  • Watch the courts, because the ground could move: The state has asked its Supreme Court to overturn the Claremont rulings that created the constitutional obligation to fund an adequate education. If that succeeds, thirty years of New Hampshire school finance assumptions change. If it fails, pressure returns to a legislature that has already refused to comply once. Either way, district leaders are planning under genuine legal uncertainty, and acknowledging that is more useful than pretending the funding picture is stable.
