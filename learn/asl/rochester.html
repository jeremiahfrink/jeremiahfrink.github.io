What this page is

This page holds your research notes for the Deaf Rochester project. Everything you write here will become the raw material for the Google Site you are going to build: a virtual tour of Deaf Rochester for hearing people.

The page has three parts, and each one may take more than one day. Take as long as you need to do them well.

  • Part 1 is about Rochester's Deaf community today. Dad has chosen the sources.
  • Part 2 is about how it all started. Dad has chosen these sources too, but you get even less help deciding what to write down.
  • Part 3 is your own research. You find the sources and keep your notes in your Deaf Culture Notebook. This page gives you a plan for how to do that.

The Tour Stops and Questions tab is open the whole time. Add to it whenever something comes up in Parts 1 and 2.

Your work saves automatically in this browser as you type. When you are ready to turn something in, go to the Turn In tab and copy your notes into a Google Doc.

Why this page gives you less help than the PBS page did

On the Through Deaf Eyes page, every question was written for you. You answered them, and that helped you understand the film. But when you start finding your own sources, nobody will be there to write the questions. You will have to decide for yourself what is worth writing down.

So this page is a step toward doing that on your own. For each source, you get a few key ideas to watch for and one big open box for your notes. You decide what goes in the box. In Part 2 you get even fewer key ideas. By Part 3, you will be setting up your own notes, with suggestions from Dad on how to do it well.

How to take good notes

  1. Read the key ideas before you start. They tell you what to pay attention to, so you are not trying to catch everything.
  2. Write in your own words. Read or watch a section, then look away from it and write down what you remember. If you cannot explain it without looking, you do not understand it yet, so go back and read it again.
  3. Do not copy and paste whole sections. Copying feels like work, but your brain skips the part where it makes sense of the information. You will not remember it, and you will not be able to use it when you build your site.
  4. Copy only when you want an exact quote. If someone says something so well that you want to use their exact words on your site, copy it, put it in quotation marks, and write who said it.
  5. Short is fine. Notes do not have to be full sentences. Phrases, lists, names, and dates are all good notes as long as you will understand them next week.
  6. Mark things as you go. Use these marks so you can find things later:
    • ? next to something that confused you or that you want to find out more about
    • STOP next to a place that could be a tour stop
    • " " around an exact quote, with the person's name

Part 1: Rochester's Deaf Community Now

In this part you are finding out what Deaf life in Rochester looks like today, and why so many Deaf people choose to live here. Read the key ideas for each source before you start, then take your notes in the big box.

Source 1: Why Deaf people come to Rochester

WXXI News, January 2022 · Resources, culture make Rochester a draw for many in deaf community

Key ideas to watch for

  • The reasons Deaf people give for moving to Rochester and staying
  • Specific places and services in the city that are set up for Deaf people
  • The people quoted in the article and what each of them says
  • Any numbers, and where those numbers come from

Source 2: The NTID population study

Hearing Review, October 2012 · Study Confirms That Rochester, NY Has Highest Per Capita Deaf Population

Key ideas to watch for

  • Who did the study and what information they used
  • What "per capita" means, and why it matters for comparing cities
  • What the study says is the main reason Rochester's number is so high
  • What the study counts, and what it does not count

Source 3: A local conversation about Deaf culture

WXXI Connections, April 2017, about 51 minutes · Connections: Deaf Culture in Rochester

Turn on captions. You can pause as often as you want. A good rhythm is to watch about five minutes, pause, and write what you remember before going on.

Key ideas to watch for

  • Who the guests are, what they do, and which of them are Deaf
  • How the show handles communication between the Deaf guests, the interpreters, and the host
  • What the guests say hearing people get wrong about Deaf people
  • Anything about Rochester specifically: jobs, history, hospitals, schools, or how the city has changed
  • One thing a guest says that every hearing person in Rochester should hear

Part 3: Your Own Research

Now you are the researcher. You will find your own sources, check them, and take your own notes. Your notes for this part go in your Deaf Culture Notebook, in a section called Deaf Rochester Research, not on this page. This page gives you a plan for setting up those notes and a place to keep track of how it is going.

Aim for at least four sources you found yourself. This part may take more than one day.

Step 1: Set up your notes before you search

Good researchers decide how they will organize their notes before they start, so that every source gets the same treatment and nothing important gets lost. Here is a structure that works well. Use it, or change it based on what you learned about yourself in Parts 1 and 2.

Make one section in your notebook for each source, and give each section these parts:

  1. Source: the title, the link, who made it, and when.
  2. Check: your answers to the five source checks in Step 3 below. Keep them short.
  3. Which question this helps answer: the starred question (or questions) this source connects to.
  4. Notes: what you learned, in your own words. Use ?, STOP, and quotation marks the same way you did in Parts 1 and 2.
  5. Quotes: only exact words you might use on your site, with who said them.
  6. New questions: anything this source made you wonder about.

Step 2: Decide what is worth writing down

You will find a lot more information than you can use. Before you write something down, ask yourself whether it helps you answer one of your starred questions, or whether it would help a hearing visitor on your tour.

Usually worth writing downUsually not worth writing down
  • Names of people and places, with what they did or why they matter
  • Dates for your timeline
  • Reasons and explanations (the "why")
  • What Deaf people say about their own experience
  • Facts that surprised you, because they will probably surprise your visitors too
  • Anything that disagrees with another source
  • Things you already wrote down from another source (just note that this source agrees)
  • Long descriptions you could find again with the link
  • Details that have nothing to do with Rochester or your questions
  • Whole paragraphs copied from the page

Step 3: Search and check

Start with your starred questions. Turn each one into a few search words. If you get stuck, here are some searches to try:

  • Rochester Recreation Club for the Deaf
  • Deaf Wellness Center Rochester
  • Robert Panara NTID
  • Dyer Arts Center NTID
  • Rochester School for the Deaf history
  • Rochester Deaf community Kodak Xerox jobs
  • Rochester History journal Deaf community
  • Deaf owned businesses Rochester NY

Look for sources made by Deaf people or Deaf organizations. A tour of Deaf Rochester should include Deaf voices, not only hearing people talking about Deaf people. NTID has a YouTube channel, and WXXI has more local stories on WXXI Video.

The five source checks

Run every source through these checks before you trust it.

  1. Who made it? A news organization, a school, a museum, a Deaf organization, a business, or a random person? If you cannot tell, be careful.
  2. How do they know? Do they show where their information came from, like a study, a record, an interview, or someone who was there?
  3. What do other sources say? Open a new tab and search for the same fact somewhere else. If two or three trustworthy sources agree, you can use it. If you only find it in one place, say so or leave it out. Professional fact checkers do exactly this: they leave the page and check what others say.
  4. When was it made? History stays true, but hours, addresses, and businesses change.
  5. Whose voice is it? Is a Deaf person or Deaf organization speaking, or a hearing person speaking about Deaf people?
Be careful with AI summaries at the top of search results. They can mix up facts or make them up. Use them to get ideas for what to search next, then go read the real source.
