The Sensory Diet

Health · a two week mini project · everything you type here saves on this device

What this is

This is a short Health project about how you keep yourself level through a day. Not level as in calm all the time. Level as in able to focus when you need to focus, able to wind down when you need to wind down, and not stuck at either end.

You have probably heard the word diet used to mean eating less. That is not what it means here. A diet is simply what you take in and when. A sensory diet is the same idea, applied to everything your body takes in that is not food: movement, sound, light, pressure, screens, quiet.

What you are making

By the end of this you will have written your own sensory diet plan, tried it for one school week, and written down honestly what happened. That is the whole assignment. You are not being graded on whether the plan worked. You are being graded on whether you built it thoughtfully, ran it, and told the truth about it.

One thing to know before you start. Most of what is written about sensory diets is written for people with autism or ADHD, and most of it is written for young kids or their parents. You have ADHD, so some of it will fit you. Some of it will not. Nothing on this page asks you to accept any of it on faith. Your job is to read like a person deciding what applies to them, not like a patient being told what to do.

1 · The idea

Think about how you eat during a day. You do not eat everything at once. You spread it out, and the timing is not random. Breakfast gets you started, lunch keeps you going through the afternoon, dinner refuels you after the day. Each meal has a job, and the job depends on the time.

Sometimes the timing matters even when your body is not sending the signal. For example, if a person takes a medication that turns down their appetite during the day, they can go until dinner without ever feeling hungry. Their body still needed the fuel at lunch. The signal was quiet, but the need was not. So that person eats lunch anyway, because they know the need is there, and then makes sure dinner is a real meal, because that is when the hunger comes back and the body can catch up.

The same thing is true of what your nervous system takes in. Movement, pressure, sound, quiet, light, and screens all change how awake, settled, or wound up you feel. Occupational therapists call this your level of alertness, and some of them describe it like a car engine: running low, running just right, or running high. The useful part of that idea is that you can change your engine speed on purpose, and the best time to do it is usually before you notice you need to.

Why this matters for you specifically

Your school day involves a lot of sitting and a lot of screens. Then, when school is done, the thing you enjoy most also happens sitting down, at a screen. There is nothing wrong with any of that on its own. But it means your day can run for hours without the kinds of input that move your engine speed, and then everything piles up at once: restless in the afternoon, wired at night, flat in the morning.

Research on teenagers backs this up. One study that put wearable sensors on nearly 400 high school students found they sat for about three quarters of class time and about three quarters of the evening, and that breaking up long sitting was linked to better health and better school outcomes. Other researchers have written guidelines saying school days should include scheduled movement breaks and that screen-based schoolwork should stop an hour before bed. Those are the kinds of claims you will check for yourself in the next tab.

The questions this project is really asking

What does your body need to take in during a day, besides food, to run well?

When does it need it? Not all at once. Spread through the day, and timed to the parts of the day that are hardest.

2 · Read and watch

Six sources. Two are videos, the rest are short reads. For each one, write what made sense for you and what did not. "This is written for six year olds" is a fair thing to notice. So is "this part is actually me."

A note on evidence: occupational therapists use sensory diets all the time, and plenty of people say they help, but the research behind them is thin and the studies are small. That is not a reason to ignore the idea. It is a reason to test it on yourself instead of taking anyone's word for it.

Read firstUnderstood.org: Sensory diet treatment, what you need to know

The plainest explanation. Written for parents of kids, so translate as you go. It has a sample sensory diet you can look at to see the shape of one.

Watch for the part about sensory seeking versus sensory avoiding, and notice that she says nobody is only one or the other.

ReadThe Alert Program engine analogy (2 page PDF)

This is the "engine running low, just right, or high" idea, and the five categories of things that change your engine speed: mouth, move, touch, look, listen. It is the simplest tool in this whole project and probably the most useful one.

4 · Build the plan

A sensory diet has the same parts as a food one: a few planned "meals" at set times, and some "snacks" you can grab when you need them. Keep it small. Five or six items you will actually do beats fifteen you will abandon by Tuesday.

Rules for a good item: it names a time, it names an action, it says how long, and it says what it is for (wake me up, settle me down, break up sitting, get me off a screen). "Move more" is not an item. "Walk to the end of the street and back before the afternoon block, 8 minutes, to reset after lunch" is an item.

5 · Run it

Five school days. Each day, check off what you did, mark your engine three times, and write two honest lines. If you skip a meal, say so. A skipped item you admit to is worth more than a checked box that is not true.

6 · Reflect

This is the part that gets graded, and it is graded on honesty and thinking, not on results. A plan that failed and taught you something is a success here.
