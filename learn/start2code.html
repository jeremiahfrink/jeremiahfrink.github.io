SETUP  the brief

What am I actually doing?

You are going to pick one small piece of a game idea, build only that piece, put it in front of somebody, and publish it. Inside a week.

Not a demo of your big idea. Not the first level of it. One piece, finished.

WHY YOU ARE DOING THIS

Almost everyone who tries to make games quits in the same place. Not at the start, where it is exciting, and not at the end, where it is nearly done. They quit about a third of the way in, on something enormous they began in a burst of enthusiasm and cannot now finish.

The habit that prevents this is not talent and it is not discipline. It is the ability to look at a big idea and correctly identify the smallest version of it that is still worth playing. That is a skill, it is learnable, and it is the single most valuable thing a fourteen-year-old who wants to build games can practise.

So this week you practise it once, on purpose, with a hard deadline. Everything else here exists to support that.

DEV DICTIONARY: scope

Scope means everything a project is going to include. When a team says "that is out of scope," they mean it is a fine idea that is not in this version.

Scope creep is when the list quietly grows while you are working: one more enemy type, one more level, just a title screen. Nobody decides to do it. It happens one reasonable-sounding addition at a time, and it is the leading cause of death for games that never come out.

Professionals fight scope creep with written rules agreed before the work starts. That is exactly what two screens from now is.

SETUP  the machine

Why we are going retro

You are building on a pretend computer from 1987 that never existed.

PICO-8 is a fantasy console: an imaginary 8-bit machine with deliberately brutal limits. A screen 128 pixels square. Sixteen colors, and you do not get to pick them. A hard ceiling on how much code fits on a cartridge.

It is not nostalgia and it is not because you are a beginner. Those limits are doing a job.

WHY YOU ARE DOING THIS

Give someone unlimited space and they build forever and finish nothing. Give them a cartridge that physically cannot hold their sprawling idea and something useful happens: they are forced to decide what the game actually is.

This is a real technique with a real name and studios use it on purpose. Some of the best games ever made were shaped by hardware that could not do what the designers originally wanted, and the workaround became the thing everybody remembers.

There is a sneakier benefit too. On a machine this size you can hold the entire thing in your head. Every pixel, every color, every function. That is not true of Unity or Unreal and it will never be true again. Learn what a whole computer feels like while a whole computer still fits in your brain.

DEV DICTIONARY: constraint-driven design

Constraint-driven design is deliberately limiting your own options to force better decisions. Game jams do it with time. PICO-8 does it with hardware. Poets do it with sonnets.

It feels backwards until the first time you experience it: a blank page with infinite options is paralysing, and a page with three options is a puzzle you can actually solve.

DEV DICTIONARY: MVP and vertical slice

A minimum viable product (MVP) is the smallest thing you can build that still works and still proves the idea. Not a sketch, not a mockup: a real thing, just small.

A vertical slice is closer to what you are building. Instead of building all of level one badly, you build one narrow slice of the game all the way through to a finished state, so you can find out whether the thing is any fun before committing months to it.

Studios build vertical slices to show publishers. You are building one to show yourself.

SETUP  the translation

You already know how to do this

PICO-8 is Scratch with typing. Same ideas wearing different clothes.

  when green flag clicked  ->  function _init()
  forever                  ->  function _update()   (runs 30x a second, by itself)
  if key pressed           ->  if btn(0) then       (0 1 2 3 = left right up down)
  change x by 2            ->  x = x + 2
  set score to 0           ->  score = 0
  stamp the sprite         ->  rectfill() or spr(), inside function _draw()
  if touching              ->  you write this one yourself. this is the hard part.

The only genuinely new idea is that thinking and drawing are separate jobs. _update() works out what happened. _draw() puts it on the screen. That is the whole language barrier, and you just crossed it.

DEV DICTIONARY: the game loop, frames, 30fps

A game is not a program that runs once. It is a program that runs the same few instructions over and over, thirty times every second, forever, until you quit.

Each pass is a frame. Thirty frames a second means every frame gets about 33 milliseconds to do its work. Miss that budget and the game stutters, which players feel instantly even when they cannot say why.

The game loop is that repeating cycle: read input, update the world, draw the world, repeat. PICO-8 hands it to you already built, as _update and _draw. Every engine you ever touch, all the way up to the ones running games on your console, is doing the same thing underneath.

DEV DICTIONARY: core loop

The core loop is the short cycle of actions a player repeats constantly: aim, shoot, reload. Mine, craft, build. Dash, strike, dodge.

Designers obsess over it, because if the core loop is boring for ten seconds it will be boring for ten hours, and no amount of story or art rescues it. "One verb" is the core loop stripped to its smallest possible version.

DEV DICTIONARY: the pitch and the design pillar

A pitch is how you explain a game to someone who decides whether it exists: a publisher, a studio head, a room of strangers at a games conference. Short, spoken, no slides needed.

A design pillar is a short statement a team agrees on and then uses to settle arguments. When somebody wants to add a feature, the pillar answers it: does this serve the pillar or not? Your one sentence is a design pillar with a team of one.

DEV DICTIONARY: acceptance criteria and definition of done

On real software teams, a task is not finished when the person who built it feels finished. It is finished when it meets its acceptance criteria: a short written list, agreed before anybody started, of what has to be true.

Teams also keep a shared definition of done, which usually includes boring things like "somebody else tested it" and "it is actually deployed, not sitting on my laptop."

Both exist for the same reason yours is sealed. Human beings are extremely good at deciding they are finished at exactly the moment they get tired.

DAY 1  plan

Draw the screen on paper

One box for the screen. What is on it, what moves, what ends the game. Two minutes with a pencil.

WHY YOU ARE DOING THIS

Changing a drawing costs four seconds. Changing code costs an hour, and it costs it after you have already become attached to what you built.

The rule that follows is one professionals live by: make your mistakes in the cheapest material available. Paper is the cheapest material there is. Board game designers prototype with index cards. Level designers block out whole levels in grey boxes before an artist touches anything. It is not being precious. It is being efficient about where you fail.

Also worth five minutes before you commit: how your verb should feel. There is more than one way to make a thing move, and each one changes the game completely.

DEV DICTIONARY: paper prototyping and greyboxing

Paper prototyping is testing an idea with drawings and cut-out pieces before writing code. You can find out a mechanic is boring in ten minutes instead of ten days.

Greyboxing (or blocking out) is building a level entirely from plain grey shapes with no art at all, purely to test whether the space plays well. Big-budget studios do this for months. The pretty version comes last, on purpose, because pretty things are painful to throw away.

Your colored rectangles this week are greyboxing. You are in good company.

DAY 1  build

Type the starter cartridge

A blue square moves. It is not a game and it is not supposed to be. It is the floor you build on.

function _init()
 p={x=60,y=60,w=6,h=6,spd=2}
end

function _update()
 if btn(0) then p.x-=p.spd end
 if btn(1) then p.x+=p.spd end
 if btn(2) then p.y-=p.spd end
 if btn(3) then p.y+=p.spd end
 p.x=mid(0,p.x,127-p.w)
 p.y=mid(0,p.y,127-p.h)
end

function _draw()
 cls(1)
 rectfill(p.x,p.y,p.x+p.w,p.y+p.h,12)
end
WHY YOU ARE TYPING IT AND NOT PASTING IT

There is no copy button on this page and that is deliberate.

Pasted code runs and teaches you nothing. Typed code makes you notice the parentheses, the end that closes the function, the difference between p.x and x. You will hit an error while typing it, and fixing that error is worth more than the whole rest of this screen.

By tomorrow you need to change this code confidently, and you cannot confidently change something you have never actually looked at.

Then break it on purpose before you build on it. Change spd to 8. Change it to 0.5. Change the 12 to any number from 0 to 15. Delete an end and read what it yells at you.

  • The game loop, as a playable cart why _init, _update and _draw are separate
  • Buttons and movement playable, heavily commented

Boilerplate is the setup code that is nearly identical at the start of every project. Nobody finds it interesting and everybody needs it.

Experienced developers keep a personal starter template and reuse it for years. Yours starts today: this is the first version of a file you may still be modifying in Grade 12.

DEV DICTIONARY: iteration, and why the bug log matters

Iteration means going round again: build, look at it, change it, look again. Games are not designed and then made. They are made badly and then improved, over and over, and how fast you can go round that circle largely determines how good the result is.

The bug log is not busywork and it is not for anyone else. Six months from now you will hit the same bug again and past-you will have left a note. Working developers keep exactly this, usually in commit messages and issue trackers, for exactly this reason.

DEV DICTIONARY: hitbox, AABB, collision detection

Collision detection is working out when two things in a game are touching. It sounds simple and it is an entire field of study.

A hitbox is the invisible shape used for that check, and it is usually not the same shape as the art. The player might be a detailed character while the hitbox is a plain rectangle slightly smaller than they look, because that feels fairer to players. Fighting games are obsessive about this.

AABB stands for axis-aligned bounding box: a rectangle that never rotates, with sides parallel to the screen edges. It is the simplest and fastest kind of collision check, it is what you are about to write, and it is still doing heavy lifting in enormous commercial games today.

DAY 2  stakes

Add a way to lose

Exactly one thing that can end the run. One. Not a health system, not enemies plural, not a boss.

WHY YOU ARE DOING THIS

A verb with no failure is a toy. Toys are fine and people put them down after ninety seconds.

The moment something can go wrong, every press of the button starts to matter, and the same mechanic you had five minutes ago becomes tense. You are not adding content here. You are adding consequence, and consequence is most of what separates a game from a screensaver.

  • Game states how to have a game-over without building a menu. take the game-over idea, ignore the menu parts, those are banned
DEV DICTIONARY: fail state and game state

A fail state is any condition that ends the attempt: dying, running out of time, letting the last ball past. A win state is the opposite. Some games deliberately have neither, and designers argue about whether those count as games, which is a fun argument to have some other week.

Game state is the bigger idea: which mode the game is currently in. Playing, paused, game over. Almost every game tracks this with a single variable, and almost every beginner discovers the need for it at the exact moment you are discovering it now.

DEV DICTIONARY: juice, game feel, timeboxing

Juice is the industry's actual word for the small responsive touches that make a game feel good: the screen shake on impact, the tiny pause when you land a hit, the particles, the sound. None of it changes the rules. All of it changes how the rules feel.

Game feel is the broader study of that, and it is taken extremely seriously. Studios spend weeks on how a jump feels, tuning numbers a player will never consciously notice.

Timeboxing means deciding in advance how long a task gets and stopping when the time is up, whether or not it is perfect. It is the standard defence against exactly the trap this screen is walking you past.

    DAY 3  diagnose

    Stop staring at it. Make it tell you.

    Your symptom: the blue square vanishes and does not come back. Do not read the code looking for the mistake. You will read what you meant to write, because you are the person who wrote it.

    WHAT PROFESSIONALS ACTUALLY DO HERE

    Nobody good debugs by reading harder. They make the program report on itself, and then they follow the report.

    The reason is the same one you met on Day 3 of the game: you cannot see your own work clearly. You know what every line is supposed to do, so your eyes slide over the line that does something else. A number printed on screen has no opinion about what you intended.

    This protocol is not a beginner's version of debugging. It is debugging. The tooling gets fancier and the idea never changes.

    Step 0. Save a copy first.

    Before you change anything, save verb3.p8. You are about to break things on purpose and you want the current state preserved.

    Step 1. Reproduce it on demand.

    Can you make the square vanish every single time, doing the same thing? If it only happens sometimes, work out what you are doing differently when it does. A bug you cannot reproduce is a bug you cannot fix, and finding the reliable steps is often most of the work.

    Step 2. Make the program talk.

    Put this line inside _draw() and run it:

    print(p.x.." "..p.y,4,4,7)

    Now play until the square disappears and watch those two numbers. There are exactly three things that can happen, and each one points somewhere completely different.

    🔴The numbers appear, but they are way outside 0 to 127

    The square walked off the screen and nothing pulled it back. This is the most literal version of "I could not get it back," and it is the easiest to fix.

    Look for these two lines in _update():

    p.x=mid(0,p.x,127-p.w)
p.y=mid(0,p.y,127-p.h)

    They are probably gone, or they moved somewhere that no longer runs, or the enemy code got written on top of them. mid() takes three numbers and returns the middle one, which is a compact way of saying "never below 0, never above 127."

    🔵The numbers look normal, around 60 and 60, but there is no square

    It is being drawn and you cannot see it. Two usual causes.

    Colour. Your background is cls(1), which is dark blue, and your player is colour 12, which is light blue. One stray keystroke turning that 12 into a 1 makes the player perfectly invisible against the background. Change the player to 8 for a moment. If a red square appears where the blue one should be, that was it.

    Draw order. Everything in _draw() happens in the order you wrote it, and later things paint over earlier things. If cls() ended up after your player's rectfill, you are wiping the player every frame. If the enemy drawing loop is after it and something is drawn full-screen, same result.

    🟡That line throws an error

    p does not exist any more, and something ate it. The usual culprit in a dodge game is this:

    for p in all(reds) do

    Using p as the loop variable overwrites your player with an enemy, and from that moment on the player is gone. Nothing looks wrong. There is no error at the line that caused it.

    Fix: name loop variables something else. for r in all(reds) do. Then check every other place you reused a name.

    This has a name, variable shadowing, and it catches everybody at least once. Now it has caught you and it will not get you again.

    Step 3. If none of the three fit, cut the problem in half.

    Comment out the enemy code entirely with --. Does the player behave? Then the bug is in the enemies. Put half of it back. Repeat. Four or five rounds of halving corners almost any bug, and it beats staring at all of it hoping to notice.

    what the bug actually turned out to be, and what fixed it
    DEV DICTIONARY: reproduce, print debugging, bisecting, shadowing

    Reproducing a bug means finding the reliable steps that trigger it. Every professional bug report starts with those steps, because a bug nobody can trigger on demand cannot be confirmed fixed.

    Print debugging is exactly what you just did. Unglamorous, universal, still used daily by people with twenty years of experience.

    Bisecting is halving the search space repeatedly. There are tools that automate this across years of a project's history, and they are doing what you just did by hand.

    Shadowing is when a name gets reused and quietly hides the original. It is the reason experienced programmers are fussy about naming things, which looks like pedantry until the first time it costs you a day.

    DAY 3  the missing piece

    The part nobody told you about: a list

    This is the piece your game needed and the site never taught. It is not your fault it was hard. It was missing.

    WHY THIS IS ITS OWN CONCEPT

    Handling one thing is easy: give it a name, move it, draw it. Handling an unknown number of things that appear and disappear while the game runs is a different job, and it needs a structure that can grow and shrink.

    In Lua that structure is a table. You will use tables in every game you ever write, for enemies, bullets, particles, pickups, and levels. Learning it today is not a detour from your dodge game. It is the single most reusable thing in this whole week.

    The four commands

    reds={}            -- an empty list
add(reds,thing)    -- put something in it
del(reds,thing)    -- take something out
for r in all(reds) -- do something to each one
#reds              -- how many are in it right now

    The pattern, working

    Red squares come in from the edges, cross the screen, and get removed when they leave. No player, no collision, no timer. Just the piece you were missing.

    function _init()
 reds={}
 t=0
end

function _update()
 t+=1

 -- every 30 frames, roughly once a second
 if t%30==0 then
  spawn()
 end

 -- move every red in the list
 for r in all(reds) do
  r.x+=r.dx
  r.y+=r.dy

  -- left the screen? take it out.
  -- without this the list grows forever
  -- and the game slowly chokes.
  if r.x<-8 or r.x>136
  or r.y<-8 or r.y>136 then
   del(reds,r)
  end
 end
end

function _draw()
 cls(1)
 for r in all(reds) do
  rectfill(r.x,r.y,r.x+r.w,r.y+r.h,8)
 end
 print("reds: "..#reds,4,4,7)
end

function spawn()
 local side=flr(rnd(4))
 local r={w=5,h=5,dx=0,dy=0}

 if side==0 then
  r.x=-8 r.y=rnd(128) r.dx=1+rnd(1)
 elseif side==1 then
  r.x=136 r.y=rnd(128) r.dx=-1-rnd(1)
 elseif side==2 then
  r.x=rnd(128) r.y=-8 r.dy=1+rnd(1)
 else
  r.x=rnd(128) r.y=136 r.dy=-1-rnd(1)
 end

 add(reds,r)
end

    DEV DICTIONARY: tables, object pools, and why del matters

    A table in Lua is a list and a container of named values at the same time. Every red square here is a small table with x, y, w, h, dx and dy inside it, and they all live in one big table called reds.

    Forgetting to del() things that leave the screen is one of the classic beginner bugs and it is a sneaky one, because nothing breaks. The list just grows, the game gets slower and slower, and you blame your computer. Professionals call the general problem a leak.

    Big games avoid creating and destroying objects constantly with a trick called an object pool: make a hundred at the start, reuse them, never allocate again. You do not need it at this size. It is worth knowing the name.

    DEV DICTIONARY: playtesting, and the curse of knowledge

    Playtesting is watching real people play, and it is a discipline with rules. The first rule everywhere is that the developer does not help. Some studios put the developer in a different room entirely, because the temptation is that strong.

    The curse of knowledge is the reason. Once you know something, you cannot imagine not knowing it, so you genuinely cannot see what is confusing about your own work. This is not a flaw you can train away. It is why every creative field has some version of showing the work to a stranger and shutting up.

    DEV DICTIONARY: triage, severity, known issues

    Triage comes from emergency medicine and means sorting problems by urgency when you cannot handle all of them. Before every release, teams go through the bug list deciding what gets fixed and what ships broken.

    Severity is how bad a bug is: does it crash the game, or is some text slightly the wrong colour? Everything gets ranked.

    Known issues is the published list of bugs a team decided to ship with anyway. Every game you have ever played launched with one. Shipping something imperfect on purpose is normal and expected, and it is completely different from shipping something imperfect by accident.

    DEV DICTIONARY: post-mortem and retrospective

      Stuck?

      Good. That means you are working on something real.

      Stuck is not a sign you are doing badly at this. It is what the job is, most days, for everybody who does it. The people you think of as good at programming are not people who do not get stuck. They are people who have got unstuck a few thousand more times than you have.

      The only actual mistake is sitting in the same stuck place for forty minutes without changing your approach.

      ⚡ DO THESE THREE FIRST, EVERY SINGLE TIME
      1. Find the line number. PICO-8 tells you which line it choked on. Go and look at that exact line.
      2. Undo the last thing you added. If it worked five minutes ago, the problem is in those five minutes. Comment lines out with -- and add them back one at a time.
      3. Print the number. Put print(p.x,4,4,7) in your _draw() and watch it while you play. You probably do not actually know what the value is.

      That protocol alone solves most of what will happen to you this week. Run it before you do anything else on this page.

      Still stuck? Pick the one that sounds like you.

      Tap one to open it. They are different problems and they need different moves.

      🔴It will not run at all. There is an error message.

      Good news: this is the easiest kind of stuck, and the computer is actively telling you where the problem is. It is not being rude. It is being specific.

      The move: read the line number, go to that line, and look at the line above it too. Errors are often reported one line after the actual mistake, because that is where the computer finally realised something was wrong.

      What the common error messages actually mean

      syntax error
      Something is typed wrong. Usually a missing then after an if, a missing end, or an unclosed bracket. Count your brackets.

      'end' expected
      You opened a function, if, or for and never closed it. Every one of those needs its own end.

      attempt to index a nil value
      You asked for something inside a thing that does not exist. If you wrote p.x and there is no p yet, this is what you get. Check the spelling, and check that _init() actually created it.

      attempt to perform arithmetic on a nil value
      You did math on an empty variable. Almost always a typo: you set speed somewhere and used spd here.

      attempt to call a nil value
      You called a function that does not exist. Check the spelling of the function name against where you defined it.

      Notice the pattern. Three of those five are spelling. Variable names are case-sensitive and the computer will not guess what you meant.

      Still not obvious? Paste the error and the line into the Coach and say what you expected that line to do.

      🔵It runs, but it does the wrong thing.

      Harder than an error, because nothing is going to tell you where to look. You have to go find it.

      The move: stop guessing and start watching. Put print() statements in and see what the numbers are actually doing. Is the value what you think it is? Is that line even running? Print the word "here" inside the if and find out whether you ever get in there at all.

      Then narrow it. Comment out half your code with --. Does the problem still happen? Now you know which half it is in. Do it again. Four or five rounds of that will corner almost any bug, and it beats staring at all of it hoping to notice.

      DEV DICTIONARY: bisecting and the minimal repro

      Bisecting is what you just did: cut the search space in half, test, repeat. Programmers use it constantly, and there are tools that automate it across years of a project's history.

      A minimal reproduction (a "minimal repro") is the smallest piece of code that still shows the bug. Stripping a problem down to a repro fixes it by itself surprisingly often, because the act of removing everything irrelevant is the act of finding out what is relevant.

      Nobody writes games from memory. The person sitting next to you at a studio has the docs open, a Stack Overflow tab, and somebody else's code they are reading for ideas.

      The move: explain it out loud, one line at a time, to something that cannot help you. A wall. A pet. Your own reflection. Say what each line does before you move to the next one.

      DEV DICTIONARY: rubber duck debugging

      This has a name and the name is rubber duck debugging. Programmers keep actual rubber ducks on their desks and explain their code to them, unironically, in professional offices, in front of colleagues, who do not find it odd because they have their own duck.

      The Coach works as a very expensive duck. Explaining your code to it, line by line, is one of the better uses of it, and better than asking it what is wrong.

      🟣It works, but it feels bad to play.

      Then you do not have a bug, you have a design problem, and that is a completely different kind of work. It is also the more interesting kind.

      The move: change one number at a time and play it after each change. Speed. Gravity. How long a thing takes. How far you move per frame. Feel is almost never one big decision, it is fifteen small numbers, and the only way to find them is to keep touching one and playing.

      If it feels floaty, things are probably too slow to start and too slow to stop. If it feels stiff, the opposite. If it feels unfair, your hitbox is probably bigger than it looks.

      Read this part properly, because it is the most likely one to be true and the one people skip.

      That feeling is not information about whether you can do this. It is information about how long you have been sitting still while something did not work, which is a completely different fact. Everybody who builds things feels it. Most of them felt it this week.

      2. Come back and read your one verb at the top of the page. Remind yourself what you are actually trying to build, because after ninety minutes of one bug you have usually forgotten.

      Twenty minutes of stuck with no movement at all is your signal to change something. Not to try harder.

      How to ask a question that actually gets answered

      Worth learning now, because it works on the Coach, on Dad, and on every forum and colleague for the rest of your life. "It does not work" gets you nothing, from anybody, ever.

      THE FOUR PARTS

      1. What I expected. "I expected the square to stop at the wall."

      2. What actually happened. "It goes straight through and disappears."

      3. What I already tried. "I printed p.x and it keeps going past 127."

      4. The smallest piece of code involved. Not the whole cart. The four lines you think are guilty.

      Writing those four things out will solve the problem before you send it maybe a third of the time. That is not a waste of the effort. That is the technique working.
