What you are doing

A studio has hired you to design a set of games, not just one. They share a world, or a feeling, or a set of ideas, and a player should be able to tell they belong together.

Before anybody builds anything, somebody has to write the series bible. That is a real thing studios make. It says what these stories are about, what they always do, and what they never do. When two people on the team disagree about whether something fits, they open the bible and it settles the argument. It is a quiet little document that ends up deciding what actually gets made.

A series bible is built on pillars. A pillar is one big idea the whole series stands on. You are picking two.

For each pillar you write a rule. A rule says what a story has to do to actually earn that pillar, instead of just gesturing at it.

Here is the part that is harder than it sounds. You write your rules first, before you look at any stories, as a guess. Then you find stories and hold them up against your rules. Sometimes the story wins and the rule has to change.

That is not you failing. That is the job.

Everything you type saves by itself. You can close this and come back.

PART 0

Start with The Taming of the Drew

You read The Taming of the Drew, a novel by Stephanie Kate Strohm. It is a retelling of The Taming of the Shrew, a play William Shakespeare wrote about four hundred years ago. You saw that play on stage, so you know both versions of this story.

You did this move once already with Hadestown, the musical you saw. When somebody retells an old story, every change they make is an argument about what the story is really about.

This time you are going faster and staying smaller. You are not writing the whole argument. You are taking stock of what made it through.

Part of Shakespeare's play
Did it make it into Strohm's book?
If it changed or disappeared, what did the story lose?

That last column is the whole point. "It's gone" is not an answer. The answer is what the story could do with that piece that it can't do without it.

1. One thing Strohm cut was the hardest part of Shakespeare's play to put in a book for teenagers. What was it? What happened to the story when she took it out? Is her book better or worse for it?

2. One thing she changed is not a cut at all. Something got flipped around instead of removed. Find it. What can she say with it flipped that Shakespeare couldn't say?

3. Strohm keeps Shakespeare's original right there inside her book, since her characters are putting on the actual play. What does she get out of doing that? What does it risk?

Now the question that opens up the rest of this project.

You just watched a story survive getting moved four hundred years forward, across an ocean, and into a completely different form. Some parts held up. Some died on the way.

What else have you read or played that has pieces in it you have seen somewhere else? Not the same plot. The same moves. Write down everything that comes to mind, even if the connection seems small or dumb. Don't filter yet.

This is not your evidence yet. It is a pile of ideas to go digging through later.

PART 2

Write your rules first

Before you go anywhere near a book, write a rule for each of your two pillars.

A rule has to be specific enough that you could hand it to a stranger and they could use it to judge a story you've never read. If everybody would just nod along, it isn't a rule. It's a nice thought.

Five soft rules

Here is a soft rule for each of the five pillars. Every one sounds reasonable. Every one is useless, because no story would ever fail it.

The same first one, fixed

The Return
A story has to show you what home was like before, so you notice the difference yourself instead of somebody telling you.

See what changed. The soft version describes the pillar. The fixed version says what the story has to do, and a story could absolutely fail it. Plenty of stories send a character home without ever showing you what home was before. Those stories now fail your rule, and that is the point. A rule that nothing fails is not sorting anything.

PART 3

Picking your shelf

Your shelf is the small set of stories you'll use to prove your pillars: three books and one game. These should be things you already know well. You are not reading four new things. You're going back through stuff you've already read and played, and looking at it the way a designer looks at it instead of the way a fan does.

When a story breaks your rule

This is going to happen and you need to know what to do about it.

You wrote a rule. Then you think of a story you know is good, you hold it up against the rule, and it doesn't fit. Maybe it does the pillar beautifully but in a way your rule never saw coming. Maybe it skips a step your rule insisted on. That story broke your rule. Your rule said "any story doing this has to do X," and here's a good story that doesn't do X.

When that happens you have two choices, and you have to pick one on purpose and say so:

The story knows something your rule didn't. Your rule was too narrow. Rewrite it so it covers what this story is doing.

Your rule is fine and this story just isn't using this pillar. Leave it off the shelf and keep looking.

The thing you are not allowed to do is quietly loosen a rule so a story you already liked can sneak in. If you're changing a rule, go back up to Part 2 and revise it there, and say which story broke it. The log writes itself from that.

One story has to be missing a pillar

This is different from breaking a rule, so don't mix them up. A story that breaks a rule has the pillar, it just handles it in a way your rule didn't expect. A story that is missing a pillar doesn't have it at all.

Somewhere on your shelf you need one of those. A story with no real homecoming. A world where nobody owes a stranger anything and nothing bad comes of it. A hero who only ever wins by hitting things.

Make this one the game. It's much easier to spot a missing pillar in a game than in a book, and what you learn from it is exactly the same.

That story isn't a mistake on the shelf. It might be your best evidence, because when a pillar is gone you can finally see what it was doing.

PART 4

Write the bible

This is the thing you turn in. Most of it is already written by the time you get here. You're assembling, not starting over.

The arc

Your two pillars aren't two separate things sitting next to each other. They're one series.

Argue this out loud to Dad before you write a word of it. Talk through how your two pillars fit together across a set of games. What's the shape? Where does a player start, and where do these two take them? Why does this pair make a series instead of just two ideas?

Now write it up short. The talking is where you figured it out. The writing is just catching what you said.
