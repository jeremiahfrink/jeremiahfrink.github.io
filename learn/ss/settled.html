Two words this module uses a lot

A forager is a person who gets food by hunting animals and gathering wild plants, and who moves when the food moves. The people in Layer 5 of your trench were foragers. A farmer plants crops, keeps animals, and stays in one place. The people in Layer 2 were farmers.

A character sheet in a game has stats: strength, speed, hit points. On this page the stats are things like Height, Teeth, and Children, and the numbers come from bones and seeds that real scientists measured. You do not roll them. You find them.

A finished stat looks like this

Forager · Food

What you found: The people of Abu Hureyra 1, the forager village in Layer 5, gathered more than a hundred kinds of wild plants from the river, the forest, and the open steppe, and killed gazelle by the hundreds each spring, drying the meat for the year.

Where it came from: The Dig, the Reveal page, the Layer 5 row. That is what Andrew Moore, Gordon Hillman, and Anthony Legge, the archaeologists who dug the site, concluded.

What it means for this character: A forager here ate from a very wide menu. If one plant failed in a dry year there were ninety nine others. This character does not starve because one crop fails, because there is no one crop.

The Farmer grows up

What to do now: first go back and finish any stat you left blank on Wednesday. Then read this screen, open the scientists' table (link on the Research page), and fill in the strip. Then write the two sentences.

In a game, a character levels up and the stats change. On this screen the whole village levels up. As it gets bigger and older, the stats of the people in it change too, and not all in the same direction.

The village is Çatalhöyük (say it chat-al-HOY-ook), a farming town in what is now Turkey. It lasted about 1,150 years, from around 7100 BCE to around 5950 BCE, and at its biggest held somewhere between 3,500 and 8,000 people. Clark Spencer Larsen, a scientist at Ohio State University who studies the bones people leave behind (the job is called bioarchaeology), led a team that spent 25 years studying 742 skeletons from the town. They split the town's life into three periods, Early, Middle, and Late, and made a table showing how the people changed from one period to the next.

Think of the three periods as Level 1, Level 2, and Level 3 of the same character. Find these five rows in their table and copy the words they use (they write things like Low, Increase, Decline, Substantial).

The map

Farming was not invented once and then copied. It was invented separately, by people who never met each other, in at least seven different parts of the world, each with its own crops. Wheat and barley in Southwest Asia, where your trench is. Rice and millet in China. Maize in Mexico. Potatoes in the Andes. Taro and bananas in New Guinea. Sorghum in Africa. Squash and sunflowers in eastern North America. Each of those places took the same trade your two character sheets describe, without knowing anyone else had.

The upgrade that traps you

What to do now: read the first part. Then open your World Engine doc and your Charter and do the ten minute reread. Then write the three slots and the last line, and date the entry.

The sword

In a lot of games there is an item like this. A sword that hits harder than anything else you can carry. The catch: while you hold it, you are poisoned. Your resistance does not help; the poison is part of the sword. And it is a binding item. Once you pick it up, you cannot put it down.

So the question is never "is it a good sword." The hit is real. The question is what it costs you every turn, and whether you can ever let go.

Farming is that sword

Look at your two sheets. The hit: more food from one place, more children, more people, more hands, and a storehouse. The poison: shorter, more cavities, worse knees and backs, more sickness, and more fighting as the village fills up. The binding: once there are three times as many people (that was the number from The Drop), there is no hunting ground on any map that could feed them all. The village cannot go back to foraging. It cannot unequip.

That is why this unit calls the farming change a trade and not an upgrade. An upgrade is better with no catch. A trade costs something, and this one locked.

A worked example, so you can see the shape

This is not your world. It is a made up one.

A town on a river that eats fish from a weir. A weir is a wooden fence built across the water that traps fish as they swim through.

The hit: fish every day without hunting for it. The town never goes hungry in summer, and it dries fish for winter. It grows to 2,000 people.

The poison: the weir has to be cleared and rebuilt every spring, in cold water, and the men who do it get sick and die younger. Two thousand people living packed along one bank share every illness. When the river floods, the town cannot move, because the weir is the town.

The binding: two thousand people cannot go back to hunting. There is no forest near enough with that much game. The day the town passed about 500 people, the weir stopped being a choice. Now it is the only thing keeping them alive, and they know it.

Notice that each part names something specific: a number, a group of people, a season. "It is good" or "it is bad" is not an answer. Say who, how many, and when.

What the Pattern Codex is

History has patterns: things that happen again and again, in different places and centuries, for the same reasons. The Pattern Codex is your own book of those patterns, written in your words, with the places you have seen them. You add to it all year, and next year too. By June it is the thing you will use to answer the course's big question: those who do not learn from history, are they truly doomed to repeat it?

Every pattern in the Codex has the same five parts:

  1. The pattern, named. A short name you would remember.
  2. How it works. The mechanism, in your own words: this happens, so that happens, so that happens.
  3. Cases. At least two times in history it happened, from two different eras. One is not enough; one time is a story, two times is a pattern.
  4. Sightings. Places you see it now, in the news or in your own life.
  5. World Engine note. How the pattern shows up, or should show up, in your own world.
