What this is and how to use it

What this is

This is the opening project for Global History Unit 1. You are going to play the lead archaeologist on a real excavation. The site is real, the finds are real, and the people who dug it were racing a dam. Its name is hidden until the end so you cannot look up the answers.

The unit's big question is What did we give up when we stopped moving? You will answer it with evidence you dig up yourself.

What you will make

Not an essay. You will make a museum exhibit about this place, on a wall or on a screen, and then give Dad a guided tour of it. The exhibit and the tour are the assessment for this unit.

How to move around

Use the buttons in the top bar to jump between parts. Each part also has a Next button at the bottom. The parts go in order, and the Reveal stays grey until you submit your claim.

The thin bar under the top row fills in as you work. The yellow number counts the finds you have logged.

The field clock in the top bar counts the days of your dig. It runs on work, not real time: every kit item, every find you log, every checkpoint, and every field event you say yes to moves it forward a day. You have two seasons, 50 field days in all. In the second season the water starts to rise.

Saving and turning in

Everything you type saves in this browser on its own. You can close the tab and come back later. When you stop for the day, click Export journal at the bottom of any page. That downloads a Markdown file of everything you wrote. Paste it into your Global History doc.

Briefing

You have two seasons.
Then the river takes it.

A dam is going up on a river in Southwest Asia. In about two years the reservoir behind it will cover a low mound on the south bank. The mound is 500 meters across and 8 meters deep, and it is made of nothing but collapsed houses, trash, ash, bones, and lost objects piled up over thousands of years. People lived here before anyone on Earth had written a single word.

You are the lead archaeologist. Nobody who lived here can tell you what happened. The only witnesses are the things they left behind, and those are buried in order: the oldest at the bottom, the newest on top. You will open one layer at a time, record what you find, and work out how these people lived, what changed, and what it cost them.

The unit's big question: What did we give up when we stopped moving?

How the dig works

  • Field Kit. Learn the job first. Write six things every archaeologist knows in your own words.
  • The Dig. Open five layers from the bottom up. For every find, write what you see, then write what you think it means. A layer will not close until every card has both.
  • Checkpoints. After each layer, write what you think life was like for those people. When a later layer proves you wrong, go back and fix it. Being wrong and fixing it is the job.
  • Field events. Things happen on a dig: weather, visitors, bad luck, lucky breaks. Each one asks you to spend a field day you do not have. Some of the most important finds at this site only end up in your record if you make the call.
  • The Lab. Look at the whole site through three lenses: geography, technology, and economics.
  • The Exhibit. Build a museum exhibit about this place, on a wall or on a screen. That is the assessment.
  • The Reveal. After you submit your claim, you find out where this site really is and what the real team concluded.

Rules of the trench

  • Do not look up the site. The dig code hides its name on purpose. You get it at the end.
  • Say what you see before you say what it means. Every card asks for both, in that order.
  • Use the links on the cards. They are there to help you figure out what a thing is.
  • Write like you are talking to Dad. Full sentences, your own words, no copying.
Part 1 · about 40 minutes

Field Kit

What to do now: research each of the six items below and write a definition in your own words, two or three sentences plus one example. Then do the practice at the bottom. When all six have something written, click Next.

Archaeologists are not treasure hunters. They are detectives whose witnesses are all dead and whose evidence is dirt. You need to know how they think before you touch the trench.

Good places to start: Society for American Archaeology: What is archaeology? · Archaeological Institute of America: Introduction to archaeology · Wikipedia: Archaeology. Each item below also has its own links.

The one rule that runs the whole dig

Say what you see before you say what it means.

An observation is something anyone looking at the object would agree on: its size, material, shape, where it was found, and what was next to it. An inference is a claim about what it means: who used it, what for, and why it was there. Observations are easy and solid. Inferences are the real work, and they can be wrong. Every card in the trench asks you for both, in that order.

Here is what a good pair looks like. Imagine a card showing a flat stone with a worn dip in the top and a smaller round stone beside it.
I see: A heavy flat stone about 40 cm long with a smooth, shallow dip worn into the top. A fist sized round stone was found next to it. Both are a hard grey rock. They were inside a house, near the fire pit.
I think: People rubbed the small stone back and forth on the big one to grind something, probably grain, because the dip is smooth and worn rather than chipped, and it was next to the cooking area. A stone this heavy also tells me they were not planning to carry it anywhere, so they probably stayed put.

Part 3 · about 20 minutes

The Lab: three lenses

What to do now: answer the four questions below using your finds. Name specific catalog numbers in each answer. This is the last part of Day 1.

You will ask the same three questions of every era this year. Ask them of Site 47 now.

Geography

Why did people settle here and not somewhere else? What did the river, the terrace, the open steppe, and the migrating herds make possible or impossible?

Technology

Which tools made settling down possible, and which ones made settling down costly? Pick the single tool you think mattered most and say why.

Economics

Once there was more food than one family could eat, where did the extra go? Who controlled it, and what did that do to who had power?

The body keeps score

The bones in Layer 2 are the most direct evidence you have of what farming cost. Were the farmers better off than the foragers in Layer 5? Answer with evidence, and say what "better off" should even mean.

Part 4 · Day 2

The Exhibit

What to do now: read the brief, pick your format, and build the seven panels. When the exhibit is done, write your claim in the box at the bottom and submit it. That unlocks the Reveal.

The museum has given you one wall, or one web page. Your job is to make a visitor who has never heard of this place understand what happened here and what it cost. This exhibit is the assessment for Unit 1. There is no essay.

The seven panels you need

  1. Title panel. Give the exhibit a real title (not "Site 47"), one line that makes a visitor want to read on, and the dates.
  2. Before. Show life in the deepest layers: food, shelter, work, and whether people moved around. Show at least three finds with their catalog numbers.
  3. The Gap. Explain what happened between the two villages and how you know.
  4. After. Show life in the farming village. Show at least three finds.
  5. The Body Keeps Score. Show the skeletal evidence plainly and respectfully. Explain what it tells us about the work and about who did it.
  6. How We Know. Explain the method: layers, observation versus inference, and one place where you changed your mind.
  7. The Claim. Put this at the center of the wall. Write your one sentence answer to What did we give up when we stopped moving? and show at least two finds that support it, with a line each on why.

Optional: add a "day in the life" scene of either village. Draw it, build it in Blender, do it in pixel art, or make a diorama.

Pick your format

Physical. Use foam board, poster board, or the hallway wall. Print the real artifact photos from the Reveal page, which links to open license images. Hand lettered headings are fine. Panel text can be printed.

Digital. Make a slide deck, a single web page, or a Canva board with the same seven panels. It has to make sense when someone opens it without you there to explain.

What a good one has

  • A visitor can follow it without you standing next to them.
  • Every claim points at a find, and every find has a catalog number.
  • At least one panel keeps observations and inferences visibly separate.
  • The claim panel is the first thing the eye lands on.

What to turn in

Paste your exported Dig Journal into your Global History doc. Add photos of the physical exhibit or the link to the digital one. Then you are ready for Day 3.

Day 3: the walkthrough

You are the museum guide. Dad is the visitor. Walk him through the exhibit panel by panel, in your own words, without reading off the panels. Expect him to stop you and ask questions like these:

  • How do you know that? Which find tells you?
  • What is an observation here, and what is a guess?
  • What did you think at first, and what changed your mind?
  • If you had skipped one field event, what would you not know?
  • So, what did they give up? Was it worth it?

Plan on about 30 minutes. You do not need notes, but you can have your Dig Journal open.

Reveal

Site 47 is Tell Abu Hureyra

What to do now: read what the real team concluded, score yourself honestly, write the two Codex entries, and then export your journal one last time.

It is a mound on the Euphrates River in northern Syria. Andrew Moore, Gordon Hillman, and Anthony Legge excavated it in 1972 and 1973 in a race against the Tabqa Dam. Lake Assad covered it in 1974. Everything we know comes from two seasons of digging and more than twenty five years of lab work afterward. The dig was one of the first anywhere to use flotation, which is why we have the seeds.

Honesty note. Your trench was built almost entirely from the real Abu Hureyra sequence. The cards marked Field reference were real finds from other sites of the same era, brought in to show things Abu Hureyra does not preserve. The full list is at the bottom of this page.

What the real team concluded

LayerTheir reading
5 · Abu Hureyra 1
about 13,300 to 11,400 years ago		A village of settled hunter gatherers, perhaps one or two hundred people, living in pit houses cut into the terrace. They harvested over a hundred wild plant species from river, forest, and steppe, and killed migrating gazelle by the hundreds each spring, drying the meat. They ground wild grain on stone. People settled before farming: the stored food was too heavy to carry and too valuable to leave.
4 · The Gap
about 12,900 to 10,600 years ago		A sudden return to cold, dry conditions (the Younger Dryas) thinned the wild food. Weed seeds of disturbed ground and a handful of rye grains with a domesticated shape suggest people tried sowing grain to make up the loss. Then the village was largely abandoned for roughly a thousand years, probably for better watered sites nearby.
3 · Abu Hureyra 2A
about 10,600 to 9,500 years ago		A new village of rectangular mudbrick houses with plastered floors. People grew domesticated emmer, barley, and lentils, and still hunted gazelle heavily. Farming and hunting ran side by side for centuries.
2 · Abu Hureyra 2B
about 9,500 to 8,000 years ago		The village grew to several thousand people, among the largest anywhere. Around 8,300 years ago sheep and goat bones overtake gazelle: herding replaced the hunt, likely because the hunt had collapsed. Theya Molleson's study of the skeletons found bent and arthritic big toes, crushed lower vertebrae, and enlarged neck vertebrae, mostly in women, from kneeling for hours at the grinding stone and carrying loads on the head. Teeth were ground down by stone grit in the flour. Grooved front teeth show a few women held basket fiber in their mouths while weaving. Childhood stress lines in tooth enamel are common. The people buried under house floors are mostly women.
1 · Abu Hureyra 2C
about 8,000 to 7,800 years ago		Pottery appears. Tooth wear drops, which Molleson reads as grain being soaked and cooked in pots instead of eaten as gritty bread. The village ends soon after.

Dates are calibrated years before present and are rounded. Specialists still argue about the early rye and about whether the gap was a complete abandonment.

For the Pattern Codex

Unit 1 hands you your first pattern. Name it in your own words. Here is a starting shape you are free to reject: stored food lets people stop moving; stopping lets numbers grow; growing numbers need more stored food; and the work of making it lands on some bodies more than others.
